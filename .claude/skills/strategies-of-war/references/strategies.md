## §1 Defensive war (Strategies 10–11)

### 10. Create a threatening presence: deterrence strategies
**Core idea.** Aggressors prey on those who seem weak. They judge you by the signals you give, they can't know your real strength, and they want easy, bloodless victories. Turn that around: make attacking you look costly, uncertain and dangerous. "Power is not only what you have but what the enemy thinks you have" (Saul Alinsky).

**Five methods of deterrence and reverse intimidation:**
1. **Surprise with a bold maneuver.** An unexpected, risky, confident action hides weakness. Opponents assume it's backed by something real and start seeing threats they hadn't imagined.
2. **Reverse the threat.** If people see you as a pushover, make a sudden, small move that hurts something they value. Show you're not afraid and can be ruthless. Send a short warning that hints at worse.
3. **Seem unpredictable and somewhat irrational.** Suggest you have nothing to lose and would take them down with you. This works especially well against people with reputations to protect. It's controlled calculation, not an emotional outburst.
4. **Play on people's natural paranoia.** Use indirect, veiled threats, through go-betweens or "overheard" plans. Let their imagination magnify the danger. Louis XI let a disloyal ambassador "accidentally" hear his rage so the threat reached the Duke of Milan.
5. **Establish a frightening reputation:** difficult, stubborn, ruthlessly efficient. Build it carefully and with no inconsistencies. Then it works on its own and you rarely have to fight. The samurai image: keep the sword sharp but sheathed, drawing it now and then.

**Examples:**
- Stonewall Jackson's 3,600 men tied up tens of thousands of Union troops with bold, baffling attacks in the Shenandoah Valley.
- Robert the Bruce made each English invasion of Scotland cost them by raiding England and burning crops (the wasp strategy).
- John Boyd at the Pentagon earned a reputation for shredding inflated briefings, so opponents stopped crossing him. He also secured a powerful patron and made generals "put it in writing."
- Jackie Robinson ended Sal Maglie's beanballs with one fearless play.

**Image:** the Porcupine. **Authority:** Sun-tzu: opponents won't fight you if they think it's against their interests.

**Reversal:**
- Sometimes it's safer to seem harmless and unassuming. Claudius survived Roman politics by appearing too dull to bother with. This needs patience and is risky: you're the lamb among wolves.
- Don't get drunk on fear. Intimidation is a defense, not your offense of choice. It creates enemies, collapses if you can't back it up, and can escalate into a retaliatory war.

### 11. Trade space for time: the nonengagement strategy
**Core idea.** Retreating in the face of a stronger enemy shows strength, not weakness. Refusing to engage buys time to recover, think and gain perspective. It also infuriates aggressors, feeds their arrogance, and lures them into overextending. Time reveals them as rash and you as wise. Sometimes you accomplish most by doing nothing (the Taoist *wu wei*: action through inaction).

**Keys:**
- *Mao and the Long March:* he refused the Soviet-style frontal defense, retreated through 24 rivers and 18 mountain ranges, and forged a hard core of believers who later won.
- *Von Lettow-Vorbeck in East Africa* led Smuts on a four-year chase; disease, distance and frustration wore down the British.
- Moses, Mohammed and Odysseus each withdrew to regroup and redefine themselves.
- Retreat makes Murphy's Law and Clausewitz's "friction" work for you. The longer the aggressor pushes into your void, the more goes wrong for them.
- Frederick the Great survived the Seven Years' War by buying time until the Czarina died.
- **Time is your only true commodity.** Others can take your space and possessions, but not your time unless you let them. Don't waste it on battles that aren't of your choosing.

**Image:** the Desert Sands: retreat there to think, and lure enemies to where they lose all sense of time and space. **Authority:** Sun-tzu: staying disciplined and calm while waiting for disorder in the enemy is the art of self-possession.

**Reversal:**
- **Martyrdom** — fighting a hopeless battle — can turn defeat into a symbol (the Alamo). It works only if your loss has symbolic meaning and highlights the enemy's ugliness. It's dangerous, uncontrollable and slow; a good strategist avoids it.
- Retreat must be temporary. If you never turn and fight, it's surrender.

## §2 Offensive war (Strategies 12–22)

**Part introduction.** The great dangers come from the unexpected. "Friction" is the gap between plan and reality. Offensive warfare reduces friction for you and multiplies it for the enemy:
- Plan meticulously around an overall goal (grand strategy).
- Know the enemy's weaknesses.
- Then strike with such audacity and momentum that the enemy stays on the defensive.

You need both strategic cleverness and boldness.

### 12. Lose battles but win the war: grand strategy
**Core idea.** Everyone around you is maneuvering. Daily skirmishes make people lose sight of what really matters: ultimate victory, lasting power. Grand strategy looks beyond the battle:
- Focus on your ultimate goal.
- Consider the political ramifications and long-term consequences.
- Act deliberately rather than emotionally.
- Coordinate every action toward the end.

"War is the continuation of politics by other means" (Clausewitz).

**Keys:**
- *Vision.* Humans rose above animals through the power to see further. Under stress, the animal part reacts to the immediate and loses that vision; tragedy, as the Greeks saw, comes from limited vision.
- Four practical principles from the chapter:
  1. **Focus on your greater goal, your destiny.** Choose a high, lasting aim, and let it organize your actions and give you patience.
  2. **Widen your perspective.** See the whole political, cultural and economic field, not just the military one.
  3. **Sever the roots.** Attack the sources of the enemy's power, especially political support, not just its surface.
  4. **Take the indirect route.** Make your moves hard to read. Set up chains of events in which one battle leads to the next, and bring the war into arenas the enemy has ignored.

**Image:** the Mountaintop: above the battle's smoke, the general sees everything. **Authority:** Thucydides: men begin wars at the wrong end, acting first and waiting for disaster to start discussing the matter.

**Reversal:**
- Success breeds overconfidence. Napoleon and others lost their sense of proportion. After every victory, retrench and regain realism.
- Too much analysis can paralyze you into Hamlet-like indecision. Combine vision with the capacity to act decisively, like Odysseus.

### 13. Know your enemy: the intelligence strategy
**Core idea.** Target the mind of the person running the army, not the army. Learn to read people from the signals they send unconsciously. A friendly front lets you watch them closely and mine them for information. Don't project your own psychology onto them; think as they think. Find their psychological weaknesses so you can unbalance them.

**Keys:**
- **Test with small actions.** The Mongols opened campaigns with probes to gauge strength and morale.
- **Quality beats quantity.** One crucial insight can be decisive:
  - Hannibal saw a Roman general's arrogance and lured him into a rash attack.
  - Churchill saw Hitler's paranoia and feinted toward the Balkans to make him spread his defenses.
  - Lee Atwater found Bob Dole's temper and kept provoking it.
- **Build an informal network of allies** close to your rival. That beats paid spies. Look for disgruntled insiders and people the enemy has fired.
- **Never rely on one source.** People reveal themselves in their writing, too: *Mein Kampf*; Rommel and Guderian on blitzkrieg.
- **Intelligence must be current.** Enemies adapt and learn from defeat.

**Image:** the Shadow: everyone has a hidden side, visible only up close, so follow quietly in the target's footsteps. **Authority:** Sun-tzu: foreknowledge comes from people who know the enemy's situation, not from spirits or calculations.

### 14. Overwhelm resistance with speed and suddenness: the blitzkrieg strategy
**Core idea.** In a world of indecisive, cautious people, speed gives enormous power. Strike first, before opponents can think or prepare. They become emotional, unbalanced and error-prone. Follow at once with another swift move to deepen the panic. It works best after a lull or setup, so the action catches them off guard. Hit with relentless force; decisiveness earns respect, awe and momentum.

**Keys:**
- 1940: German tanks and aircraft broke the Allies' static defense faster than the Allies could process what was happening.
- Modern life's pace makes people defensive and slow to decide, so sudden decisive action forces them to respond before they're ready. Each surprise pushes them into a downward psychological spiral.
- Caesar's sudden moves and unexpected pardons kept everyone off balance.
- *I Ching*, Chen (thunder): the shock of the sudden. Surprise frightens even the stronger side.

**Reversal / cautions:**
- Speed without preparation is recklessness.
- If the first blow doesn't break them, you can lose momentum and get stuck in a long fight on bad terms.
- Use a lull or decoy first, and have the follow-up ready.

### 15. Control the dynamic: forcing strategies
**Core idea** (from the chapter's logic; its opening page wasn't legible in the scan). People constantly try to control you. Don't fight over each move. Shape the relationship and its terms:
- set the tempo;
- define the battleground;
- push emotional buttons so opponents react instead of act;
- keep the initiative so they're always responding.

"The one who excels at warfare compels others and is not compelled by them" (Sun-tzu). Ways to do it include putting opponents on the defensive, being the one who frames the issues, and making your passivity itself a form of control.

**Authority:** keep the enemy occupied; once they're on the defensive, they can't rise again for the whole campaign.

**Reversal: none.** Seeming to give up control — letting the other side lead — is itself a form of control: you've granted them a passive authority you can use later. There's no escaping the control dynamic. People who claim to be above it are playing its most insidious version.

### 16. Hit them where it hurts: the center-of-gravity strategy
**Core idea.** Every person, group or organization depends on a source of power: wealth, popularity, a key position, a leader, a winning strategy, a critical alliance or network. Look beneath the surface for the center of gravity that holds the structure together. Hitting it causes disproportionate damage. Find what the other side most cherishes and protects; that's where you strike.

**Keys:**
- Train yourself to see where strength really comes from, which is often not where it appears. That opens many angles to undermine it subtly instead of meeting it head-on.
- The story of Judith and Holofernes: kill the leader and the Assyrian army panics and flees.
- Even decentralized organizations depend on a network that is vulnerable at key nodes.

**Authority:** Clausewitz: reduce the enemy's strength to as few sources as possible, ideally one, and attack it with everything.

**Reversal: none.** Every structure has a center of gravity.

### 17. Defeat them in detail: the divide-and-conquer strategy
**Core idea.** Don't be intimidated by how big your enemies look. Look at the parts that make up the whole. Separate them and sow dissension inside, and even the most formidable foe weakens. Look for the joints and links that connect people within a group or connect groups to each other. **Division is weakness; joints are the weakest part of any structure.** Apply the same idea to problems: break a large problem into small, beatable parts.

**Keys:**
- Napoleon defeated coalitions by striking each army before they could combine.
- Samuel Adams used minor issues (the Stamp Act, the Tea Act) as wedges to separate colonists emotionally from Britain. Rational argument rarely moves people; emotional separation from the past opens them to change. "Divide their minds in order to conquer them."
- Musashi against many opponents: put yourself in a central position and cut down the line one by one, so the attackers become the flustered ones.
- With many problems, **move quickly.** Start with the smallest to build momentum while keeping the most dangerous at bay. Waiting lets problems combine.

**Image:** the Knot: cut it with one blow and it comes undone. **Authority:** Sun-tzu: the skilled keep the enemy's parts from connecting, cooperating or trusting each other.

### 18. Expose and attack your opponent's soft flank: the turning strategy
**Core idea.** A direct attack stiffens resistance. Instead, hold the opponent's attention to the front, then hit from the side, where they least expect it and are soft and unprotected. The shock creates a moment of weakness. Bait people into going out on a limb, then rake them from the side. Indirection is the only way to move stubborn opponents.

**Keys:**
- Napoleon offered Russia protection of Malta, knowing England would seize it. That flank move split the Anglo-Russian alliance.
- Theodore Roosevelt flanked politicians through threats to their popularity with constituents.
- **Social flanking:** most people defend their front (their stated positions). Their flank is their vanity, their need for support and kindness, their reputation.
- Disarm opponents and make them allies through strategic kindness, generosity and charm (Caesar's "new way of conquering").
- The more angles your attack bounces off, like a cue ball, the harder it is to defend against.

**Image:** the Lobster: armored on top, helpless when flipped to its tender underside. **Authority:** Napoleon: battles are won by turning the enemy, by attacking a flank.

### 19. Envelop the enemy: the annihilation strategy
**Core idea.** People use any gap in your defenses to attack or intrude, so offer no gaps. Envelop opponents:
- apply relentless pressure from all sides;
- dominate their attention;
- cut off their access to the outside world;
- make your attacks unpredictable, creating a vague sense of vulnerability.

As their resolve weakens, tighten the noose. The best encirclements are psychological: surround their minds.

**Keys:**
- Hannibal at Cannae; the Zulu "horns of the buffalo" (keep a reserve for the kill).
- Psychological enclosure is deeply disturbing to humans, who are nomads at heart; feeling trapped triggers panic and bad decisions.
- In social and political space, narrow your opponents' options until their willpower collapses. "You must make your opponent acknowledge defeat from the bottom of his heart."

**Authority:** the *Huainanzi*: a caged tiger is no better than a pig because it has no room to use its abilities.

**Reversal:**
- An incomplete envelopment leaves you overextended and exposed. You've revealed your intent to annihilate, so the enemy will fight desperately.
- Use it only when you have a reasonable chance of finishing it.

### 20. Maneuver them into weakness: the ripening-for-the-sickle strategy
**Core idea.** Endless battles are exhausting, costly and unimaginative. Wise strategists prefer maneuver: put opponents in positions so weak that victory is quick and easy before the battle even starts. Bait them into positions that look attractive but are traps and dead ends. Draw them out of strong positions with wild-goose chases. **Create dilemmas:** moves that leave them only bad choices. Channel chaos their way. Confused, frustrated, angry opponents are ripe fruit; the slightest breeze brings them down.

**Keys** (from the Part IV discussion of maneuver warfare). Two styles of war run through history: **attrition** (frontal, costly) and **maneuver**, codified by Sun-tzu. Four principles of maneuver:
1. Craft a plan with branches: flexible options for every contingency.
2. Give yourself room to maneuver; don't get boxed in.
3. Give the enemy dilemmas, not problems.
4. Create maximum disorder for the enemy.

**Reversal / cautions:** maneuver needs patience and good intelligence. Overly elaborate maneuvers can confuse your own side; keep your ultimate aim simple.

### 21. Negotiate while advancing: the diplomatic-war strategy
**Core idea.** People will try to take from you in negotiation what they couldn't win in battle, often using appeals to fairness and morality as cover. Negotiation is maneuvering for power and position. Keep advancing before and during talks, applying relentless pressure so the other side has to settle on your terms. The more you take, the more you can give back as meaningless concessions. A reputation for toughness puts people on their back foot before they even meet you.

**Keys:**
- Never negotiate from weakness. Create facts on the ground first.
- Hold something they fear (Teddy Roosevelt's "big stick").
- With an unknown opponent, play the warrior yourself: you can always ease off, but if you play the shopkeeper against a warrior you'll lose everything.

**Image:** the Big Stick. **Authority:** Metternich: always carry the sword in one hand and the olive branch in the other; negotiate only while advancing.

**Reversal:**
- Don't advance too far. A punitive settlement, like Versailles after WWI, creates embittered enemies who seek revenge.
- The aim of any settlement is your long-term security, not greed or punishment. Leave the other side something; don't let them feel wronged.

### 22. Know how to end things: the exit strategy
**Core idea.** You're judged by how well you end things. A messy or incomplete ending lasts in people's memories. Know when to stop. Avoid the trap of fighting on, or of letting things drag out until you're worn down. Plan your exit before you begin, leaving no loose ends, and leave on your terms, in a way that sets up the next round.

**Keys:**
- **Three kinds of people:**
  1. Dreamers, who start with enthusiasm, hit reality and quit.
  2. Those who finish only because they must, limping to the line.
  3. The rare few who begin with the end in mind and finish with energy.

  Be the third.
- Don't think in absolute terms of winning and losing. Everything is a transition; "the only real ending is death." Some victories lead nowhere; some defeats are useful lessons.
- Decide whether generosity at the end — turning enemies into allies — will serve you better than crushing them.
- **Plant future victory in present defeat:** heroic, dignified losses can become rallying points (the Alamo).
- End on a note of ambiguity or mystery when it helps you, so people can't predict your next move.

**Image:** the Sun, whose setting leaves a brilliant afterglow and whose return is always wanted. **Authority:** Napoleon: to conquer is nothing; one must profit from one's success.

**Reversal: none.** There's no value in ending badly.

## §3 Unconventional (dirty) war (Strategies 23–33)

**Part introduction.** The greatest advantage is surprise: strategies outside the enemy's experience. Over time every tactic gets tried, so the search for the unconventional becomes more extreme. As moral codes loosen, it slides into "dirty war": political, deceptive, highly manipulative, often the last resort of the weak to level the field. Know these strategies so you can **recognize and defend against them** in a world full of covert and indirect aggression.

### 23. Weave a seamless blend of fact and fiction: misperception strategies
**Core idea.** No creature survives without seeing what's around it, so make it hard for enemies to see what you're doing. People filter perception through emotion and see what they want to see. Feed their expectations and build a reality that matches their desires, and they'll fool themselves. The best deceptions rest on ambiguity, mixing fact and fiction so the two can't be separated. Control people's perceptions and you control them.

**Main forms of military deception** described in the chapter:
- the **false front**;
- the **decoy attack**;
- **camouflage**;
- the **hypnotic pattern**: establish a routine, then break it;
- **planted information**;
- **shadows within shadows**: layered deceptions that keep opponents guessing even after they spot one ruse.

As Yagyu Munenori notes, even an opponent who doesn't fall for the ploy has, in effect, already fallen for it.

**Reversal:**
- Discovered deception is dangerous: you lose credibility, and the enemy can feed you false information. Use the smallest number of people possible. Always have a cover story and an escape route.
- Don't get drunk on deception's power; keep it subordinate to your overall strategy.
- If you're known as a deceiver, being straightforward and honest will confuse people. Honesty can be the higher deception.

### 24. Take the line of least expectation: the ordinary-extraordinary strategy
**Core idea.** People expect behavior that fits known patterns. Upset those expectations. Surprise lets chaos and unpredictability — which people desperately try to hold at bay — into their world, and their defenses drop.

The sequence:
1. Do something **ordinary and conventional** to fix their picture of you.
2. Then hit them with the **extraordinary**. The shock is greater for its suddenness.

Never repeat an unorthodox strategy that worked before; the second time it's conventional. Sometimes the ordinary is extraordinary because it's unexpected.

**Keys:**
- From Sun-tzu: the orthodox (*cheng*) and unorthodox (*ch'i*) produce each other in an endless cycle.
- In culture and business, mere weirdness or shock fades fast. Lasting impact comes from the unconventional that grows out of the ordinary and makes people question reality.

**Image:** the Plow: the ground must be constantly turned or weeds and clumps choke life. **Authority:** Sun-tzu: engage with the orthodox, win with the unorthodox.

**Reversal:** there's never value in attacking from the direction opponents expect, unless your strategy depends on it (e.g., to fix them in place).

### 25. Occupy the moral high ground: the righteous strategy
**Core idea.** In a political world, your cause must look more just than the enemy's. Treat it as moral terrain you're fighting over:
- question the enemy's motives and make them look bad, which narrows their support and room to maneuver;
- target the soft spots in their public image and expose hypocrisy;
- never assume your cause's justice is self-evident; publicize and promote it.

When attacked morally, don't whine or get angry; fight fire with fire. If possible, position yourself as the underdog, the victim or the martyr. Learn to use guilt as a moral weapon.

**Keys:**
- Luther's campaign against the Roman church.
- **Wars of self-interest** end when interests are satisfied. **Wars of morality** are longer and bloodier, and churn up uncontrollable emotions (the sack of Rome, 1527).
- People who fight "for morality" can be the most dangerous: hungry for power, or driven by grievance.

**Image:** Germs: once inside they spread, so inoculate yourself in advance.

**Reversal / caution:**
- Avoid wars of morality when you can; they aren't worth the time and the bad feeling.
- When someone uses morality against you, anticipate it, inoculate yourself, and expose their hypocrisy rather than defending yourself emotionally.

### 26. Deny them targets: the strategy of the void
**Core idea** (from the chapter's guerrilla logic). Don't give enemies anything solid to hit. Stay dispersed, mobile and elusive. Wear them out with irritating raids rather than decisive battles, and turn time into an offensive weapon. A guerrilla force reverses conventional war's natural logic of concentrating, holding a front and ending things quickly. Conventional armies can't cope with an enemy that won't stand and fight.

**Keys:**
- Origins in weaker peoples who fled invaders. The word *guerrilla* ("small war") comes from the Spanish resistance to Napoleon, 1808–14.
- T. E. Lawrence's Arab revolt: a war of detachment; "the silent threat of a vast unknown desert."
- In daily life, stay hard to pin down. The less people can see of you, the more they wear themselves out chasing.

**Authority:** anything with form can be overcome; sages hide their form in nothingness.

**Reversal (countering guerrillas):**
- Conventional responses play into their hands.
- Reverse their reversal: deny them freedom of time and space, and isolate them physically, politically and morally.
- **Never escalate gradually**, as the US did in Vietnam. Seek a quick, decisive outcome, or pull out before you're dragged into a protracted war.

### 27. Seem to work for the interests of others while furthering your own: the alliance strategy
**Core idea.** The cheapest way to advance is a constantly shifting network of alliances:
- get others to make up for your weaknesses, do your dirty work and fight your wars;
- choose the allies who fit the moment;
- bind them with gifts, favors and help in time of need, so they're obligated to you;
- sow dissension in your enemies' alliances to isolate them;
- form convenient coalitions while keeping yourself free of entanglements that drag you down.

**Keys:**
- Louis XI used allies (the Swiss, the English) against Charles the Bold, who overreached with grand alliances.
- Aesop's dog and the fox: divert attackers toward someone better able to handle them.
- Alliances are temporary and based on mutual need. Treat permanent loyalty as a lure, not a reality.

**Image:** Stepping-stones: move from stone to stone and never linger.

**Reversal:**
- Others will play the same game, so don't take it personally.
- Beware people who are overeager to ally, make big promises, or talk about friendship and loyalty instead of tangible mutual benefit. They're often con artists.
- Check prospective allies' past for greed and betrayal, and keep your eye on the interests on both sides.

### 28. Give your rivals enough rope to hang themselves: the one-upmanship strategy
**Core idea.** The worst dangers often come from supposed colleagues and friends who pretend to share the cause while sabotaging you and stealing your ideas. Keep up an appearance of civility, but:
- plant doubts and insecurities in such rivals so they overthink and get defensive;
- bait them with subtle challenges that get under their skin and provoke an overreaction or embarrassing mistake.

The goal is to isolate them. Let their own self-destructive tendencies hang them while you stay blameless.

**Ethical note:** use this to protect yourself from saboteurs and to recognize when it's being done to you. Don't use it to harass colleagues.

### 29. Take small bites: the fait accompli strategy
**Core idea.** Overt power grabs and visible ambition breed resentment, envy and suspicion. Take small bites instead: swallow little territories and rely on people's short attention spans. Stay under the radar so they don't see your moves. By the time they notice, it's too late; the territory is yours, a done deal. You can always claim you acted defensively. Before people realize it, you've built an empire.

**Keys:** each bite seems too small to fight over. Together they change the balance of power. Act, then present the result as settled.

**Reversal / counter:** watch for incremental encroachment. Set clear boundaries early and respond to the pattern, not just the single bite.

### 30. Penetrate their minds: communication strategies
**Core idea.** Communication is a kind of war. Your ideas are useless unless they get past people's defenses and take root. Direct advice and argument make people defensive or merely polite. Communicate indirectly instead, so the idea feels like their own experience:
- images, stories, parables and metaphor;
- actions rather than words;
- humor and entertainment;
- questions that unsettle certainty.

**Keys:**
- **Socrates**, the "midwife" method: profess ignorance, praise the listener, then use questions to reveal the flaws in their ideas, so the doubts are their own.
- Jesus taught in parables. Machiavelli hid dangerous ideas inside history and examples.
- Focus on the audience, not on expressing yourself:
  - bored audiences need entertainment;
  - leaders need indirect channels and third parties;
  - the young respond to boldness.
- Decode others' communication as well. Vague abstractions ("justice," "morality," "liberty") often hide self-interest. Flowery language or slang often aims to charm rather than inform.

**Reversal:** some situations call for direct, plain speech. Overusing indirect, manipulative communication can make you seem slippery.

### 31. Destroy from within: the inner-front strategy
**Core idea.** An enemy can only be fought when it shows itself. By infiltrating an opponent's ranks and working from inside, you give them nothing to see or react against. From inside you learn their weaknesses and can sow internal dissension. Hide your hostile intentions, join them, and either take over slowly or wait for the moment to strike.

**Keys:**
- *Fortresses* fall more easily through tunnels and inside allies than through walls: the "inner front."
- Join the group that best serves your long-term interests, and gather intelligence on its hypocrisies and weaknesses.
- Small acts of internal sabotage and dissuasion (Admiral Canaris inside the Nazi regime) can be safer and more effective than dramatic conspiracies.
- Conspiracies are risky: too few people and you can't control the consequences, too many and you'll be exposed. They often have unintended effects; Caesar's assassination brought Augustus, not the Republic.
- Undermining enemy morale from within ("removing the firewood from under the cauldron") is powerful. Discontent spreads like a disease.

**Image:** the Termite.

**Reversal (defense):**
- There will always be disgruntled people in your group. Paranoia is the worst mistake.
- The only real safeguard is to keep your people satisfied, engaged and united by their cause. Healthy groups police themselves; cancer takes root only in unhealthy bodies.

### 32. Dominate while seeming to submit: the passive-aggression strategy
**Core idea.** Any attempt to bend people to your will is aggression. In a political world, the most effective aggression is the best hidden: behind a compliant, even loving exterior. The passive-aggressive person never openly resists. They agree, seem helpless or noncommittal, and yet everything ends up revolving around them. If anyone accuses them, they can deny it, and the accuser feels guilty. **Know the strategy so you can recognize and defend against the legions of passive-aggressive people in daily life.**

**Keys:**
- People prefer simple, single judgments ("nice" or "nasty"). Passive aggressors exploit this by being both at once.
- Examples:
  - a spouse whose "dizzy spells" or anxiety attacks control the partner's life;
  - chronic lateness, forgetfulness and procrastination as covert resistance;
  - Gandhi's nonviolence as passive aggression put to noble use.
- **Counters:**
  - Name the dynamic. Invite open criticism, which is easier to handle than covert sabotage.
  - Like Hernán Cortés, quietly identify the passive aggressors, keep a friendly front, isolate them, and bait them into revealing themselves.
  - Sometimes give them a taste of their own medicine.
  - Never leave them the time and space they need; stay alert and keep your mind free of their influence.

**Image:** the River: don't dam it; channel its power. **Authority:** Sun Haichen: as dripping water wears away rock, the weak and yielding can overcome the firm and strong.

**Ethics:** apply this mainly defensively. Using passive aggression to control a partner or family member is abusive.

### 33. Sow uncertainty and panic through acts of terror: the chain-reaction strategy
**Core idea, for understanding and defense only.** Terror aims to paralyze people's will and disrupt their capacity for a strategic response. Sporadic, unpredictable acts create a sense of threat out of all proportion to the attackers' real strength. The goal isn't battlefield victory but chaos, and above all **provoking the other side into a desperate, unstrategic overreaction.** Attackers blend into the population and stage their actions for the media to seem everywhere. The defense is rationality.

**Keys:**
- The mechanism is fear spreading through a group (Hasan-i-Sabbah's Assassins). Panic is contagious and erodes everyone's judgment; overreaction shows the terrorists have won.
- **Counterstrategy:**
  1. Stay calm and refuse to overreact; resolve and patience are strength.
  2. Gather solid intelligence, including from inside.
  3. Cut off the attackers' resources and support.
  4. Keep the moral high ground. As the victim you hold it, and you lose it if you strike back indiscriminately.
  5. Build alliances that isolate the attackers.
  6. Wage a patient, long campaign, mostly behind the scenes.
  7. Accept that perfect security doesn't exist. Ask how much risk you can live with, rather than letting fear hold your whole society or organization hostage.

**Image:** the tsunami: a small disturbance far out at sea builds into a wave of unimaginable force.

**Use in this skill:** only to understand fear-driven campaigns — in security, crisis communications, corporate smear or harassment campaigns, and online disinformation — and to build resilience against them. Never for planning intimidation or violence.
