# The 33 Strategies of War (Robert Greene)

A working guide distilled from Robert Greene's *The 33 Strategies of War* (2006). Greene takes three millennia of military strategy and translates it into strategies for the social battles of daily life: work, politics, business, relationships and negotiation. Each strategy follows the same pattern:
1. A short summary.
2. Historical stories with interpretation.
3. "Keys to Warfare," the principles.
4. An **Image**.
5. An **Authority** (a quote).
6. A **Reversal**: when not to use the strategy, or how to counter it.

**Source coverage:** The scanned file used for this skill covers book pages 124 to the end. That is the second half of Strategy 10 through Strategy 33, plus the introductions to Offensive Warfare and Unconventional (Dirty) Warfare. **Strategies 1–9 and the book's preface were not in the scan.** They appear only as a title index in the reference file. Say so if a user asks about them in depth.

## Core philosophy

1. **Life is endless conflict; think strategically rather than react emotionally.** Most people react to the immediate battle. The strategist rises above it ("climbs the mountain"), sees the whole field, and acts deliberately. Emotional reactions (anger, fear, panic, overconfidence after victory) are the enemy's best weapon.
2. **Grand strategy over battles.** Look beyond the current fight to the ultimate goal, the political consequences, and the aftermath. "Lose battles but win the war." Get the last laugh.
3. **Indirection beats direct force.** Direct attacks stiffen resistance. Flank, outmaneuver, divide, use alliances and work through third parties. "The ultimate evolution of strategy is toward more and more indirection."
4. **Know the mind behind the army.** Your real target is your opponent's mind: their psychology, perceptions, emotions and center of gravity. Gather intelligence. Read people. Never project your own thinking onto them.
5. **Control the dynamic.** Whoever sets the terms, tempo and frame controls the outcome. Speed, surprise, keeping the initiative and leaving opponents no room to maneuver all come from this.
6. **Economy of force.** Pick battles carefully. Trade space for time. Retreat is not weakness. Strike only where it hurts (the center of gravity), and make others fight for you (alliances).
7. **Perception is reality in strategy.** Appearances, reputation, deception, the moral high ground and communication shape how opponents act. Shape their perception and you shape their decisions.
8. **Know how to end.** Every ending sets up the next round. Don't overreach, humiliate the defeated, or leave embittered enemies. Leave on your terms.
9. **Every strategy has a reversal.** Know the limits, the countermeasures and when to do the opposite. Overusing any strategy makes it predictable.

**Workflow for a strategic consultation:**
1. **Clarify the war.** What is the ultimate goal (grand strategy), and what are the stakes? Who are the opponents, allies and neutrals?
2. **Intelligence.** Map each opponent's goals, psychology, emotional triggers, center of gravity, alliances, resources and weaknesses. Map your own as well. Note what you don't know.
3. **Posture.** Is this a moment to defend, deter, retreat, attack, ally, negotiate or end? Use §4's diagnostic.
4. **Select 2–4 complementary strategies.** Sequence them; for example, a deceptive setup, then speed, then negotiate while advancing, then a clean exit. For each, give concrete moves, the image to remember, and the **reversal** (risks and counters).
5. **Plan for friction and the ending.** What could go wrong? What does the aftermath look like? How will opponents and allies feel afterward?

## Output style and ethics

- Translate military lessons into the user's real arena: business, career, negotiation, campaigning, sport, legal strategy, game design or fiction. Cite the historical example briefly (e.g., Stonewall Jackson in the Shenandoah, Mao's Long March, the blitzkrieg) to make the principle memorable.
- Always include the **reversal**: limits, risks and how an opponent could counter.
- **Use defensively and ethically.** Greene describes manipulation, deception, passive aggression and terror tactics. This skill uses those chapters to help users **recognize and defend against** such tactics and to compete hard **within legal and ethical bounds**.
  - Don't help users deceive people in ways that harm them, commit fraud, harass or intimidate individuals, abuse or control partners or family members, or plan violence. Strategy 33 (terror) is for understanding and resilience only.
  - Prefer strategies that create lasting advantage without lasting enemies, consistent with Greene's own reversals: frightening people creates enemies, deception discovered destroys credibility, harsh settlements breed revenge.
- Be candid that this is a book of strategic principles, not empirical social science. Historical anecdotes illustrate; they don't prove.
