# Conditions, medications, assessment and practice

From *Linking Nutrition to Mental Health* (Leyse-Wallace, 2008). Where the book is dated, the update is flagged ⚠️.

---

## §1 Index of suspicion: who should be checked for nutritional status

Evaluate nutritional status when any of these apply (compiled from the book's Table 9.1 and chapters):

| Condition or history | Nutrients most at risk |
|---|---|
| Heavy alcohol use | Thiamin, folate, B6, B12, niacin, magnesium, zinc, vitamin A, protein, calories |
| Persistent vomiting (hyperemesis, bulimia, after bariatric surgery) | Thiamin and other water-soluble vitamins, potassium, chloride |
| Bariatric (gastric bypass) surgery | Thiamin, B12, folate, iron, calcium, vitamin D, protein |
| Eating disorders or very restrictive diets (including fad and "orthorexic" diets) | Many: energy, protein, EFAs, B vitamins, C, zinc, iron; general malnutrition |
| Vegan or macrobiotic diets | B12, possibly iron, zinc, omega-3s, calcium, vitamin D |
| Low-fat or low-cholesterol diets | Essential fatty acids, vitamin E |
| Omitting whole food groups (allergy or preference); eating once a day | Nutrients supplied by those groups (see §8) |
| Older age | B12 (low stomach acid), folate, vitamin D, overall intake |
| Malabsorption (Crohn's, celiac/sprue, IBS, GI surgery), frequent diarrhea | B12, folate, fat-soluble vitamins, minerals |
| Pernicious anemia or a family history of it | B12 |
| Pregnancy and breastfeeding | Folate, DHA/omega-3, B12, iron; avoid alcohol entirely |
| Food insecurity or low income | Overall adequacy; depression and suicidality risk in teens |
| Anticonvulsants, long-term antibiotics, dialysis, long-term TPN, raw egg whites | Biotin, folate |
| IV glucose in a depleted or alcoholic patient | Thiamin (can trigger Wernicke's) |
| Psychiatric medications (see §3) | Weight, glucose, lipids, sodium (lithium), tyramine (MAOIs) |
| Mega-dose supplement use | Toxicity (B6, niacin, A, minerals) |
| Poor response to standard psychiatric treatment | Check for EFA, B-vitamin, thiamin and C deficiencies |
| Occupational or environmental exposure, old housing | Lead, mercury, manganese |

## §2 Conditions linking nutrition and mental status

### Starvation, fasting and dieting
- **Minnesota semi-starvation study (Keys, 1940s):** Healthy conscientious-objector volunteers ate about 1,600 kcal/day for 6 months and lost about 25% of their body weight. They developed depression, irritability, apathy, emotional instability, social withdrawal, and loss of humor and ambition. They became preoccupied with food: hoarding, slow eating, licking plates, reading cookbooks, drinking up to nine cups of coffee and chewing up to 40 packs of gum. MMPI scores rose on depression, hypochondriasis and hysteria (the "neurotic triad"). Intellectual capacity was preserved, but they felt less able to concentrate. Recovery took up to a year and tracked calorie intake; those refed more generously recovered faster.
  - **Lesson:** Many "personality" symptoms of eating disorders and chronic dieting are effects of starvation that reverse with nourishment.
- **The body adapts to starvation** by lowering metabolic rate, heart rate and temperature, shifting the brain toward ketones, and sparing protein. Losing about 50% of body protein is fatal. Watch micronutrients during restriction; supplements help but are often incomplete.
- **Chronic restrictive dieting (Polivy & Herman):** Weight loss rarely lasts. It predicts food preoccupation, disinhibited overeating, emotional eating, anxiety, distractibility, higher stress and lower well-being. A better approach is a healthful lifestyle, moderate enjoyment of foods, gradual activity, and **intuitive eating** (hunger-based eating guided by internal cues, practiced mindfully).
- **Food cravings:** These are tied to mood (boredom, anxiety, low mood), neurotransmitters, and possibly nutrient deficits. Dieting did not increase cravings in one trial. Tips for coping:
  - Identify and avoid triggers.
  - Relax and exercise.
  - Drink about 64 oz of non-caloric fluid a day.
  - Eat the craved food mindfully in small portions.
  - Address the "why" behind non-hunger eating (boredom, loneliness, emotions) and build other coping skills.
- **Orthorexia:** An obsession with a "pure" or perfect diet that impairs daily life. Challenge the distorted beliefs behind it.

### Eating disorders
- **Anorexia nervosa:** The starvation effects listed above. Poor concentration limits how much a person can benefit from therapy until they are nourished. Deficiencies include scurvy, folate and zinc (which stay low after refeeding, so supplement), and possibly EFA abnormalities. Low cholesterol was linked to suicidality. There is neurochemical overlap with OCD.
- **Bulimia nervosa:** Cycles of losing and regaining a third to half of body weight. Vomiting and laxatives cause potassium and chloride loss, dental erosion and gut dysfunction. There is frequent comorbid substance use.
- **Binge-eating disorder:** Often comes with obesity, depression and personality disorders. Treated with CBT, and sometimes SSRIs or topiramate.
- **Genetics:** Twin studies show substantial heritability. MC4R mutations were present in all obese binge-eaters carrying them. Several chromosomal regions are linked to drive for thinness and obsessionality.
- ⚠️ The book cites DSM-IV-TR criteria; use DSM-5-TR today (which includes binge-eating disorder, ARFID and OSFED).
- **Screening tools:** EAT-26, Yale-Brown-Cornell Eating Disorder Scale, EDI-2 (see §7).

### Obesity and bariatric surgery
- Mood disorders are common in people seeking weight loss, so screen weight-management clients for them.
- Psychopathology tends to improve after surgical weight loss, and binge eating stops in the short term. But after about two years, anxiety, depression and self-control scores can return to pre-surgery levels, and suicides and alcohol problems have been reported. **Long-term psychological support is essential.**
- **Nutrition after surgery:** Monitor thiamin, folate, B12, vitamin D, calcium and iron at 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly. B12 often needs injections. Vomiting can cause Wernicke's.
  - Staged diet: clear liquids → high-protein liquids → purées → soft foods → regular textures.
  - Small portions (2–3 oz), 5–6 meals a day, fluids between meals.
  - Lifelong multivitamin/mineral and protein supplements as needed.

### Metabolic syndrome, diabetes and mood
- People with diabetes (especially type 2 on oral hypoglycemics) scored lower on cognitive tests. Early control may protect cognition.
- Depression, tension and anger predicted metabolic syndrome in women. Hostility was linked to insulin, triglycerides and waist size in older men.
- In teens with type 1 diabetes, poor control and repeated ketoacidosis predicted psychological problems.
- People with schizophrenia store more visceral fat, even without medication.

### Alcohol
- Alcohol provides calories but almost no nutrients. It raises nutrient needs and excretion and impairs absorption and use. The result can be deficiencies of thiamin (Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome), folate, B6, vitamin A, magnesium, zinc and calcium.
- Heavy drinkers substitute alcohol for food; drug users often become emaciated.
- Alcohol use disorder frequently co-occurs with anxiety, depression, bulimia and schizophrenia. Former dependence raised the risk of major depression 4.2-fold.
- Nutrition education improved psychological and family outcomes in substance-abuse treatment (Addiction Severity Index).
- Alcohol during pregnancy can cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders. Avoid alcohol if conception is possible.
- Screen psychiatric inpatients with **CAGE** or **AUDIT** (see §7).
- Alcohol can cause hypoglycemia in people with diabetes taking insulin.

### Schizophrenia
- Reduced intake of many nutrients alongside higher caffeine intake. Resting energy needs may be overestimated by standard formulas (about 280 kcal/day), which contributes to weight gain.
- EFA/phospholipid membrane abnormalities; high homocysteine and low folate/B12 in some patients. Supplement trials are mixed.
- Food paranoia (for example, when medication is hidden in food) can cause malnutrition. Work patiently with "safe" foods while trust builds.

### Depression and related conditions
- Linked with low omega-3, folate, B12, B6, vitamin E, low cholesterol, low HDL (a marker of inflammation), and low tryptophan availability (inflammation).
- Associated with osteoporosis (low bone density in depressed adults), so screen bone health.
- Folate deficiency was associated with depression in older Latina women even after fortification.
- Food insufficiency in teens was associated with dysthymia, thoughts of death and suicide attempts.

### Autism, behavior and criminality
- Autism: altered methylation and glutathione markers improved with folinic acid, betaine and methyl-B12 (a small study). Children with autism still need nutritionally adequate diets; restrictive eating is common. ⚠️ Vaccines and thimerosal do not cause autism (the book found no conclusive link, and later research confirms there is none).
- Violence and impulsivity were linked to low DHA and low serotonin metabolites.
- In an RCT, young adult prisoners given vitamin, mineral and EFA supplements cut minor disciplinary incidents by 33% and violent incidents by 37%, compared with 6.5% and 10% reductions on placebo (Gesch). Nutrition is adjunctive to rehabilitation.

## §3 Psychiatric medications and nutrition

| Drug class | Nutrition issues |
|---|---|
| **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram) | Appetite drop at first, then possible weight gain (paroxetine most). **Do not combine with 5-HTP, tryptophan or St John's wort.** |
| **Tricyclics** (amitriptyline, imipramine, nortriptyline) | Carbohydrate cravings and slower metabolism cause dose-dependent weight gain. They inhibit riboflavin coenzyme synthesis. |
| **MAOIs** (phenelzine, tranylcypromine; selegiline patch) | **Tyramine restriction:** aged cheese, red wine, yeast extracts, cured, fermented or spoiled meat and fish, fermented sausage, fava beans, sauerkraut, overripe fruit; limit caffeine and alcohol. Risk of hypertensive crisis. Continue the restriction for about 2 weeks after stopping. Store food properly and eat it fresh. |
| **Anticonvulsants / mood stabilizers** (valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, gabapentin, topiramate) | Weight gain, hyperinsulinemia. Biotin and folate depletion. |
| **Lithium** | Weight gain in one- to two-thirds of patients, thirst, edema, GI upset. **Keep salt and fluid intake consistent**, since sodium changes shift lithium levels. |
| **Atypical antipsychotics** (clozapine, olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine) | Major weight gain, insulin resistance, diabetes and lipid problems. An FDA warning applies. Check fasting glucose at baseline and regularly. Refer to weight management at about 5% weight gain; losing 2–15 lb brings real health gains. Molindone was the antipsychotic reported not to cause weight gain. |

Always consider baseline risk (age, race, and higher diabetes risk in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder) before blaming a medication.

## §4 Genetics, inborn errors and individuality

- **Inborn errors of metabolism** can cause intellectual disability (⚠️ the book uses the older term "mental retardation") and death if untreated. Newborn screening and diet treatment prevent harm:
  - **PKU:** A lifelong low-phenylalanine diet with a special formula. Women with PKU should restart the diet before pregnancy (maternal PKU). Some people respond to BH4.
  - **Galactosemia:** Lactose- and galactose-free diet (soy formula).
  - **Maple syrup urine disease:** Restrict branched-chain amino acids; some forms respond to thiamin.
  - **Homocystinuria:** Low-methionine diet; some forms respond to B6.
  - About 70 disorders of amino-acid metabolism are known.
- **Variant enzymes (Bruce Ames):** Roughly 50 genetic diseases, and many common variants (for example MTHFR), involve enzymes whose weak binding to their vitamin cofactor is partly overcome by high doses of that vitamin. This is a reason for **individualized, tested** therapy, not blanket megadosing. Deficiencies of folate, B12, B6, niacin and zinc damage DNA much as radiation does.
- **Alcohol-metabolism genes** (ADH1B, ALDH2) vary between populations and affect the risk of alcoholism.
- Theme: diet and environment influence how genetic potential is expressed (functional medicine / systems biology). Genomics may eventually allow personalized nutrient therapy.

## §5 Comprehensive Assessment of Nutritional Status (ANS)

This is a template adapted from the book's suggested form, inspired by Mary Ann Kight. The book offers it as a set of ideas, not a validated instrument.

1. **Health history:** Circle any that apply: anorexia, bulimia, bipolar disorder, depression, diabetes, food allergy, cancer, heart disease, hemochromatosis, kidney disease, intestinal disorder, osteoporosis, thyroid disorder, alcohol or substance use, bariatric surgery.
2. **Physical status / body composition:** Height, weight, BMI, weight change over 3–6 months, body fat (low: <20% for women, <10% for men), waist circumference.
3. **Diet:**
   - Eats fewer than 3 times a day?
   - Omits any food group?
   - 2–3 servings of dairy?
   - Added sugar ≤10% of calories?
   - Alcohol ≤1 drink/day (women) or ≤2 (men)?
   - Salt use?
   - Caffeine >400 mg/day?
   - Any nutrient below 100% of the DRI, or above 500% of the DRI / UL?
   - Eats mono- and polyunsaturated fats and fish?
4. **Labs, biochemistry and metabolism:**
   - Fasting glucose, 2-hour postprandial glucose, HbA1c.
   - Lipids (HDL, LDL, triglycerides).
   - EFA profile (EPA, DHA, omega-3 and omega-6).
   - Amino acids; homocysteine; phenylalanine metabolites.
   - Vitamins, preferably functional or enzyme tests: thiamin (TPP effect), B6, B12 (methylmalonic acid), folate, niacin, A, D, E, K.
   - Minerals: iron (Hct, TIBC, Hgb, MCV), zinc, magnesium, calcium (DEXA scan), chromium, selenium, potassium.
   - Heavy metals: lead, mercury.
5. **Clinical signs (nutrition-focused physical exam):** Tongue, lips, gums, teeth, skin, hair, nails; diarrhea (more than 2 loose stools a day), constipation, dental pain that affects eating.
6. **Drug–nutrient interactions:** Antidepressants (SSRI, tricyclic, MAOI), antipsychotic, anticonvulsant, lithium, hypoglycemics, laxatives, lipid-lowering drugs, other.
7. **Symptoms reported by the client:** Energy, appetite, attention, sleep, mood, headaches, irritability, concentration, nausea, and so on.
8. **Summary:** Risks in each area, stage of nutritional injury (§6), and the mental-health diagnosis or functional score for comparison over time.

## §6 Stages of nutritional injury

Assign the **highest stage present**:

| Stage | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Health, or possible risk of nutritional injury (genetics, host factors, environment) | Family history, genotype |
| **I** | Diminishing reserves or building excess; adaptation | Omits food groups, eats one meal a day, mega-dose supplements, fad diets |
| **II** | Reserves exhausted (Stage I lasting weeks or longer) | Same as above, sustained |
| **III** | Physiologic and metabolic changes (abnormal labs) | Weight change over 3–6 months; high cholesterol, glucose or homocysteine; relevant eating-disorder screening score |
| **IV** | Nonspecific signs and symptoms | Fatigue, headache, irritability, trouble concentrating, oral lesions, nausea, diarrhea, poor appetite |
| **V** | Illness | Metabolic syndrome, bulimia, gestational diabetes, alcohol dependence, medication with drug–nutrient interaction |
| **VI** | Permanent damage, or damage that responds poorly to treatment | Bariatric surgery (lost absorption), dementia, PKU damage, nerve damage |

Water-soluble vitamins can be depleted within weeks: thiamin in 1–2 weeks; B6, C and B2 in 2–6 weeks. Vitamin E takes months and vitamin A 1–2 years.

## §7 Screening tools named in the book

- **Depression:** Beck Depression Inventory; Hamilton Depression Rating Scale; CES-D; Zung Self-Rating Depression and Anxiety scales; PHQ-9 (usable by phone); Geriatric Depression Scale; a 3-item distress screen.
- **Cognition:** Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).
- **Eating disorders:** EAT-26; Yale-Brown-Cornell Eating Disorder Scale; Eating Disorder Inventory-2.
- **Alcohol:** CAGE (Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener); AUDIT (10 items).
- **Addiction severity:** Addiction Severity Index.
- **Overall health and quality of life:** SF-36; EQ-5D (validated in schizophrenia); Global Assessment of Functioning (⚠️ dropped in DSM-5).
- **Mental status assessment domains (Jarvis):** Appearance, behavior, cognition, thought processes. Also consciousness, language, mood and affect, orientation, attention, memory, abstract reasoning, thought content, perceptions; delirium (sudden onset) vs. dementia (slow onset).

## §8 Everyday guidance for brain health

**Food pattern** (aligned with the Dietary Guidelines; ⚠️ now MyPlate):
- Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, dairy, and lean protein including fish, poultry, beef and eggs.
- Fresh rather than processed foods.
- Some mono- and polyunsaturated fats, including omega-3s.
- Limit added sugar. Alcohol: no more than 1 drink/day for women or 2 for men (1 drink = ½ oz ethanol).
- Keep excesses of any single nutrient in check.

**What each food group contributes** (useful for estimating shortfalls when a group is omitted):
- **Dairy:** Calcium, riboflavin, protein, vitamins A and D, B12, potassium, magnesium.
- **Meat, fish and eggs:** Protein, B12, iron, zinc, niacin, B6, thiamin (pork), omega-3 (fish).
- **Grains:** Carbohydrate, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, folate, iron (if enriched); zinc, magnesium and fiber (if whole grain).
- **Vegetables and fruit:** Vitamin C, vitamin A (carotenes), folate, potassium, fiber, phytochemicals.
- **Fats and oils, nuts and seeds:** Essential fatty acids, vitamin E.

**Beyond food** (Tufts recommendations cited in the book):
- Aerobic exercise (for example, walk a mile or more a day).
- Mental stimulation: hobbies, reading, music, puzzles, clubs.
- Less TV.
- Blood pressure control.
- Social connection.
- Adequate sleep.
- Stress management.

**Mind–body–spirit:**
- Mindful eating: be present, savor, and express gratitude. Spiritual traditions honor food.
- The Slow Food movement.
- Biofeedback and volition in changing habits: habits resist change, so change one thing and let it ripple.
- Stress shifts cells from "growth" to "protection" mode, reducing nutrient intake (Lipton, a speculative model).
- Eating to fill emotional emptiness leaves people "wanting more." Attend to the inner need.

**For professionals:**
- Screen patients for nutritional risk.
- Take a diet history and do a nutrition-focused physical exam.
- Order appropriate labs and track change over time.
- Refer to a registered dietitian.
- Coordinate supplements with the prescriber.
- Document in records.
- Research needs rigorous, interdisciplinary methods and shared terminology, for example "high-calorie malnutrition," "subclinical deficiency," "nutrient-responsive condition."
