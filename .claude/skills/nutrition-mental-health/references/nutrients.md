# Nutrients and mental status

Organized by nutrient. Findings are summarized from *Linking Nutrition to Mental Health* (Leyse-Wallace, 2008) and the studies it reviews. **Doses are those reported in studies. They are not recommendations.** Evidence is often at the observational or small-trial level. Check current guidance before using any clinically.

Mechanisms the book proposes for how nutrients affect the mind:
1. Nutrients build and maintain the brain and nerves. EFAs make up myelin. Lead and mercury block enzyme sites. Inborn errors let toxic metabolites build up.
2. Nutrients supply energy and the machinery that produces it (glucose, the TCA cycle, B vitamins).
3. Nutrients influence which genes are switched on (folate and methylation, zinc).
4. At high doses some nutrients act like drugs, either compensating for variant enzymes or becoming toxic.
5. Food and nutrition affect mood secondarily: restriction, comfort eating, caffeine withdrawal.
6. Thoughts, emotions and stress may change how cells take up and use nutrients.

## Macronutrients

Typical adult needs are roughly 50–100 g protein, 100–350 g carbohydrate and 60–100 g fat per day. Enough carbohydrate and fat spares protein for growth and repair.

### Carbohydrate and blood glucose
- Glucose is the brain's preferred fuel. Healthy fasting blood glucose is about 70–100 mg/dL; 100–125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose.
- **Hypoglycemia** can be primary (not enough carbohydrate) or reactive (too much insulin after refined sugar).
  - Autonomic symptoms: palpitations, sweating, shaking, hunger.
  - Neuroglycopenic symptoms (brain short of glucose): confusion, drowsiness, poor concentration, speech difficulty, blurred vision.
  - The author also observed anxiety, nervousness, "fuzzy thinking," emotional swings, crying, trembling and fainting, usually 2–5 hours after eating.
  - In children with type 1 diabetes, glucose of 55–75 mg/dL slowed performance.
  - Short-term memory worsens at about 2.5 mmol/L (about 45 mg/dL).
- **Hyperglycemia** (above ~250 mg/dL) can also change mental status: fatigue and irritability. Rule out diabetes, tumors and medication effects.
- Long periods below the recommended carbohydrate intake bring tiredness and fatigue, and can alter mental functioning ("hitting the wall" in athletes).
- **Prevention pattern:**
  - Complex, high-fiber carbohydrate in mixed meals with protein and fat.
  - Eat every 3–5 hours, including breakfast. Some people do best with six small meals 2.5–3.5 hours apart.
  - Have sweets only in small amounts, at the end of a mixed meal.
  - Eat a minimum of about 100–130 g of complex carbohydrate a day, spread across waking hours.
  - Sample breakfasts: egg and whole-grain toast; whole-grain cereal and milk; yogurt, granola and banana; whole-grain bagel and peanut butter; whole-wheat crackers, fruit and nuts.
- **Carbohydrate and serotonin:** Carbohydrate raises insulin, and insulin pulls competing amino acids into muscle. That lets relatively more tryptophan into the brain and more serotonin gets made (Wurtman). But as little as about 4% of calories from protein in the meal blunts the effect, so the practical effect of an ordinary meal may be small. People over 40 felt calmer after a high-carbohydrate lunch but did worse on a distraction task.

### Fats and essential fatty acids
- Two fats are essential (the body can't make them): **linoleic acid** (omega-6) and **alpha-linolenic acid (ALA)** (omega-3). The longer-chain omega-3s **EPA** and **DHA** build nerve membranes and myelin. Nerve dendrites and synapses are about 80% lipid, and DHA makes up about 30% of neuronal fatty acids.
- The historical human diet had an omega-6 to omega-3 ratio of about 1–2:1. The modern Western diet is far higher. This imbalance changes membrane fluidity and serotonin and catecholamine signaling.
- **Depression:**
  - Populations that eat more fish have less depression. In cross-national data, Japan (high fish intake) had among the lowest depression rates; high-fish-consuming countries eat an estimated ~730 mg EPA+DHA/day.
  - Depressed and manic patients have lower omega-3 levels in red cells.
  - The Rotterdam Study found depressed older adults had a higher omega-6 to omega-3 ratio.
  - EPA and DHA act somewhat like lithium and valproate.
  - As an add-on to antidepressants, EPA at 1 g/day halved Hamilton scores in about 50% of patients (Peet & Horrobin).
  - Hibbeln's thresholds: 180–500 mg/day is inadequate, about 750 mg/day is possibly adequate, and 1 g/day is clearly adequate for lowering depression risk.
  - The 2006 APA committee concluded the evidence supports a protective effect of EPA/DHA in unipolar and bipolar depression, with weaker evidence for schizophrenia.
- **Bipolar disorder:** Stoll's trial used about 9.6 g/day of omega-3 (EPA-rich), added to usual treatment. An EPA:DHA ratio of about 3:1 was favored.
- **Schizophrenia:** Red-cell membranes are depleted of arachidonic acid (AA) and DHA, possibly because of faster phospholipid breakdown rather than low intake. Trial results are mixed: a Cochrane review found some benefit but no clear dose response, and Fenton's 3 g/day EPA trial found no benefit.
- **Other areas:**
  - Aging and cognition: fish-oil users in the Scottish 1936 birth cohort scored better cognitively at 64.
  - Alzheimer's: the Chicago study found a lower risk with more omega-3; the Rotterdam Study did not.
  - Impulsivity and violence: lower DHA was linked to lower serotonin metabolites in cerebrospinal fluid.
  - Autism: altered omega-6 to omega-3 ratios in one study.
- **Pregnancy and infancy:** EFAs and preformed DHA are critical in the third trimester and early breastfeeding. A depleted mother may be at risk of postpartum depression. Breast milk from a well-nourished mother is optimal.
- **Low cholesterol and low-fat diets:** Men with chronically low cholesterol (below ~4.5 mmol/L or ~174 mg/dL) had more depressive symptoms. Low cholesterol has been associated with suicide, homicide and accidents. Very low-fat diets risk shortfalls of EFAs and vitamin E.
- **Intake guidance in the book:**
  - Adequate Intake of linoleic acid: 17 g/day (men), 12 g/day (women).
  - ALA: 1.6 g/day (men), 1.1 g/day (women).
  - NIH: about 650 mg/day of omega-3. American Heart Association: about 1 g/day.
  - Eat **two 3–5 oz servings of low-mercury fish a week**.
- **Food sources:**
  - EPA/DHA: herring, salmon, mackerel, sardines, anchovy, trout, oysters. Salmon and herring give about 1.5 g per 3–4 oz.
  - ALA: flaxseed, canola, walnut and soybean oils, walnuts.
  - Omega-6: corn, sunflower and soybean oils.
- **Supplements:** Look for a USP seal, since the FDA does not regulate supplement quality. See §Safety for cautions.

### Protein and amino acids
- There are 20 amino acids. Nine are essential (must come from food) and others are conditionally essential. Adult protein needs are about 0.8 g/kg. Egg white and milk are the highest-quality proteins. Amino-acid supplements are often unbalanced.
- **Tryptophan** becomes serotonin (affects sleep, impulse control, aggression, pain, cravings). **Tyrosine** becomes dopamine and norepinephrine.
  - Tryptophan at ≥1 g helped people with mild insomnia fall asleep.
  - Tyrosine relieved depression in some patients.
  - A 1,000-kcal diet depleted tryptophan, and women with a history of depression regulated it less well.
- **5-HTP:**
  - A 2003 Cochrane review found it possibly better than placebo for depression, but the research quality was too poor to recommend it.
  - Typical doses reported: 300–900 mg/day in divided doses. Start low if nausea occurs.
  - It may increase satiety.
  - See §Safety for serotonin syndrome risk.
- **Lysine:** Lysine-fortified wheat reduced stress-induced anxiety in a Syrian community whose staple food was low in lysine.

## Vitamins

The general rule: vitamins are coenzymes (helper molecules) for enzymes, including brain enzymes. Deficiency diseases usually affect both the central and peripheral nervous systems. They rarely involve just one vitamin, and they often come with other signs of malnutrition.

**Who is at risk of low vitamin status:**
- persistent vomiting;
- heavy alcohol use;
- a poor-quality diet;
- eating disorders;
- malabsorption or gastrointestinal surgery;
- needing IV glucose;
- poor dentition;
- aging.

Several low intakes together may be riskier than any single one.

| Vitamin | Mental and neurological effects of deficiency | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Thiamin (B1)** | Anxiety, irritability, fatigue, poor sleep, personality change, depression. Beriberi symptoms look like "anxiety neurosis." Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome: confusion, apathy, disorientation, ataxia, eye signs, then lasting memory loss. | High-risk groups: alcoholism, hyperemesis gravidarum, vomiting after bariatric surgery, IV glucose given to depleted patients, raw fish/shellfish (thiaminases). Diets heavy in refined "junk" carbohydrate with high-sugar symptoms responded to thiamin (Lonsdale). In a placebo-controlled trial (Benton), healthy volunteers taking thiamin for two months reported feeling more clear-headed, composed and energetic. Test with red-cell transketolase / TPP effect. Emergency treatment is parenteral. Under 20% of Wernicke-Korsakoff patients recover fully. |
| **Riboflavin (B2)** | Fatigue from inefficient energy production. Sore throat, cheilosis, glossitis, dermatitis, anemia. | Tricyclics and phenothiazines inhibit flavin coenzyme synthesis. Riboflavin 400 mg has been studied for migraine. |
| **Niacin (B3)** | Pellagra: dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, death. Early signs: depression, insomnia, fatigue, anxiety, apathy. Later: confusion, hallucinations, memory loss, psychosis. | About 10 mg/day plus dietary tryptophan cures dietary pellagra. Hartnup disease and carcinoid syndrome need 40–200 mg. Low niacin intake was linked to about 70% more Alzheimer's risk (Chicago). Niacin for migraine is only hypothesis-generating. Orthomolecular megadose therapy for schizophrenia was rejected by the APA in 1973 and has not been replicated. |
| **Pyridoxine (B6)** | Irritability, depression, confusion. Needed to make GABA and serotonin. | Low B6 predicted depressed, fatigued and confused mood in bereaved men. Deficiency seizures occur in infants with genetic forms. **Toxicity:** ≥2 g/day (and lower chronic doses) causes sensory neuropathy. The adult DRI is 1.3–2.0 mg. |
| **B12 (cobalamin)** | Irritability, apathy, suspiciousness, paranoia, mood swings, memory loss, slowed thinking, depression, violence, "organic brain syndrome." Nerve damage (numbness, unsteadiness). **Can occur without anemia.** | High-risk groups: older adults (low stomach acid), pernicious anemia, Crohn's disease, GI surgery, bariatric surgery, vegans and macrobiotic diets. Macrobiotic-raised teens had lower fluid intelligence. High methylmalonic acid and homocysteine are more sensitive markers than serum B12. Folate masks the anemia but not the nerve damage. Treat early, since long-standing damage may be permanent. Oral doses of about 1,000 µg/day can normalize status in older adults. Typical multivitamins are inadequate after bariatric surgery. Low B12 was linked to depression independently of other factors (Rotterdam). |
| **Folate** | Depression (folate deficiency is common in depressed patients), insomnia, forgetfulness, irritability. Negative symptoms of schizophrenia (with high homocysteine). | Folate added to antidepressants improved response in a Cochrane review. Folate and B12 together keep methylation (SAM) working. The MTHFR variant raises homocysteine. Alcohol and many drugs worsen folate status. Low folate is linked to depression even in the era of food fortification. |
| **Biotin** | Depression, lethargy, hallucinations, numbness, muscle pain, rash, hair loss. | High-risk groups: raw egg whites, long-term TPN without biotin, anticonvulsants, long-term antibiotics, dialysis (intractable hiccups responded to 10 mg/day). |
| **Vitamin C** | Scurvy: fatigue, lassitude, depression, hypochondriasis, hysteria. Personality changes appear **before** physical signs. Physical signs: bleeding gums, petechiae, corkscrew hairs, splinter hemorrhages. | Needed to make norepinephrine from dopamine. Cases involved psychiatric patients on extremely limited diets (one dollar a day, food fads, anorexia). Improves within about a week on 500 mg twice daily. The adult DRI is 75–90 mg. |
| **Vitamin E** | Low plasma levels were found in depressed patients despite adequate intake. | An antioxidant in cell membranes. Food sources: oils, nuts (almonds), sunflower seeds. Low-fat diets risk low intake. The UL is 1,000 mg/day. |
| **Vitamin A** | Excess causes raised pressure in the skull, headache, irritability, dizziness. | Watch for toxicity from fish-liver oils and some supplements. |
| **Multivitamin/mineral** | In an RCT, healthy men on a B-complex/C/Ca/Mg/Zn supplement felt less anxious and stressed. Schoolchildren on RDA-level supplements gained nonverbal IQ. | A reasonable safety net for people with poor diets. It does not undo heavy alcohol use. |

## Minerals (elements)

- **Iron:** Deficiency impairs cognition, especially in children. Excess iron (hemochromatosis) and iron overload have been linked to psychiatric symptoms and to oxidative damage in Alzheimer's.
- **Zinc:** Needed by about 200 enzymes, for DNA synthesis, and for brain development. It is concentrated in the hippocampus. Deficiency impairs cognition and development. Zinc status changed how children with ADHD responded to stimulants and evening primrose oil. Zinc is lower and copper higher in criminal schizophrenic men and in alcohol withdrawal with delirium tremens or hallucinations. Toxicity is possible, since the requirement is about 10 mg/day.
- **Magnesium:** Low levels in alcohol withdrawal are linked to hallucinations. Supplementing magnesium to prevent delirium tremens is not recommended.
- **Chromium:** Case reports and a small trial suggest chromium picolinate reduced carbohydrate cravings and improved atypical depression. The evidence is limited.
- **Copper:** Wilson's disease (copper buildup) causes neuropsychiatric symptoms and is treatable with chelation and zinc.
- **Selenium:** The brain holds onto selenium preferentially. Supplement studies showed no clear mood benefit. Excess is toxic.
- **Iodine and thyroid:** Deficiency causes cretinism and hypothyroidism, whose symptoms include depression and slowed thinking. Check thyroid function when evaluating mental symptoms.
- **Toxic metals:**
  - **Lead:** No safe threshold for children. Effects on IQ, attention and reading appear below 5 µg/dL and last into adulthood. In adults it causes depression, confusion and memory problems. Low calcium, zinc and iron increase lead absorption.
  - **Mercury:** Methylmercury comes from fish. Organic mercury causes lassitude, timidity, memory loss, insomnia, excitability and delirium ("mad hatters"). Choose low-mercury fish (see §Safety).
  - **Manganese:** Occupational inhalation causes manganism, which resembles Parkinson's.
  - Thallium, cadmium and arsenic are mostly physical toxicities. Hair and nails can document exposure.

## Other substances

- **Caffeine:**
  - Peak blood level about 60 minutes after intake; half-life 3–7.5 hours.
  - It reduces fatigue and sleepiness and speeds simple tasks, but it can hurt fine motor control. Coffee 30–60 minutes before bed delays sleep and worsens its quality.
  - Much of the felt "benefit" is reversal of overnight withdrawal (James & Rogers). Withdrawal brings sleepiness, headache and lower alertness.
  - Keep intake under about 400 mg/day. Energy drinks' effects come mainly from caffeine.
- **Alcohol:** See `conditions-and-assessment.md` §2.
- **Chocolate:** Self-described "chocolate addicts" felt better briefly after eating it, then guilty. There is little evidence of true pharmacological addiction.

## Safety (always relay the relevant items)

| Item | Caution |
|---|---|
| 5-HTP / tryptophan | **Serotonin syndrome** (confusion, agitation, sweating, rigidity, and in severe cases coma) when combined with SSRIs, MAOIs, weight-loss drugs or St John's wort. Not established as safe in children, pregnancy, or liver or kidney disease. With carbidopa (Parkinson's) it can cause skin changes like scleroderma. A contaminated tryptophan supplement once caused eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome. |
| Fish oil / omega-3 | Avoid or supervise with blood thinners (warfarin, high-dose aspirin). Excess may raise bleeding risk, impair wound healing, raise LDL or suppress immunity. Fish-liver oils add vitamin A. Coordinate doses with the treating physician. Look for a USP-verified product. |
| Mercury | Pregnant women, women who may become pregnant, and children should avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel and tilefish, and limit albacore/fresh tuna. Low-mercury choices: shrimp, scallops, flounder, cod, whiting, porgy, croaker, salmon, sardines. Up to about 12 oz/week of low-mercury fish (FDA advice cited in the book). |
| Pyridoxine (B6) | Sensory neuropathy at high doses (≥2 g/day, and at lower chronic doses). |
| Niacin | Flushing. Liver toxicity at ≥1.5 g/day, especially with slow-release forms. |
| Vitamin A | Toxicity from preformed A in liver oils and supplements. Beta-carotene is safer. |
| Zinc, selenium, iron, other minerals | Narrow margin between need and toxicity. Stay under the UL unless directed by a clinician. |
| High-dose "orthomolecular" regimens | Evidence is weak or conflicting. Use only under clinician supervision, with testing. |
| All supplements | Tell every provider what you take, start or stop. |
