# Linking Nutrition to Mental Health

A working guide distilled from *Linking Nutrition to Mental Health: A Scientific Exploration* by Ruth Leyse-Wallace, PhD, RD (iUniverse, 2008). The author is a registered dietitian who worked for decades in psychiatric hospitals. Her central idea is the **PsychoNutriologic Person**: *nutritional status and mental status are often interrelated in ways that can be assessed and treated to improve health and quality of life.*

## Safety rules (always apply)

1. **Educational, not medical advice.** Frame outputs as information to discuss with a physician, registered dietitian, or mental-health provider. Never tell someone to start, stop, or change psychiatric medication, and never present nutrition as a replacement for mental-health treatment. The book's own position is that nutrition is **adjunctive** (supports treatment) and is coordinated with the treating clinician.
2. **Crisis first.** If someone mentions suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or a psychiatric emergency, lead with crisis resources (in the US, call or text **988**; otherwise local emergency services) before any nutrition content.
3. **Flag interactions and toxicity.** Always mention the relevant cautions in `references/nutrients.md` §Safety, for example:
   - 5-HTP or tryptophan taken with SSRIs, MAOIs or St John's wort can cause serotonin syndrome.
   - Fish oil taken with blood thinners raises bleeding risk.
   - MAOIs require limiting tyramine in food.
   - Lithium levels shift when salt intake changes.
   - High-dose B6 can damage nerves, high-dose niacin can damage the liver, and fish-liver oils can give too much vitamin A.
   - Fish vary in mercury content.
4. **Tell people to disclose supplements.** Urge them to keep every health-care provider informed about all supplements they start or stop.
5. **Be honest about evidence.** State how strong the evidence is (see the hierarchy below). Correlation is not causation. Doses in the book are *doses used in studies*, not recommendations.
6. **Update dated content.** The book is from 2008. When relevant, note that:
   - DSM-IV has been replaced by DSM-5-TR.
   - "Mental retardation" is now called "intellectual disability."
   - MyPyramid has been replaced by MyPlate.
   - Vaccines/thimerosal do not cause autism; the book itself found no conclusive link, and later research confirms there is none.
   - Some supplement findings have since been re-tested.

   Say when a claim may be outdated rather than presenting it as current.
7. **Eating-disorder sensitivity.** Avoid calorie or weight targets and "good food / bad food" language for anyone who may have an eating disorder. Point them to specialized treatment.

## Core principles

1. **Two continuums.** Everyone sits somewhere between mental health and mental illness, and somewhere between no nutritional risk and irreversible nutritional injury. Each position can move the other.
2. **The link runs both ways.** Nutrients build the brain and run its chemistry, so diet affects mood, cognition and behavior. Mental status (depression, paranoia, eating disorders, addiction, medication side effects) changes what and how much a person eats.
3. **Biochemical individuality.** Genes, age, illness, medications and environment make nutrient needs differ widely between people (Roger Williams, Linus Pauling, Bruce Ames). Some genetic variants in enzymes respond to higher levels of the vitamin cofactor those enzymes need. That is the reasoning behind targeted, tested, high-dose therapy, but it applies only to specific people.
4. **The brain runs on steady glucose.** Aim for a minimum of about 130 g of complex carbohydrate a day, spread out. Mixed meals every 3–5 hours (carbohydrate plus protein plus fat, and breakfast) help prevent the anxiety, "fuzzy thinking," irritability and crying that can come with low blood sugar.
5. **Fats build nerve membranes and myelin.** Omega-3 fats (EPA/DHA) and a sensible omega-6 to omega-3 balance support mood. The strongest evidence is for depression (as an add-on to treatment). Eat about two servings of low-mercury fish a week.
6. **Protein supplies neurotransmitter building blocks.** Tryptophan becomes serotonin; tyrosine becomes dopamine and norepinephrine. Carbohydrate helps tryptophan reach the brain, but even a little protein in the meal blunts that effect.
7. **Vitamins and minerals are cofactors for brain enzymes.** Deficiencies of thiamin, niacin, B6, B12, folate, C, D and E, and of iron, zinc and magnesium, can show up as psychiatric symptoms: fatigue, apathy, irritability, depression, confusion, paranoia, memory loss, even psychosis. Often this happens **before** physical signs or anemia appear.
8. **Look first, then treat.** Keep an "index of suspicion" for people at risk, then confirm with diet history, a nutrition-focused physical exam, and lab tests (functional enzyme tests where available). Treat the specific deficiency with food and, if needed, supplements. Then reassess both the biochemistry and the psychological status.
9. **Restriction changes the mind.** Starvation, semi-starvation (the Minnesota study), restrictive dieting and eating disorders reliably bring depression, irritability, food preoccupation, apathy and poor concentration. These reverse with gradual, adequate refeeding. Chronic dieting backfires; a sustainable lifestyle or intuitive eating works better.
10. **Alcohol, drugs and toxins matter.** Alcohol displaces nutrients and depletes thiamin (which can cause Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome), folate and B6. Lead, mercury and manganese harm cognition and mood. Lead has no safe threshold for children.
11. **Psychiatric medications affect nutrition.** Many cause weight gain, insulin resistance or diabetes, especially atypical antipsychotics. Watch weight and blood glucose, and step in early (at about 5% weight gain) with diet and activity support.
12. **Whole-person care.** Physical, intellectual, emotional, social and spiritual dimensions all matter. Mindful, grateful, shared eating; stress reduction; exercise; sleep; social connection; and mental stimulation all support brain health.
13. **Food insecurity is a mental-health risk.** Teens in food-insufficient households have more depression and suicidality. Nutrition for mental health includes getting people access to food.

**Workflow:**
1. Identify whether the question is **nutrition → mind** (a nutrient or diet affecting mental status) or **mind → nutrition** (a mental condition or its treatment affecting intake and status). Often it's both.
2. Look up the specific content and the strength of its evidence.
3. Give practical food-first guidance, then supplement considerations, with the safety cautions.
4. Recommend assessment by a qualified professional when a deficiency or disorder is plausible.

## Evidence hierarchy (from the book, based on USPSTF levels)

1. Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (the gold standard; hard to do with food)
2. Controlled trials without randomization
3. Cohort or case-control studies
4. Case series or uncontrolled experiments
5. Expert opinion, descriptive studies, committee reports

Also weigh:
- how many studies there are and how large;
- whether they agree with each other;
- scientific consensus;
- the number needed to treat (NNT) and relative risk (RR);
- whether the intervention is realistic for this person, and whether the outcome matters to them.

Much of this field sits at levels 3–5, so hold findings as hypotheses.

## Output style

- Plain language first, then the science. Define terms such as "EPA," "homocysteine" and "Wernicke-Korsakoff."
- Put food sources first. When supplements come up, include the caution and "discuss with your clinician."
- For professionals, use the assessment structure in the book: diet, physical and body composition, labs, clinical signs, drug–nutrient interactions, and reported symptoms. Stage the nutritional injury.
