# High-Priority Exotic Pest Threats (as listed in 2010)

Source: Manual pp. 8–9 and fact sheets pp. 28–40, drawn from the Nursery and Garden Industry Biosecurity Plan. Status reflects 2010: some of these may have since been detected in or spread within Australia, and scientific names may have been revised. Check current status with PHA or the state department before telling anyone a pest is "exotic". Never confirm a detection yourself; tell the user to report and let experts identify it. Every fact sheet's protective advice is the same: check the nursery frequently for new pests and unusual symptoms, know your common pests so you notice something different, and report anything unusual.

## Guava (Eucalyptus) rust — *Puccinia psidii* — impact HIGH–EXTREME
- **Hosts:** 100+ species, mainly Myrtaceae (eucalypts, melaleuca, guava, Malaysian apple etc.).
- **Look for:** brown to grey lesions on actively growing leaves, shoots, fruit and sepals, surrounded or covered by masses of bright yellow to orange spores; older lesions may have purple margins. Severe infection in young trees kills shoot tips (loss of leaders, bushy growth); also prolific branching, galling, persistent lesions, stem swellings, leaf distortion.
- **Confused with:** other fungi causing shoot dieback, especially under extreme conditions. Rule: report *any* rust symptom on Myrtaceae.
- **Spread:** vast numbers of spores by wind and rain splash; also on clothing, equipment, vehicles; infected plant material.
- **Where (2010):** native to South America; established through the Americas, India, Japan.
- **Why it matters:** native biodiversity, commercial operations, forestry, wildflowers, streetscapes and gardens.

## Glassy-winged sharpshooter — *Homalodisca vitripennis* — impact HIGH
- **Hosts:** 200+ species.
- **Look for:** 12–14 mm dark-brown to black leafhopper with yellow dots on head and thorax, translucent wings with reddish veins, lighter underside; sits visibly on leaves and stems. Watery excrement showing as white spots. Egg clutches (up to 27) laid side by side just under the leaf surface on the underside, giving a blistered look. Leaf scorch if *Xylella fastidiosa* is present.
- **Main threat:** highly efficient vector of *Xylella fastidiosa* (Pierce's disease in *Vitis*, leaf scorch in *Prunus*); also direct feeding damage and excrement "showers".
- **Confused with:** native Australian sharpshooters; head shape and size help, but species ID needs an expert.
- **Spread:** strong-flying adults; nymphs walk and jump; long distance mostly as egg masses on nursery stock or crops.
- **Where (2010):** eastern and western USA, Mexico; recently Tahiti and the Cook Islands.

## Serpentine leafminer — *Liriomyza huidobrensis* — impact HIGH
- **Hosts:** 40+ species incl. vegetables, cut flowers, grain crops.
- **Look for:** mines in leaves (adults and larvae rarely seen). Mines white with damp black and dried brown areas, serpentine or irregular, widening as larvae grow. Adult: 1–2.5 mm black fly, yellow head and yellow spots on thorax.
- **Damage:** stippling from female feeding/egg laying; internal mining; secondary fungal infection through mines and punctures; mechanical virus transmission.
- **Confused with:** many Australian Agromyzidae flies, which rarely attack economic crops.
- **Spread:** flight within crops; long distance in plant material carrying larvae.
- **Where (2010):** Africa, the Americas, Europe, Asia, parts of Oceania.

## Asian gypsy moth — *Lymantria dispar* — impact HIGH
- **Hosts:** 650+ species; forest, horticultural and urban trees.
- **Look for:** egg masses (2–3 cm × 1–2 cm) covered in yellowish/tan fuzz on solid objects (trees, rocks, outdoor furniture, machinery, structures), the stage most likely to be found. Hairy caterpillars 3–65 mm; mature ones have a double row of spots along the back, usually five pairs blue then six pairs red. Heavy defoliation; webbing from larval "ballooning". Males greyish-brown, 30–40 mm wingspan; females white with grey markings, 40–70 mm.
- **Confused with:** other moths in the same family; refer any match to an expert.
- **Spread:** young larvae balloon several km on wind; adults fly up to 40 km before mating (as stated in the manual); eggs hitchhike on nursery stock, plant parts, vehicles, machinery, equipment.
- **Where (2010):** Asia (eastern Russia, China, Korea, Japan); repeated North American introductions under eradication; introduced to New Zealand in 2003 and eradicated.

## Sudden oak death — *Phytophthora ramorum* — impact HIGH
- **Hosts:** 130+ tree, shrub and herbaceous species (oaks, rhododendron, Douglas-fir, tanoak etc.); commonly found in nurseries. Unlike most *Phytophthora*, it attacks aerial parts (stems, trunks, leaves).
- **Look for:** in shrubs, diffuse dark brown spots/blotches with fuzzy margins usually starting at the leaf tip, spreading over the leaf; blackened shoots. In trees, trunk canker oozing thick burgundy-red to tar-black sap; sharp edge between healthy and dead tissue in young trees; stem necrosis kills the crown.
- **Confused with:** other bleeding-canker fungi (but this one affects only aerial parts), other foliar pathogens, sunburn. Typically irregular necrotic lesions rather than distinct spots.
- **Spread:** wind- and water-borne spores; infected plant material, water, growing media and compost that touched infected plants; long distance via ornamental/nursery stock trade and infected green waste.
- **Where (2010):** North America and Europe only.

## Citrus longicorn beetle — *Anoplophora chinensis* — impact MEDIUM
- **Hosts:** 100+ woody species, especially citrus and other fruit trees; kills young/small plants.
- **Look for:** round exit holes 6–11 mm at the trunk base, sap bleeding from egg sites, trunk swellings at pupal chambers, frass (sawdust) at the base. Adult shiny black with 10–12 irregular white spots on wing covers; males to 21 mm, females to 37 mm (the summary page says 20–40 mm); banded antennae 1.2–2× body length. Eggs creamy-white, 2 × 6 mm, turning yellow-brown before hatching. Larvae creamy-white, black head, legless, to 60 mm, inside wood.
- **Confused with:** large native beetles (>2 cm) and the Asian longicorn beetle (also exotic). Rule: report any large beetle with antennae longer than its body.
- **Spread:** flies over 1 km; long distance as eggs, larvae, pupae in woody nursery stock.
- **Where (2010):** endemic through Asia; isolated populations in Europe and North America.

## Poinsettia thrips — *Echinothrips americanus* — impact MEDIUM
- **Hosts:** 100+ species, particularly greenhouse and ornamental crops.
- **Look for:** light spots like typical mite/thrips damage, more on lower leaf surfaces; many shallow punctures giving shrunken tissue; reduced leaf size and lighter colour; numerous black specks (faecal droppings); also feeds on flowers. Adults up to 1.6 mm, dark brown with red internal pigment, slender forewings with light and dark bands, bicoloured brown/yellow legs, mostly yellow antennae. Larvae yellow to pale green with bright red eyes and rows of hairs along the abdomen.
- **Confused with:** common greenhouse thrips (*Heliothrips haemorrhoidalis*), which lacks red pigment and yellow legs and has paler forewings.
- **Risks:** potential virus vector; disrupts IPM programs.
- **Spread:** adults fly; larvae walk and hitchhike on people and animals; long distance via infested nursery stock.
- **Where (2010):** eastern USA (mostly south), Europe, Japan, Taiwan, Thailand.
