# Soil properties and processes

Distilled from Weil & Brady, *The Nature and Properties of Soils* (15th ed.), chapters 1–12. Numbers are typical values from the text; local conditions vary.

## §1 Soil formation (Ch. 1–2)

- **Soil vs regolith:** Soil is the biologically active, horizonated upper part of the regolith (the loose material over bedrock). The **pedosphere** sits within the **critical zone**, the layer from the tops of trees to the bottom of groundwater.
- **Weathering:**
  - Physical: temperature, frost, abrasion, roots.
  - Chemical: hydrolysis, hydration, dissolution, carbonation, oxidation, acid attack, complexation.
  - Resistant minerals such as quartz persist; primary silicates weather into clays and oxides.
- **Parent materials:**
  - Residual (from bedrock in place).
  - Colluvial (slope deposits).
  - Alluvial (floodplains, terraces, fans).
  - Marine and lacustrine.
  - Glacial (till, outwash).
  - Eolian (loess, sand dunes, volcanic ash).
  - Organic (peat).
  - Human-transported (urban fill).
- **Factors** (Jenny's equation: soil = f(climate, organisms, relief, parent material, time)):
  - **Climate:** precipitation drives leaching, clay formation and acidity; temperature drives reaction rates and the organic matter balance.
  - **Organisms:** vegetation (grasses produce deep, dark, organic-rich A horizons — Mollisols; forests produce O and E horizons), fauna, microbes and humans.
  - **Topography:** slope, aspect and landscape position create **catenas** (drainage sequences from summit to toe slope).
  - **Time:** from young Entisols to ancient, highly weathered Oxisols.
- **Four processes:** additions, losses, transformations, translocations (for example clay moving down to form an argillic horizon, podzolization, calcification, gleying).
- **Horizons:**
  - **O** (organic).
  - **A** (topsoil, mineral with humus).
  - **E** (eluviated, bleached).
  - **B** (illuvial or altered subsoil: Bt clay, Bh/Bs humus and sesquioxides, Bk carbonates, Bw color/structure).
  - **C** (little-altered material).
  - **R** (bedrock).
  - Suffixes add detail, e.g. **g** (gleyed), **p** (plowed), **ss** (slickensides).
  - Topsoil holds most of the roots, organic matter and nutrients; subsoil governs drainage, rooting depth and water storage.
- **Urban soils:** Disturbed, compacted and layered fill, often with high pH (concrete), contaminants (lead) and poor aeration. Assess them before landscaping or food growing.

## §2 Classification (Ch. 3)

**USDA Soil Taxonomy** is based on measurable properties and diagnostic horizons:
- surface epipedons: mollic, umbric, ochric, histic, melanic;
- subsurface horizons: argillic, natric, spodic, oxic, cambic, calcic, gypsic, petrocalcic, fragipan, duripan;
- moisture regimes: aquic, udic, ustic, aridic, xeric;
- temperature regimes.

Hierarchy: order → suborder → great group → subgroup → family → series.

**12 orders:**

| Order | Key feature | Typical setting and use |
|---|---|---|
| Entisols | Little profile development | Floodplains, dunes, steep slopes |
| Inceptisols | Beginning of B horizon (cambic) | Young landscapes, mountains |
| Andisols | Volcanic ash, allophane, high P fixation, low density | Volcanic regions; fertile but fix P |
| Gelisols | Permafrost, frost churning | Arctic; carbon-rich, sensitive to warming |
| Histosols | Organic soils (peat, muck) | Wetlands; subside when drained; huge C stores |
| Aridisols | Dry, often calcic/gypsic/salic | Deserts; need irrigation; salinity risk |
| Vertisols | Swelling clays, cracks, slickensides | Shrink-swell; hazardous for foundations |
| Mollisols | Thick, dark, base-rich A (mollic) | Prairies; among the most productive |
| Alfisols | Argillic horizon, moderate leaching, >35% base saturation | Temperate forests; productive |
| Ultisols | Argillic horizon, highly leached, acid | Warm humid regions; need lime and fertilizer |
| Spodosols | Spodic (humus/Al/Fe) horizon, sandy, acid | Conifer forests; infertile |
| Oxisols | Oxic horizon, highly weathered Fe/Al oxides | Tropics; low CEC, P fixation, good structure |

International systems: WRB (Reference Soil Groups such as Chernozems, Ferralsols, Podzols, Vertisols), plus the Canadian and Australian systems (book Appendix A).

## §3 Physical properties (Ch. 4)

- **Color** (Munsell hue/value/chroma):
  - Dark usually means organic matter.
  - Red/yellow means oxidized iron (good drainage).
  - Gray with mottles (**redoximorphic features**) means saturation and reduction. This is key for judging drainage, wetlands and septic suitability.
- **Texture:** sand 2–0.05 mm, silt 0.05–0.002 mm, clay < 0.002 mm (USDA). There are 12 textural classes on the texture triangle (loam is balanced). Field "feel" method: ribbon length and grittiness. Texture sets surface area, water holding, CEC, drainage and workability.
- **Structure:** granular, platy, blocky, prismatic, columnar, massive, single grain.
  - Aggregates form through flocculation (Ca²⁺ helps; Na⁺ disperses), wetting–drying and freezing–thawing, roots and hyphae (glomalin), earthworms, microbial glues (polysaccharides), and humus.
  - Aggregates are destroyed by tillage, traffic on wet soil, raindrop impact on bare soil, sodium, and loss of organic matter.
- **Tilth guidelines:**
  1. Minimize tillage (moldboard plowing, disking, rototilling).
  2. Traffic and till only when the soil is at or below field capacity.
  3. Mulch.
  4. Add residues, compost and manure.
  5. Include sod or perennials in the rotation.
  6. Use cover crops and green manures.
  7. Use gypsum and/or polyacrylamide (PAM) on irrigated or crusting soils.

  Commercial "humate" conditioners at low rates generally show no proven benefit in research.
- **Density:**
  - Particle density is about 2.65 Mg/m³.
  - Bulk density (Db) is typically 1.0–1.6 Mg/m³: lower in aggregated, organic-rich soils; higher in sands, subsoils and compacted layers (compact subsoils and glacial till can exceed 2.0).
  - Porosity = 1 − Db/Dp.
  - Root growth becomes restricted as Db rises, at roughly >1.6 in clays, >1.75 in silts and >1.8 in sands.
  - Compaction causes include wheel traffic (plan controlled-traffic lanes, lower axle loads and tire pressure), plow pans and livestock.
  - Remedies: deep-rooted cover crops ("bio-drilling," e.g. forage radish), subsoiling (short-lived), and adding organic matter.
- **Pores:** Macropores (>0.08 mm) drain and aerate; micropores hold water. Biopores from roots and worms are key for infiltration.
- **Engineering properties:**
  - Atterberg limits (plastic and liquid limits).
  - Shear strength and bearing capacity.
  - Shrink-swell potential (smectite, Vertisols).
  - Collapsible soils; quick clays.
  - Frost heave in silts.
  - Use Unified or AASHTO classifications for engineering.

## §4 Soil water and the hydrologic cycle (Ch. 5–6)

- Water is polar, so it undergoes adhesion, cohesion and capillarity. Smaller pores produce a higher capillary rise.
- **Water potential (ψ)** = matric (attraction to solids) + osmotic (solutes) + gravitational (+ pressure). Water flows from higher to lower ψ.
- **Key states:**
  - Saturation.
  - **Field capacity** (≈ −10 to −30 kPa, 1–3 days after wetting): maximum useful water, adequate aeration, near the lower plastic limit, so the upper limit for tillage.
  - **Wilting point** (−1500 kPa).
  - **Hygroscopic** water (about −3100 kPa and drier).
  - **Plant-available water** = FC − WP. It is highest in silt loams and organic-rich soils, and low in sands and in heavy clays (where much water is held too tightly).
- **Flow:** Saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat) depends on macropores and texture; unsaturated flow is slow. Layers and textural contrasts (for example a coarse layer under fine soil) hold water above them, which is why pots stay wetter than field capacity (a perched water table).
- **Infiltration and percolation:** Maximize them with surface cover and aggregation. Preferential flow through macropores carries chemicals quickly toward groundwater.
- **Fate of precipitation:** interception, runoff, infiltration, evapotranspiration (ET), drainage and recharge. "Green water" is soil water used by plants; "blue water" is streams and aquifers.
- **Soil–plant–atmosphere continuum:** Transpiration is driven by the vapor-pressure deficit. Control of ET:
  - reduce evaporation with residue mulch and canopy cover;
  - favor transpiration (productive water use);
  - weed control;
  - windbreaks.
- **Drainage:** Surface drains and subsurface tile raise aeration and trafficability. Wetlands need wetness; don't drain hydric soils without permits.
- **Septic drain fields:** Need moderately permeable, unsaturated soil above the seasonal high water table (use redox features to judge). Unsuitable: very slow permeability, shallow water table, shallow bedrock, or excessively rapid drainage (poor treatment). Alternatives: mound systems.
- **Irrigation:**
  - Schedule by soil water depletion (for example, refill at 50% of available water depleted) or ET.
  - Methods and typical efficiency: furrow ~60%, surge ~80%, sprinkler ~80%, LEPA 90–95%, drip ~95%.
  - Micro-irrigation plus mulch raises water-use efficiency.
  - In arid areas, include a leaching requirement (§8).

## §5 Aeration and temperature (Ch. 7)

- Water displaces air. Aeration needs roughly >10–20% air-filled porosity. O₂ moves by diffusion (10,000× slower in water).
- **Redox (Eh) sequence** as O₂ runs out: O₂ → NO₃⁻ (denitrification) → Mn⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ (gray colors) → SO₄²⁻ (H₂S) → CO₂ (methane).
  - Results: N loss as N₂/N₂O, Mn/Fe solubility (toxicity), methane emissions, root death in most plants.
- **Wetlands and hydric soils:** Defined by hydrology, hydric soils and hydrophytic vegetation. They provide filtering, flood control, habitat and C storage. Delineate them by redox features.
- **Urban aeration:** Compacted, paved sites suffocate tree roots. Use structural soils, aeration and mulch; avoid grade changes over roots.
- **Temperature:**
  - Controls biological rates (Q₁₀ ≈ 2), germination (for example, corn needs roughly >10 °C) and nutrient uptake.
  - Moist soil warms slowly because water has high specific heat.
  - Dark, dry, bare soil warms fastest.
  - Mulch cools and stabilizes; plastic mulch warms.
  - Drainage warms soils in spring.
  - Frost action heaves plants and foundations.
  - Thermal properties matter for ground-source heat pumps and buried cables.

## §6 Colloids and ion exchange (Ch. 8)

- **Colloids** (< 1 µm) have enormous surface area and charge, making them the seat of chemical activity.
  - **1:1 clays** (kaolinite): low activity, CEC ~1–15 cmolc/kg, little shrink-swell.
  - **2:1 clays:** smectite (high CEC 80–150, swelling), vermiculite (highest, 100–200; fixes K and NH₄⁺), fine mica/illite (K-rich), chlorite.
  - **Fe/Al oxides:** low CEC, pH-dependent charge, strong P fixation.
  - **Allophane** (volcanic).
  - **Humus:** CEC 150–300+, pH-dependent.
- **Charge:**
  - Permanent charge from isomorphous substitution.
  - pH-dependent charge on edges, oxides and humus (CEC rises with pH).
  - **Inner-sphere** complexes are tightly bound (e.g. phosphate on oxides); **outer-sphere** complexes are exchangeable.
- **Cation exchange:** reversible, charge-for-charge, affected by mass action. Adsorption strength: Al³⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺ = NH₄⁺ > Na⁺.
  - Whole-soil CEC is about 1–50 cmolc/kg. Estimate it from clay type and amount plus organic matter.
  - **Base saturation** = the share of CEC held by Ca, Mg, K and Na.
  - The "ideal Ca:Mg ratio" (basic cation saturation ratio) concept is **not** supported by research. Focus on sufficiency levels instead.
- **Anion exchange** occurs on oxides at low pH (nitrate, sulfate retention in some tropical subsoils).
- **Pesticide sorption:** Koc and Kd. Leaching risk is high for low-Koc, persistent compounds in sandy, low-organic-matter soils over shallow groundwater.
- **Biomolecules:** Clays and humus bind enzymes, DNA and prions, protecting or inactivating them.

## §7 Soil acidity (Ch. 9)

- **Causes:** carbonic and organic acids, leaching of base cations, NH₄⁺ fertilizer nitrification, legume N fixation, plant uptake imbalance, acid deposition, oxidation of sulfides (acid sulfate soils; never drain or excavate pyritic materials without planning), and erosion.
- **Aluminum:** Below pH ~5.5, Al³⁺ dissolves. It is toxic to roots, and its hydrolysis produces H⁺.
- **Pools:** active (solution) + exchangeable (salt-replaceable) + residual (bound). Buffering: the reserve acidity can be 1,000 times or more the active acidity, so lime needs depend on the **buffer pH** and CEC, not just pH.
- **Measure:** pH in water (1:1 or 1:2), pH in CaCl₂ (about 0.5 units lower), buffer pH (SMP, Mehlich, Sikora) for lime requirement. **Sample shallow (0–5 cm) separately** where amendments were surface-applied or no-till is used (see the rooftop-garden case in the book).
- **Biological effects:**
  - Nutrient availability is best around pH 5.5–7.0 (about 1 unit lower in organic/potting media).
  - Low pH: Al and Mn toxicity; Ca, Mg and Mo deficiency; P fixation by Fe/Al; reduced nitrification and rhizobia.
  - High pH: Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu and B deficiency; P fixation by Ca.
  - Crops differ: blueberries and azaleas prefer 4.5–5.5; alfalfa, asparagus and cantaloupe prefer about 7.
- **Liming:**
  - Materials: ground limestone (calcitic CaCO₃; dolomitic adds Mg), burned or hydrated lime (fast, caustic), wood ash, and by-products (sugar-beet lime, slag).
  - Effectiveness depends on neutralizing value (CCE) and fineness.
  - Mix into the root zone; lime moves slowly downward. Apply months before sensitive crops. Split high rates.
  - Don't overlime (it induces micronutrient deficiencies).
- **Alternatives:**
  - **Gypsum** supplies Ca and reduces subsoil Al (moves deeper than lime) but does not raise pH.
  - Organic matter complexes Al.
  - Choose Al-tolerant species and varieties.
- **Lowering pH:** elemental sulfur, oxidized by bacteria. It takes weeks to months and warm, moist conditions. Be patient and don't double-apply. Iron sulfate or aluminum sulfate act faster. Acidifying N fertilizers (ammonium sulfate) also lower pH.

## §8 Dry-region soils: alkalinity, salinity, sodicity (Ch. 10)

- Dry-region features: high pH, CaCO₃ (calcic) and gypsum horizons, desert pavement, biological soil crusts, "islands of fertility" under shrubs. Watch for Fe, Zn and P deficiency and possible B, Mo or Se excess.
- **Measures:**
  - **EC** (electrical conductivity of the saturated paste extract, ECe, dS/m).
  - **SAR** = Na⁺ / √((Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺)/2), concentrations in mmolc/L.
  - **ESP** (exchangeable sodium percentage).
- **Classes:**

| Class | ECe (dS/m) | ESP / SAR | pH | Physical condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saline | > 4 | < 15 / < 13 | < 8.5 | Good structure; osmotic stress |
| Saline–sodic | > 4 | > 15 / > 13 | < 8.5 | OK while salty; disperses if leached without Ca |
| Sodic | < 4 | > 15 / > 13 | > 8.5 | Dispersed, crusted, very slow infiltration |

- **Plant effects:** osmotic stress (roughly >4 dS/m hurts many crops; tolerance varies widely: beans are sensitive, barley and cotton tolerant) and specific-ion toxicity (Na, Cl, B).
- **Irrigation water quality:** EC and SAR together (low-EC, high-SAR water is especially dispersive); also bicarbonate, B and Cl.
- **Reclamation:**
  - Saline: leach with good-quality water, which requires **drainage**. Leaching requirement ≈ ECw/ECdw.
  - Sodic and saline–sodic: first replace Na⁺ with Ca²⁺ (gypsum) or H⁺ (sulfur, acids), then leach.
  - Then manage: salt-tolerant crops, planting on the sloping side of beds, careful irrigation scheduling, monitoring.
- About 20% of the world's farmland is irrigated and produces about 45% of food. Irrigation in arid lands is inherently prone to salinization and demands constant management.

## §9 Soil organisms and ecology (Ch. 11)

- Enormous diversity: macrofauna (earthworms, ants, termites, beetles, millipedes), mesofauna (mites, springtails), microfauna (nematodes, protozoa), fungi (saprotrophs, mycorrhizae), bacteria and archaea, algae, and roots.
- **Functions:**
  - Decomposition and humus formation.
  - Nutrient mineralization (the microbial loop: protozoa and nematodes grazing bacteria release N).
  - N fixation, nitrification and denitrification.
  - Aggregation and bioturbation (earthworms are ecosystem engineers).
  - Pathogen suppression.
  - Degrading pollutants.
- **Rhizosphere:** Root exudates feed microbes, and chemical signaling recruits allies. **Mycorrhizae:** arbuscular (most crops) and ectomycorrhizal (many trees). They extend P and water uptake, improve aggregation (glomalin) and support disease resistance. Tillage, bare fallow, high soluble P and non-host crops (brassicas) reduce them.
- **Controls on activity:** food supply (organic C), moisture and aeration, temperature, pH (fungi tolerate acid better than bacteria), nutrients.
- **Plant damage:** pathogenic fungi (Pythium, Fusarium, Rhizoctonia), nematodes, some insects. **Suppressive soils** arise from diverse, active microbial communities built with organic amendments and rotation.
- **Earthworms:** Increase infiltration and mixing. Invasive species in northern forests remove the O horizon (a harm). Encourage them in farm soils with residue cover and less tillage.

## §10 Soil organic matter and carbon (Ch. 12)

- **Carbon cycle:** Soils hold more carbon than vegetation and the atmosphere combined. Soil respiration is a large flux; land-use change releases soil C.
- **Decomposition:**
  - Rate depends on residue quality: C:N, lignin and polyphenols slow it. Legumes decompose fast; straw and wood decompose slowly.
  - Also on environment (warm and moist is fast; anaerobic or cold is slow), placement (incorporated residues decompose faster than surface ones) and soil protection (aggregates, clay).
  - **Priming:** fresh inputs can speed decomposition of existing SOM.
- **C:N ratio:** Microbes need about 24:1 C:N in their food. Residues with **C:N above about 25:1** immobilize soil N temporarily (the "nitrate depression period"); residues below about 20:1 release N. Soil humus is about 10–12:1.
- **Pools:**
  - **Active** (microbial biomass, particulate organic matter, soluble C): nutrient cycling, aggregate stability, food for biota. Lost first under cultivation, and responds quickly to management.
  - **Slow.**
  - **Passive** (mineral-associated, aggregate-protected, char): CEC, water holding, persistence for centuries or millennia.
  - Modern view: persistence depends mainly on physical and chemical protection, not intrinsic "recalcitrance."
- **Benefits:** aggregation, water holding, CEC and buffering, nutrient supply (N, P, S), chelation of micronutrients, biological diversity, dark color (warming), sorption of pollutants.
- **What sets SOM levels:** climate (higher in cool, moist conditions), drainage (higher when poorly drained), vegetation (grasslands with deep roots are highest), texture (clay protects), management.
- **Building SOC:**
  - Maximize plant production and return residues (including roots).
  - Cover crops and perennials.
  - Manure and compost.
  - Reduce tillage.
  - Control erosion.
  - Supply N (legumes), since humus needs N.
  - Diverse rotations.
  - Grazing management.

  Gains are finite and slow, and reversible if practices stop.
- **Climate change:** Soils can sequester C and cut N₂O and CH₄ (N management, drainage and rice water management). Warming risks C loss from peat and permafrost. The "4 per 1000" type goals.
- **Composting:**
  - Thermophilic composting passes through three stages: mesophilic → thermophilic (50–75 °C; kills weed seeds and pathogens; turn for oxygen) → curing (mesophilic recolonization, including disease-suppressive microbes).
  - Starting mix C:N about 25–35:1, moist like a wrung-out sponge.
  - Finished C:N about 10–20:1; volume shrinks 30–60%; 50–75% of the C is lost while mineral nutrients are conserved.
  - Metals are not destroyed, so check the feedstocks.
  - Benefits: safe storage, easier handling, stable N, no immobilization, pathogen and weed kill, a potting-mix component.
  - Vermicomposting (worms) runs at lower temperatures.
