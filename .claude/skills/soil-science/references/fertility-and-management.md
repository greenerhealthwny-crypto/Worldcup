## §1 Nitrogen and sulfur

### Nitrogen: the most limiting nutrient and the biggest polluter
Plants take up more N than any other mineral nutrient, typically 1–5% of dry matter. It is also the nutrient that most often escapes to water and air.

**The N cycle in soil:**
- **Mineralization:** microbes decompose organic N to NH₄⁺. More than 90% of soil N is organic; only about 1–3% of that mineralizes each year.
- **Immobilization:** microbes take up mineral N when they decompose low-N residues (C:N above about 25:1). This is temporary, but it can starve a crop during the first weeks after straw, sawdust or wood chips are incorporated.
- **Nitrification:** NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ by nitrifying bacteria and archaea. The process is fast in warm, moist, well-aerated soils, and it acidifies the soil (it releases H⁺). Nitrification inhibitors slow it.
- **Nitrate leaching:** NO₃⁻ is an anion that is not held by CEC, so it moves with drainage water. It causes groundwater contamination (drinking-water limit 10 mg NO₃-N/L) and hypoxic "dead zones" in coastal waters such as the Gulf of Mexico and Chesapeake Bay.
- **Denitrification:** NO₃⁻ → N₂O → N₂ in waterlogged, low-O₂ soils with available carbon. It wastes fertilizer, and **N₂O is a potent greenhouse gas** (about 300× CO₂) that also depletes ozone. It is useful in riparian buffers and constructed wetlands. **Anammox** (anaerobic ammonium oxidation) is another route to N₂.
- **Ammonia volatilization:** NH₃ loss from urea or manure left on the surface, especially at high pH, in warm windy conditions, or on residue-covered no-till. Incorporate the material, or apply it just before rain or irrigation, or use urease inhibitors.
- **Biological N fixation (BNF):** N₂ → NH₃ by nitrogenase.
  - Symbiotic fixation by *Rhizobium*/*Bradyrhizobium* in legume nodules. Pink nodule interiors mean they are actively fixing. It can supply 50–300+ kg N/ha/yr.
  - *Frankia* on alder and other actinorhizal plants.
  - Cyanobacteria (e.g., *Azolla*–*Anabaena* in rice paddies).
  - Free-living and associative fixers.
  - Inoculate legume seed when that legume hasn't been grown recently. High soil nitrate suppresses fixation.
- **Ammonium fixation** in 2:1 clays such as vermiculite and illite.

**Practical N management:**
- Match N rate to realistic yield goals **and** credit the other N sources: soil organic matter, previous legume (often 50–150 kg N/ha), manure, and irrigation-water nitrate.
- Use split applications and side-dressing near the time of peak crop uptake rather than a large preplant dose. Avoid fall-applied N on drained or leaching-prone soils.
- Use diagnostic tools: the pre-sidedress nitrate test (PSNT), chlorophyll meters or canopy sensors, end-of-season stalk nitrate test, and tissue tests.
- Use cover crops as "catch crops" (cereal rye scavenges leftover nitrate) and legume covers as N sources.
- Consider enhanced-efficiency fertilizers (slow- or controlled-release, urease or nitrification inhibitors).
- **Nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE):** a crop typically recovers only about 30–50% of applied N. US corn grain produced per kg of fertilizer N has risen over recent decades through better timing and hybrids. That trend shows N can be managed better.

### Sulfur: increasingly deficient
- S is part of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, and of proteins and oils. Crucifers (canola, brassicas) and legumes need a lot of it.
- Crops need much less S than N (on the order of 10 kg S/ha vs about 100+ kg N/ha).
- Deficiency is becoming more common because clean-air rules have cut atmospheric S deposition, high-analysis fertilizers contain little S, and yields are higher.
- Deficiency looks like N deficiency, but it shows on **younger** leaves (S is not very mobile in the plant).
- Most soil S is organic. Sulfate (SO₄²⁻) leaches, though less than nitrate, and is adsorbed in acid, Fe/Al-oxide-rich subsoils.
- Sources: gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O; also improves the subsoil and supplies Ca), ammonium sulfate, K-Mg sulfate, elemental S (must oxidize first, which acidifies the soil, so it is also used to lower pH), and manure.
- **Acid sulfate soils:** draining or excavating pyrite-rich coastal or mine materials oxidizes sulfides to sulfuric acid, which can drop pH below 3.5. Keep these soils flooded or lime them heavily. Mine spoils cause acid mine drainage.
- In waterlogged soils, sulfate reduces to H₂S (rotten-egg smell, toxic to rice roots).

## §2 Phosphorus and potassium

### Phosphorus: the double-edged sword
- **Scarce in the soil solution:** often less than 0.1 mg P/L. Plants depend on diffusion to roots, mycorrhizae, and root exudates.
- **Fixation (sorption and precipitation):**
  - In acid soils, P binds to Fe and Al oxides and precipitates as Fe/Al phosphates.
  - In calcareous soils, it forms Ca phosphates.
  - **Availability peaks around pH 6–7.**
  - Highly weathered soils (Oxisols, Ultisols, and Andisols with allophane) fix enormous amounts of P.
- **Management:**
  - Band P near the seed row rather than broadcasting it on high-fixing soils.
  - Keep pH near 6.0–6.5.
  - Add organic matter (organic anions compete for sorption sites).
  - Encourage mycorrhizae (avoid long fallows and excessive P).
  - Build up P, then maintain it by replacing crop removal.
- **Environmental side:**
  - P moves mostly attached to eroded sediment, and dissolved P runs off from high-P surface soils.
  - Even small amounts in fresh water cause **eutrophication** (algal blooms, cyanobacterial toxins, hypoxia).
  - Legacy P in over-fertilized, manured soils keeps leaking for years.
  - Tools: the **P site/risk index** (combines source factors — soil test P, rate, method, timing — with transport factors — erosion, runoff, distance to water, drainage), erosion control, buffer strips, and no manure on frozen or saturated ground.
  - Base manure rates on P, not N, where soil test P is high.
- **Finite resource:** mined rock phosphate is non-renewable and concentrated in a few countries (Morocco holds most reserves). There is no substitute for P in biology. Recycle it from manure, biosolids and food waste, and recover struvite from wastewater.

### Potassium
- K is needed in large amounts, often **5–10× P uptake** and near N levels. It regulates stomata, enzymes and water relations, so deficiency lowers drought and disease tolerance and quality.
- Deficiency symptoms: marginal scorching of **older** leaves (K is mobile in the plant). Forages and fruits need a lot.
- **Soil pools:**
  - Solution K and exchangeable K are quickly available.
  - Nonexchangeable (fixed) K is trapped between layers of illite and vermiculite and released slowly.
  - Mineral K in feldspars and micas is very slow.
  - Soils rich in micaceous clays buffer K supply; sandy and highly weathered soils do not.
- **Luxury consumption:** forages take up far more K than they need. Split applications on hay fields.
- High K can induce Mg deficiency (grass tetany in cattle grazing high-K, low-Mg forage).
- Sources: potassium chloride (muriate of potash, most common), potassium sulfate, K-Mg sulfate, wood ash, manure.
- **Nutrient budgets:** US agriculture's overall balance is roughly N surplus +5.1, P +0.3, K −1.4 million Mg/yr. K is being mined in many fields. Budget inputs against removals.

## §3 Calcium, magnesium, and silicon

- **Calcium:**
  - Deficiency is rarely a soil problem except in very acid, low-CEC soils. It is usually a problem of **distribution within the plant**: blossom-end rot of tomato, bitter pit of apple, tipburn of lettuce. These come from uneven water supply and transpiration more than from low soil Ca.
  - Fix these with steady irrigation and foliar Ca, not more soil Ca.
  - Sources: lime, gypsum.
- **Magnesium:**
  - Deficiency shows as interveinal chlorosis of older leaves.
  - Most common on acid, sandy soils and where heavy K or NH₄⁺ is applied.
  - Use **dolomitic lime** if the soil also needs liming; otherwise use Epsom salt or K-Mg sulfate.
- **Base cation saturation ratio (BCSR):** "Ideal Ca:Mg:K ratios" (e.g., 65:10:5) have little research support. Crops grow well over a wide range of ratios as long as each nutrient is sufficient. **Use the sufficiency-level approach**, not the ratio approach, to avoid needless purchases.
- **Silicon:** "quasi-essential" or beneficial. It strengthens cell walls. Rice, sugarcane and grasses accumulate it. It improves resistance to lodging, pests, fungal disease, drought, salt and metal stresses. Highly weathered and organic soils can be low in Si. Sources: calcium silicate slag, wollastonite.

## §4 Micronutrients

**Essential micronutrients:** Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni. Cobalt is needed by legume N fixation, and Na is needed by some C4 plants.

**Key ideas:**
- **The window between deficiency and toxicity is narrow**, especially for B, and for Cu and Mo in livestock forages. Test the soil or tissue first. Don't add "insurance" micronutrients.
- **pH controls availability.**
  - Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu and B become **less** available as pH rises. That causes iron chlorosis in calcareous soils and when "acid-loving" plants (blueberries, azaleas, pin oak) are grown at high pH.
  - **Mo** does the opposite: it is more available at high pH, so liming often fixes Mo deficiency (e.g., whiptail in cauliflower, poor legume nodulation).
  - Mn toxicity is common in very acid soils and in waterlogged soils (reduced Mn²⁺).
- **Chelates:** organic molecules (EDTA, DTPA, EDDHA, natural humic and fulvic acids, root exudates, siderophores) keep metal cations soluble. For calcareous soils, **Fe-EDDHA** is stable enough to work; Fe-EDTA is not.
- **Where deficiencies occur:**
  - Zn: high-P, eroded or leveled calcareous soils; corn, beans, rice.
  - B: sandy, leached, low-organic-matter soils and dry spells; alfalfa, brassicas, root crops.
  - Cu: peats and mucks.
  - Mn: high-pH, organic or overlimed sandy soils; soybeans, small grains.
- **Strategies:**
  1. Adjust soil pH (the most economical fix where feasible; acidify with elemental S for Fe/Mn problems).
  2. Band or seed-row applications of sulfates or chelates.
  3. Foliar sprays for fast in-season correction (Fe, Mn, Zn).
  4. Choose tolerant or "Fe-efficient" cultivars.
  5. Add organic matter or manure (natural chelation, a broad micronutrient supply).
  6. Seed treatments (Mo, Zn).
- **Human and animal nutrition link:** Zn- and Se-deficient soils produce low-Zn, low-Se foods and forages. Zn-enriched fertilizers (agronomic biofortification) can raise grain Zn. Se can be toxic in some seleniferous western US soils (e.g., the Kesterson Reservoir episode).

## §5 Practical nutrient management

**The three goals:**
1. Supply enough nutrients for optimal plant growth and quality.
2. Balance inputs with removals over time (neither mine the soil nor build excess).
3. Prevent environmental damage (water, air, soil).

Include economics: the most economic rate is below the maximum-yield rate.

### Diagnosis
- **Soil testing:** measures the *available* fraction with a calibrated extractant (e.g., Mehlich-3, Bray-1, Olsen for calcareous soils, ammonium acetate).
  - The result is an *index* interpreted as very low, low, optimum, high or very high. Only the low categories predict a yield response.
  - Use a lab whose methods are calibrated for your region, and keep the same lab to track trends.
- **Soil sampling** (the biggest source of error):
  - Take **12–15 or more cores** in a zig-zag pattern across each uniform area, mix them in a clean plastic bucket, and send about 250 g (roughly 1 pint).
  - Depth: the plow layer (0–15/20 cm) for tilled fields. For **no-till, pasture and lawns**, also sample **0–5 cm** separately (lime and P stratify near the surface). Use deeper samples for nitrate (0–30/60 cm) and for subsoil pH.
  - Sample distinct areas separately: different soils, slopes, cropping history, eroded knolls, old barnyards or manure-pile sites. Avoid fence lines, fertilizer bands and dead furrows.
  - Sample at the same season each time, every 2–3 years.
  - Air-dry samples (no oven or heat, which alters K and other results). Galvanized or brass tools contaminate Zn and Cu.
  - Grid or zone sampling feeds precision agriculture (§8).
- **Plant tissue analysis:** sample the specified part at the specified stage and compare to sufficiency ranges. Good for micronutrients, perennial crops and troubleshooting.
- **Visual symptoms:**
  - Older leaves first (mobile nutrients): N, P, K, Mg.
  - Younger leaves first (immobile nutrients): S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Ca.
  - By the time symptoms show, yield is already lost ("hidden hunger").
- **Omission (missing-element) strips and plots**, and PSNT for corn N.

### Nutrient sources
- **Inorganic fertilizers:**
  - Analysis is labeled N–P₂O₅–K₂O. To convert to element: P = P₂O₅ × 0.44; K = K₂O × 0.83.
  - Common N sources: anhydrous ammonia (82% N), urea (46%), UAN solutions (28–32%), ammonium nitrate (34%), ammonium sulfate (21% N, 24% S).
  - Common P sources: MAP (11-52-0), DAP (18-46-0), TSP (0-46-0).
  - Common K source: potassium chloride (0-0-60).
  - Ammonium-based N sources acidify; account for this in lime programs.
- **Animal manures:**
  - Variable composition, so test them.
  - Much of the N is organic and releases over several years. The NH₄⁺ fraction is lost unless incorporated.
  - The N:P ratio (about 2–4) is lower than crop needs (about 7–8), so N-based manure rates build up P. Limit rates to crop P needs where soil P is high.
  - Store and apply to avoid runoff. Composting stabilizes manure and reduces pathogens and weeds.
- **Biosolids** (treated sewage sludge): a valuable source of N, P and organic matter, regulated for pathogens and trace metals (US EPA Part 503 "Class A/Class B"). Watch for emerging contaminants: PFAS, pharmaceuticals, microplastics.
- **Other organic by-products:** food-processing wastes, crop residues, compost, green manures, cover crops.
- **Combine organic and inorganic sources.** Integrated nutrient management gives the most stable yields and builds organic matter. In sub-Saharan Africa, small doses of fertilizer plus manure or residue ("microdosing," fertilizer trees such as *Faidherbia*) raise yields much more than either alone.

### The "4 R" approach
Use the **right source** at the **right rate**, **right time** and **right place**:
- Band or inject near roots rather than broadcasting.
- Split N applications.
- Incorporate manure.
- Don't spread on frozen, snow-covered or saturated soil, or near waterways or wells.
- Keep records and write a nutrient management plan (required for large livestock operations, CAFOs).

## §6 Soil erosion and its control

**Scale:**
- Erosion is the leading form of soil degradation worldwide.
- The US loses on the order of **4 billion Mg/yr** to water and wind erosion. Croplands have averaged more than 10 Mg/ha/yr in the past, and losses fell substantially after conservation programs began in the 1980s.
- Soil forms very slowly (about 0.5–1 Mg/ha/yr under good conditions).
- **Tolerable soil loss (T value):** usually 5–11 Mg/ha/yr (2–5 tons/acre/yr) for deep soils, lower for shallow ones. Many ecologists consider this too high.

**On-site damage:** loss of the organic-rich topsoil, nutrients and water-holding capacity; exposed subsoil; gullies; lower yields.
**Off-site damage** (often costlier): sediment in reservoirs and streams, turbidity, P-driven eutrophication, dredging costs, harm to fish habitat, dust and air quality.

**Water erosion stages:** raindrop splash detaches particles (the reason cover is critical) → sheet → rill → gully.

**RUSLE (Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation):** A = R × K × LS × C × P
- **A:** predicted average annual soil loss.
- **R:** rainfall/runoff erosivity (climate).
- **K:** soil erodibility. Silty, low-organic-matter, weakly structured soils are the most erodible; sands and well-aggregated clays are less.
- **LS:** slope length and steepness.
- **C:** cover and management. This is the factor a farmer controls most. It ranges from about 0.001 (forest, dense sod) to 1.0 (clean-tilled fallow).
- **P:** support practices (contouring, strip cropping, terraces).
- RUSLE2 and WEPP are the process-based successors.

**Controls:**
- **Keep the soil covered.** Conservation tillage leaves at least **30% residue cover** after planting. No-till can cut erosion by 90% or more vs moldboard plowing. Also use cover crops, mulch, sod-based rotations, and pasture or perennials on steep land.
- **Slow and spread the water:** contour tillage, strip cropping, terraces, grassed waterways (for concentrated flow), vegetative barriers (stiff grass hedges such as vetiver), riparian buffers, filter strips.
- **Gullies:** divert the water above the gully, shape and vegetate it, and add check dams. Stabilize before it grows.
- **Construction sites** (erosion rates can be 10–100× cropland):
  - Phase the grading to limit bare area and time.
  - Preserve topsoil and stockpile it.
  - Mulch and hydroseed quickly.
  - Use silt fences, straw wattles, sediment basins, erosion-control blankets and check dams.
  - Divert clean runoff around the site.
  - Local stormwater permits apply.
- **Wind erosion:**
  - Modes: saltation (most movement), suspension (dust and PM10/PM2.5 health hazard), creep.
  - Controls: keep surface residue and cover; windbreaks and shelterbelts; roughen the surface (clods, ridges perpendicular to the wind); strip cropping crosswise to the wind; keep the soil moist.
  - The Dust Bowl is the classic lesson.
  - Wind Erosion Equation: E = f(I, K, C, L, V).
- **Tillage erosion:** plows move soil downslope, thinning topsoil on knolls. Reduce tillage passes.
- **Land capability classification (USDA I–VIII):**
  - Classes I–IV are suitable for cultivation with increasing limitations.
  - Classes V–VIII are best for pasture, range, forest, wildlife or recreation.
  - Match land use to capability.

## §7 Soil pollution, contaminated sites, and remediation

**Soils as filters.** Soils filter, sorb and degrade huge amounts of contaminants, but that capacity is finite. The best strategy is **prevention**.

### Organic contaminants
Pesticides, petroleum hydrocarbons, solvents, PCBs, PAHs, explosives, and emerging contaminants: PFAS, pharmaceuticals, microplastics.
- **Fate:**
  - Sorption to organic matter. **K_oc** (organic-carbon partition coefficient): high K_oc means the chemical is strongly held and less leachable.
  - Volatilization, leaching (worst in sandy, low-organic-matter soils over shallow water tables), plant uptake, and chemical and microbial degradation.
  - Persistence is expressed as half-life.
  - Aged residues become less bioavailable.
- **Remediation:**
  - **Bioremediation:** stimulate native microbes with O₂ (bioventing), nutrients or moisture; landfarming petroleum; bioaugmentation.
  - **Phytoremediation:** plants and their rhizosphere degrade, contain or take up contaminants. It is slow and suited to shallow, moderate contamination.
  - Physical and chemical methods: soil vapor extraction, soil washing, thermal treatment, excavation and landfill. These are fast but expensive and destroy soil function.

### Inorganic contaminants (trace elements and heavy metals)
Lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, chromium, nickel, zinc, copper, selenium.
- **Sources:**
  - Lead: leaded gasoline and lead paint. Urban soils, especially near old houses and busy roads, commonly exceed safe levels.
  - Arsenic: pesticides in old orchards, CCA-treated lumber, mining, some geologic sources.
  - Cadmium: phosphate fertilizers, biosolids, smelters.
  - Mining and smelting; industrial sites; biosolids and manures (Cu, Zn in swine and poultry feed).
- **Behavior:** metals don't degrade; they persist. Mobility and bioavailability are **lowest at near-neutral to high pH and high organic matter**, except As, Se and Mo, which are more mobile at high pH as anions.
- **Remediation and management:**
  - Lime to pH ≥ 6.5 to immobilize Pb, Cd and Zn (these are more available in acid soil).
  - Add P to form insoluble Pb phosphates (pyromorphite).
  - Add compost and organic matter.
  - Cap with clean soil or mulch.
  - Phytoextraction with hyperaccumulator plants (slow).
  - Excavate hot spots.
- **Urban gardens:**
  - Test for Pb (and As if near old orchards or treated wood) **before** growing food.
  - Where levels are elevated, use raised beds with clean soil or compost over a barrier, keep pH near neutral, add organic matter, and mulch paths.
  - Wash and peel root crops. Favor fruiting crops over leafy greens and roots.
  - Keep children from ingesting soil (the main exposure route). Wash hands, and remove shoes indoors.
  - Consult extension and health agencies for interpretation.

### Biosolids and wastes
Regulations cap cumulative metal loadings and pollutant concentrations. Ongoing research concerns PFAS and other emerging contaminants in biosolids. Some US states now restrict land application.

### Landfills and waste disposal
- **Site selection uses soil properties:** low-permeability clay liners (plus geomembranes), deep water tables, and no floodplains, karst or sandy aquifers. A final cap and leachate collection are required.
- Landfill gas contains methane (a greenhouse gas; can be recovered for energy).
- Prime farmland should not be used for landfills.
- **Septic system drain fields** depend on soil percolation, depth to water table or bedrock, and texture. Use the soil survey's septic-limitation ratings (see `soil-properties.md` §4).

### Radionuclides and radon
- Radionuclides such as Cs-137 and Sr-90 come from fallout and accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima). Cs is held tightly by micaceous clays; K fertilization reduces its uptake.
- **Radon (Rn-222):** a radioactive gas from uranium decay in soils and rocks, the second leading cause of lung cancer.
  - It enters buildings through foundation cracks, driven by pressure differences.
  - Soils and bedrock high in uranium (some granites, shales, phosphatic materials) and highly permeable soils raise the risk.
  - **Test the home.** The US EPA action level is 4 pCi/L (148 Bq/m³). Mitigate with sub-slab depressurization and by sealing entry points.

**Safety note:** For suspected contamination (lead, arsenic, petroleum, solvents, radon), get professional sampling and testing, and follow state and national environmental agency guidance. Don't attempt DIY remediation of hazardous sites.

## §8 Soil survey, GIS, and precision agriculture

- **Soil survey:**
  - Soil scientists map soils by relating landscape position, vegetation and parent material to profiles described in pits and auger borings (the soil–landscape paradigm).
  - **Map units** (consociations, complexes, associations) always contain **inclusions**. Maps at 1:12,000–1:24,000 can't resolve small areas, so verify on site for critical uses (septic, foundations).
  - The USDA **Web Soil Survey** (free online) gives map units, properties, and interpretations for crops, septic, buildings, roads, erodibility and hydric soils.
  - Soil series are named for a locality.
- **Tools:**
  - GPS and **GIS** layering (soils, topography, land use).
  - Remote sensing: aerial and satellite imagery, multispectral and hyperspectral, LiDAR for terrain.
  - Proximal sensing: electromagnetic induction (EC_a), ground-penetrating radar, gamma-ray, VNIR spectroscopy.
  - **Digital soil mapping:** statistical and machine-learning predictions from environmental covariates, with uncertainty estimates.
- **Uses:** land-use planning (protect prime farmland and wetlands, direct development to suitable soils), engineering, conservation plans, hydric-soil and wetland delineation, and carbon accounting.
- **Precision agriculture:**
  - Yield monitors and grid or zone soil sampling produce **management zones**.
  - Variable-rate application of lime, fertilizer and seed; guidance and auto-steer; controlled traffic.
  - Benefits: fewer over-applied inputs, less environmental loss, better economics. It works best where within-field variability is large and understood.

## §9 Soil health, cover crops, and sustainable systems

### Soil health (soil quality)
The capacity of a soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains plants, animals and people, regulates water and air, and resists degradation.

- **Indicators** (a minimum data set combining biological, chemical and physical measures):
  - **Biological:** active carbon (permanganate-oxidizable C, POXC), respiration (CO₂ burst), potentially mineralizable N, microbial biomass, earthworm counts, enzyme activities, ACE protein.
  - **Chemical:** pH, soil test P and K, CEC, EC, organic matter.
  - **Physical:** aggregate stability (wet sieving or slake test), infiltration rate, bulk density, penetration resistance, available water capacity, rooting depth.
  - Scoring frameworks such as the Cornell CASH and USDA-NRCS assessments combine these into a rating.
- **Resistance and resilience:** a healthy soil *resists* damage (drought, compaction, erosion) and *recovers* quickly after disturbance.
- **Principles (NRCS "soil health principles"):**
  1. Minimize disturbance (tillage, chemical, compaction).
  2. Maximize soil cover.
  3. Maximize continuous living roots.
  4. Maximize biodiversity (rotations, cover-crop mixes).
  5. Integrate livestock where feasible.

### Cover crops
- Functions: erosion control, N scavenging (cereal rye, oats, radish), N fixation (hairy vetch, crimson clover, other legumes), better structure and infiltration, weed suppression, biological "bio-drilling" of compacted layers (forage radish), habitat for beneficial organisms, and carbon inputs.
- **Management keys:**
  - Choose species for the goal and the planting window. Mixes combine benefits.
  - Terminate at the right time: rolling and crimping, herbicide, tillage, or winter-kill.
  - Manage N immobilization after high-C:N cereal covers (legume mixes, timing).
  - Watch soil moisture use in dry regions.

### Sustainable, conservation and organic agriculture
- **The Green Revolution** (improved varieties, fertilizer, irrigation) greatly increased food supply but brought problems: soil degradation, nutrient pollution, salinization, and loss of crop diversity. The challenge now is "sustainable intensification": more food per hectare with less environmental harm.
- **Conservation agriculture:** minimal tillage, permanent soil cover, diverse rotations.
- **Organic farming:** relies on legumes, manure, compost and rotations, with no synthetic fertilizers or pesticides.
  - Often builds soil organic matter and biology.
  - Yields are usually somewhat lower, with the gap narrower in good systems.
  - It can still lose N or P from heavy manure or compost use.
- **Agroforestry:** alley cropping, fertilizer trees, silvopasture.
- **Perennial crops and integrated crop–livestock systems.**
- **Resource-poor farmers** (especially in the tropics): the focus is low-cost soil improvement.
  - Legume intercrops and relays, fertilizer trees.
  - Microdosing small amounts of fertilizer; water harvesting (zaï pits, half-moons, tied ridges).
  - Mulching, conservation agriculture.
  - Liming or P amendments where soils are strongly acid or P-fixing.
  - Secure land tenure, so farmers invest in their soil.

### Biochar: hype or hope?
- Biochar is charcoal made by pyrolysis of biomass and applied to soil. It is inspired by Amazonian *terra preta* (dark earths).
- **Likely benefits:**
  - Very persistent carbon (centuries or longer), so it can sequester C.
  - Liming effect.
  - Improved CEC and water retention, especially in sandy or highly weathered acid soils.
  - Habitat for microbes.
- **Limitations:**
  - Results vary widely with feedstock, pyrolysis temperature and soil.
  - Often little benefit in fertile temperate soils.
  - Freshly made char can sorb nutrients or herbicides (charge it with compost or nutrients first).
  - Costly at the high rates used in trials.
  - Contaminants are possible if feedstocks are dirty.
- **Verdict:** a useful tool for specific conditions, not a universal cure.

### Soils and climate
- Soils hold more carbon than the atmosphere and vegetation combined.
- Building soil organic carbon ("4 per 1000" initiative) helps mitigate climate change but reaches a new equilibrium after decades. Gains are reversible if management changes.
- Also manage N₂O (N rate and timing, drainage) and CH₄ (flooded rice: alternate wetting and drying).
- Protect peatlands and wetlands, the largest soil carbon stocks. Draining them releases massive CO₂.

| Particle density (mineral) | 2.65 g/cm³ |

- 1 cmolc/kg = 1 meq/100 g
- 1 kg/ha ≈ 0.892 lb/acre; 1 lb/acre ≈ 1.12 kg/ha
- 1 Mg/ha ≈ 0.446 ton (US)/acre
- 1 dS/m = 1 mmho/cm; total dissolved salts ≈ 640 × EC (mg/L)
- P₂O₅ × 0.436 = P; K₂O × 0.830 = K
- 1 bar = 100 kPa = 0.1 MPa
