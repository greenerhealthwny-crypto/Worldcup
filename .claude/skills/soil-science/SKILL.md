# Soil Science (Weil & Brady)

A working guide distilled from *The Nature and Properties of Soils*, 15th edition, by Ray R. Weil and Nyle C. Brady. This is the standard university soil-science textbook: 20 chapters from soil formation to soil health in the Anthropocene. The central view: **soil is a living, three-dimensional natural body, and an ecosystem in its own right. It performs essential ecosystem services and must be managed as a system.** "If we take the time to learn the language of the land, the soil will speak to us."

## Core principles

1. **Soils perform six ecosystem functions:**
   - medium for plant growth;
   - regulator of water supplies;
   - modifier of the atmosphere (C, N and other gases);
   - recycler of raw materials and wastes;
   - habitat for organisms;
   - engineering medium.

   Judge any management decision against all six, not only yield.
2. **Soil is about half solids and half pores.** A good surface soil is roughly 45% mineral, about 5% organic matter, and 50% pore space shared between water and air. Water and air compete for the same pores.
3. **Five soil-forming factors:** climate, organisms (including people), topography (relief), parent material and time. They act through **four processes**: additions, losses, transformations and translocations. The horizons (O, A, E, B, C, R) record this history; read the profile before prescribing.
4. **Texture is fixed; structure is manageable.** You can't change sand/silt/clay proportions in a field, but you can build or destroy aggregation. Protect structure by:
   - minimal tillage;
   - no traffic when wet;
   - keeping the soil covered;
   - roots year-round (cover crops, sod in rotation);
   - returning organic residues.

   "Nature takes good care of soil structure": emulate natural systems.
5. **Water moves from high to low water potential.**
   - Field capacity ≈ −10 to −30 kPa (after 1–3 days of drainage).
   - Wilting point ≈ −1500 kPa.
   - Plant-available water lies between the two.
   - Maximize infiltration, minimize evaporation (mulch, residue, shade), and favor transpiration.
6. **Colloids are the chemical engine.** Clays and humus carry charge (mostly negative), which gives cation exchange capacity (CEC). They hold nutrients against leaching and filter pollutants. Humus supplies most of the CEC in topsoil; clay supplies most in subsoil.
7. **pH is the master variable.** For most mineral soils, nutrient availability is best at about pH 5.5–7.0 (about 1 unit lower for organic soils and potting mixes). Humid regions acidify naturally, and the process is sped up by N fertilizer and acid deposition. Correct with lime (or gypsum and organic matter for subsoil aluminum), or choose acid-tolerant plants. Arid regions tend toward high pH and salts; manage with leaching and drainage, and use gypsum for sodicity.
8. **Soil is alive.** Microbes, fungi (including mycorrhizae), fauna ("ecosystem engineers" such as earthworms) and roots run decomposition and nutrient cycling and build aggregates. The rhizosphere is one of Earth's richest habitats. Feed it with diverse, living plant cover.
9. **Organic matter is the linchpin.**
   - The active pool (microbial biomass, particulate organic matter) drives fertility and structure.
   - The passive pool (protected humus, char) provides CEC and water holding and persists for centuries.
   - Soil C:N stays near 12:1, so building organic matter requires nitrogen (legumes).
   - Residues with C:N above about 25:1 temporarily immobilize nitrogen.
   - Build soil organic carbon with residues, cover crops, perennials, manure and compost, reduced tillage and erosion control. It is also a climate lever.
10. **Nutrient management has three goals:** (1) supply enough for plants, (2) balance inputs with removals, (3) prevent pollution.
    - Test the soil. Don't fertilize by habit or "for insurance."
    - Nitrogen is the most widely limiting nutrient and also the biggest polluter (nitrate leaching, eutrophication, N₂O).
    - Phosphorus is a double-edged sword: deficient in much of the world, excessive near livestock operations, a finite resource ("there are no substitutes for phosphorus").
    - Recycle nutrients. Combining organic and inorganic sources beats either alone.
11. **Cover is the best erosion control.** Keep at least 30% residue cover (the definition of conservation tillage), use rotations with sod crops, contour farming, strip cropping and terraces, and controls on construction sites. Erosion by water, wind and tillage is the top form of soil degradation, followed by acidification. "Poor land makes poor people, and poor people make poor land."
12. **Soil health = a living, self-regulating system.** Promote plant and microbial diversity (cover crops, diverse rotations), minimal disturbance, continuous living roots and organic inputs. Measure with biological, chemical and physical indicators, and value resistance and resilience.
13. **Use soils information.** Soil surveys, GIS and digital soil maps say what should go where. Protect prime soils from sprawl and landfills, and match land use to land capability.

**Workflow for a site or soil problem:**
1. Gather context: location and climate, land use and goals, parent material and landscape position, the soil survey map unit, history (tillage, amendments, fill).
2. Get data: a soil test (pH, buffer pH, P, K, Ca, Mg, organic matter, CEC, EC/SAR if arid or irrigated), texture, a look at the profile (depth, color and redox features, compaction layers), and plant symptoms.
3. Diagnose the limiting factor. Water, aeration, pH, a nutrient, salts, compaction or biology — fix the most limiting first.
4. Prescribe food-first/soil-first practices (organic matter, cover, rotation, reduced traffic) plus targeted amendments at rates from the soil test and local extension guidance.
5. Monitor: retest every 2–3 years (sooner for problem fields), track indicators, and adapt.

## Output style

- Explain the mechanism in plain language, then give practical, rate-based recommendations with units (kg/ha or lb/acre; cmolc/kg; dS/m; kPa).
- Defer to local soil-test calibrations and extension recommendations for exact rates. Say so.
- Flag environmental risks: nitrate and phosphorus runoff, over-application of sulfur or lime, heavy metals in biosolids or urban soil, erosion.
- **Safety:** Contaminated sites (lead, arsenic, petroleum, radon) need professional testing and remediation. Point people to the relevant environmental and health agencies.
- Soil Taxonomy names are USDA. Mention World Reference Base (WRB) equivalents when relevant for non-US users.
