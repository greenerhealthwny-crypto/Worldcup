# Designing and Building for Containment

Source: Guide Section VI (pp. 37–58). Covers most hardware in NIH Appendix P and the APHIS-PPQ containment facility "suggestions". Match features to need; cost rises steeply with level. Many modern research greenhouses already meet BL1-P or can at low cost. Build new containment greenhouses to last; an experienced design team and high-quality construction give the best long-term value.

## Team and process
- **Team:** researchers and staff (biology knowledge), architects/engineers (design), construction firms (hired independently of designers), commissioning agents (verify systems and compliance), plus IBC members and APHIS/state regulators kept informed and invited in. APHIS Containment Facility Evaluation Specialists can review designs and arrange inspections.
- Learn from others who've built high containment: visit facilities, talk to managers early; the Association of Education and Research Greenhouse Curators runs a forum and meetings.
- **Phases:** programming → design → construction → commissioning. Programming/design steps: pre-planning, planning, pre-design, schematic design, design development, construction documents.
- **Delivery:** design-bid-build, or design-build (faster for complex jobs but costly if changes come after construction starts).
- Owner, designers and builders meet regularly in every phase; all "owners" (facility manager, containment officer, greenhouse manager, researchers) convene often in planning. Ideas and budgets go through several iterations.
- Construction: weekly or bi-weekly meetings with owner reps, architect, GC, subs and commissioning agents; strong owner presence and regular walk-throughs. Post-start design changes are expensive.
- **Commissioning** is the most critical phase: test and document all systems against spec, ideally by an objective third party; may include initial staff training; warranties start after. APHIS prefers its first visit once all systems are online.
- Practitioner tips quoted in the guide: set a commissioning budget early; users stay involved from the start and ask for what they need first ("there are cost/budget implications to everything"); prefer the simple solution.

## Location
- Natural light is the greenhouse's advantage, but APHIS wants sites that minimize risk to environment, agriculture and people. High containment benefits from a separate dedicated facility where escapees can't survive or do harm.
- Balance risk against light, convenience, access and cost; consider climate, street traffic, staff access, room for windbreaks.
- APHIS discourages rooftop greenhouses for some containment because of wind exposure.

## Layout
- Support spaces (headhouse prep, labs, growth chambers, incubators, tissue culture, inoculation, maintenance) sit next to or near the greenhouse. Analyze traffic, process flow and security.
- Everything in a room runs at the highest level any material there needs. Big rooms off a central corridor force people through rooms and make per-room restriction impossible. Better: many small rooms/compartments off common walkways, so different levels and environments coexist.
- Typical plan (Guide Fig. 7) separates containment compartments and prep/growth/inoculation/sterile pass/receiving spaces from non-containment offices and break rooms.

## Security equipment
- Fencing, bollards, CCTV/webcams, motion or sound sensors, in addition to door hardware.
- APHIS suggested a **≥15 ft plant-free buffer** (gravel or pavement) around the facility and a **≥6 ft perimeter fence**; bollards to stop vehicles hitting it.
- Tuned glass-break sensors cut false alarms. Anti-breakage window films reduce UV, so check before applying to glazing.
- Select Agents: at least three locked barriers; periodic testing of security equipment; extensive logs; card readers on doors and on freezers/storage.
- Don't over-engineer: one facility's six-figure infrared system was unusable because local fog set it off.

## Structure
- High containment needs a reinforced rigid frame for security and for heavy double-paned, break-resistant sealed glass. Meet or exceed codes; have local licensed engineers certify for wind and snow loads.
- Primary roof, secondary structure, columns, foundations, cladding. Modern frames: aluminum or galvanized steel (prefab trusses speed building); curtain-wall construction integrates structure and glazing.
- Best design: few hiding places for pests, secure, durable, easy to clean, tolerant of repeated disinfection.
- Common research styles: even-span with standard peak, Venlo, attached even-span, lean-to, gutter-connected ridge-and-furrow.
- Contiguous headhouse and halls are part of the containment area. Masonry knee walls add base security; masonry must be epoxy-coated or sealed at BL3-P and up. Standards: NGMA Book of Standards.
- Plan maintenance access to vents, motors, screens and rack-and-pinion gear (one facility with 12 ft sidewalls couldn't safely reach 20 ft+ equipment).

## Glazing
- Standard glazing satisfies BL1-P, BL2-P and some permitted material. Clear glass lasts longest and passes most light. Tempered, laminated, chemically strengthened and/or double/triple glass preferred for high containment (also helps meet occupancy codes). Panes over about 6 ft long get impractical.
- Lapped glass seals less tightly than gasketed glass but is adequate for many uses. Bedding putty wears out before the glass; when it needs redoing, consider reglazing with new glass or sheet materials at the same time for tighter containment, better control and energy savings.
- Rigid thermoplastics (polycarbonate e.g. Lexan™, acrylic e.g. Exolite™), single to triple wall: polycarbonate cheaper and more fire resistant; acrylic lasts longer and transmits more light. They shift with temperature, so inspect seasonally for gaps and cracks. Impact-resistant grades suit hail areas. APHIS allowed them if security is maintained.
- Film plastics (polyester, polyethylene, PVC): short life (<4 years average), brittle with age, easily penetrated, need checks for loose clamps and tears. BL1-P at best; listed as unacceptable in APHIS guidelines.
- Higher containment: windows closed, sealed and break-resistant (double-paned sealed glass, laminated glass, or sometimes rigid double-wall plastic). Must withstand temperature extremes, flexing, UV and disinfectants. Gaskets dry (TPE, EPDM), wet (silicone) or both; structural silicone glazing bonds glass to frame.
- Retrofitting glazing is possible but may not be practical; reglazing single-pane houses with rigid sheet or double-pane glass needs a whole new glazing system.
- Install glass-breakage sensors (sound, motion or pressure) in high containment; SOPs should cover temporary and permanent repairs.
- One high-containment design chose insulated units with tempered outer and laminated inner panes attached with structural silicone, and judged off-the-shelf commercial envelopes inadequate.

## Floors and drains
- BL1-P: gravel/soil under benches only if material can't travel through and out; concrete walkways suggested at low levels. Solid concrete sloped to drains is preferred for all research greenhouses; retrofitting concrete substantially improves containment and sanitation.
- APHIS: impermeable floors that tolerate disinfectants; filters or screens in drains for small arthropods or pathogens (e.g., filter socks; one company built 100-mesh drain baskets with enough area to clear debris, maintained by trained staff). Sealed/coated concrete (e.g., slip-resistant polymer) is the practical route. Rough finishes are harder to clean but less slippery.
- BL3-P/BL4-P: non-porous disinfectable floors and collection of all runoff to a decontamination tank or treatment before sewer. BL4-P: HEPA-filtered sewer vents certified annually.

## Entry doors and locks
- Entries are where most containment and security breaches happen. Self-closing, locking steel door always recommended.
- Hinged lockable doors for exterior/corridor entrances. Sliding doors acceptable at BL1-P and BL2-P only.
- Extended-height kick plates protect against carts.
- Doors fit tightly with a threshold sweep (neoprene/rubber strip or brush). Sweeps meet lower-level needs (exclude rodents, birds, larger insects). Higher containment: magnetic seals and solid or air-filled rubber/neoprene gaskets; BSL-3Ag may use air-lock doors with inflatable seals and electromagnetic locks. High containment requires a double set of self-closing, locking, gasketed doors.
- Shoe baths and sticky mats at doors and vestibules trap pollen and seed on footwear (very useful with *Arabidopsis*); placing them on the exit path only extends their life. Shoe covers for higher containment.
- Cylinder locks are fine with strict key control; limit total keys and especially masters. Electronic/card systems give tighter control, entry logs and quick code changes. Re-key dedicated rooms.
- Emergency exits per building code: interior panic bars, no exterior hardware. Consult local officials before changing exits.

## Vestibules
- Recommended, if not required, by APHIS for pathogen and arthropod work. A walkway, headhouse or prep room can serve in some cases.
- Dark vestibule between two doors; black-light traps inside; APHIS recommended **≥6 ft between doors**; interlock so only one opens at a time. Shower rooms can serve as vestibules in high containment. Air curtains over exit doors blow organisms back in. A small retrofitted vestibule may be enough for some work.

## Screening
- Size mesh to the organism's size and shape. Commercial holes aren't perfectly square, so larger-hole screens sometimes exclude as well as smaller ones; thread diameter and material matter. Rigid stainless steel can outperform softer mesh of similar hole size; insects can chew polyester.
- APHIS suggested **80-mesh metallic screen for arthropod containment** and **3.25-mesh for noxious weeds or parasitic plants**.
- Fine screen cuts airflow, cooling, CO₂ retention, humidity and light: e.g., 64-mesh with 0.008 in thread is only 23.8% open. Size screens to the ventilation system (passive, fan, fan-and-pad or mechanical). Dust lowers efficiency, so finer screens need more washing/vacuuming; consider ease of cleaning and replacement.
- Screened side vents recommended at BL1-P, required at BL2-P. Screen even behind aspen or cellulose cooling pads; insects get through pads.
- Restore lost airflow with screen boxes (pleated, box, flush, lead-to styles) that add surface area outside or inside the pad frame. Confirm with static pressure measurements; fan suppliers can calculate pressure loss.

## Ventilation, cooling and heating
- Cooling options: natural ventilation (ridge/sidewall vents; most energy-efficient), shade systems, exhaust fans, evaporative cooling, mechanical air conditioning.
- Louvered exhaust fans often pair with evaporative pads; sequence motorized louvers with fan start/stop. Pad performance depends on sizing, installation and maintenance.
- Containment measures: insect screening recommended at BL1-P and required at BL2-P on all vents and exhaust louvers; brushes or flexible barriers where vent arms pass through screens; maintain pads gap-free, louvers tight-closing, screens clean.
- High-pressure fog (droplets ideally ≤20 µm, RO-purified water) cools uniformly and avoids pad openings, so it's better for containment where climate and structure allow.
- When outside vents must be reduced or eliminated, a closed greenhouse overheats fast, so mechanical cooling is the only option; it gives the most precise, uniform setpoints but dries air, so add humidification.
- **BL3-P and up:** filter exhaust air and hold the room under negative pressure; intake is routinely filtered too. For fungal pathogen work under APHIS permit, tandem 99.97% HEPA filters were suggested (the guide's text gives the particle size as ".03 microns", which conflicts with its own 0.3 µm HEPA definition elsewhere; treat it as 0.3 µm and confirm). Roughing pre-filters (ASHRAE 52) protect HEPAs; monitor and change filters on a schedule. Bag-in/bag-out HEPA housings on air handlers.
- AHU in an adjacent mechanical space; **5–10% fresh air** in the recirculated stream replenishes CO₂ and other gases. Add coils and humidifiers as needed.
- BSL-3Ag requires single-pass (100% outside) air, which is energy-expensive. Alternative where regulators and researchers accept it: recirculate conditioned air inside the growth space while supplying and exhausting HEPA-filtered air; gives directional airflow, lower energy cost, possibly lower breach risk.
- One design used a central AHU for base heating/cooling plus per-compartment fan coils and independent humidity control; airflow rates must be designed carefully so this doesn't raise breach risk.
- Heating (hot water, steam, infrared, solar, forced air; in-floor, finned tube, unit heaters, bench heat; overhead tubes or HAF fans) is fine at every level if it doesn't create hard-to-clean spaces.

## Pressurization and infiltration
- High-containment facilities with multiple rooms keep differential pressures so air flows from cleanest (positive or least negative) to most contaminated (most negative). **0.05 in. water gauge** between adjacent spaces is enough. Set by air balancing after installation. Magnehelic® gauges or digital transducers let occupants check; alarm on out-of-range pressure. Reverse the gradient if outside contamination must be excluded.
- Infiltration can't be eliminated. ASABE: no more than **one internal air volume exchange per hour**. Tight houses are well under 1/h; older ones 3/h or more. Find leaks with a smoke test.

Estimated infiltration rates (Table 5, from ANSI/ASAE EP406):

| Type and construction | Air exchanges per hour |
|---|---|
| New, double plastic film | 0.75–1.5 |
| New, glass or fiberglass | 0.50–1.0 |
| Old glass, good maintenance | 1.0–2.0 |
| Old glass, poor maintenance | 2.0–4.0 |

Wind exposure raises rates; shelter lowers them.

- BSL-3Ag greenhouses (ARS): air infiltration test per ASTM E 283-91, static water resistance per ASTM E 331-93, dynamic water resistance per AAMA 501.1-94 (or later standards).
- BL4-P (NIH guidance): leak/decay rate ≤7% per minute (log pressure vs. time) over 20 minutes at 2 in. water gauge (nominally 0.05 in. loss in 1 minute). Many greenhouses can't survive 2 in. w.g.; test only a robust, very well sealed house, cautiously.
- CO₂ decay test: release a measured amount of CO₂, track decay to derive infiltration; do it on a windless night with all fans and air handling off.

## Benching
- Aluminum, galvanized steel and some plastics last longest and clean easily; wood hides pests. Expanded-metal tops drain and circulate air, resist water and chemicals, and meet higher containment. Must withstand repeated disinfection at high containment.
- Ebb-and-flow benches can drain to a holding tank for chemical or heat treatment when runoff collection is occasional; high containment needs all effluent collected and treated.
- Rolling benches add growing space (valuable in costly high-containment houses); collapsible/removable benches add flexibility if storage exists.

## Lighting
- Light attracts arthropods, and some luminaires let them persist. HPS (open bulb) and metal halide (jacketed bulb or lens because of rupture risk) run hot, which limits arthropod survival; fluorescent task lighting runs cool and may let them survive and breed.

## Controls and electrical
- Research greenhouses need high-quality, large-capacity electrical systems; high containment adds redundancy and backup generation, and all receptacles, outlets and conduit sealed against organism escape.
- Use control systems built for greenhouses, not generic building controls. Modern digital systems handle control, logging, sensing, alarms and remote access; weather stations let them anticipate conditions. Analog, pneumatic or mechanical controls are fine at BL1-P/BL2-P; log data separately if wanted. Updating old controls is an economical upgrade.
- All critical systems monitored with an alarm response. Remote alarm response is useful anywhere and critical at high containment (auto-dialers that call until answered; email/phone/text; remote correction via the control system, then physical check soon after). High containment sensors also watch pressure differentials and security.

## Piping
- Minimize intrusions; route pipes so they don't shade plants; seal all intrusions durably. High containment: seal conduit and pipe penetrations on both sides of the barrier so pressures hold and organisms can't pass.
- Automated watering and fertilizing reduce foot traffic (less pollen/seed movement) but don't replace staff checks.

## Screenhouses
- Acceptable for GEOs only if they meet BL1-P or BL2-P greenhouse requirements, including floors, and hold organisms with minimal impact if released. Low-cost option in suitable climates.
- Common upgrades: concrete floors, well-fitting lockable doors, compartments, sealed joints and intrusions, special screening.
- APHIS could permit BL1-P/BL2-P-level work in approved screenhouses, never BL3-P, and almost always required metallic screen.

## Growth chambers and rooms
- Advantages: precise year-round control; place anywhere in a building; more resistant to earthquakes, weather, vandalism; better security; maintenance done outside the containment area; HEPA-filtered exhaust; less foot traffic; smooth, cleanable interiors.
- NIH: usable at BL1-P and BL2-P under certain conditions, and BL3-P with stricter building conditions. APHIS routinely permitted them if the facility met standards and passed inspection.
- "Room within a room": chambers sit inside a containment lab, headhouse or dedicated facility that supplies filtered air and runoff decontamination; walk-in rooms come with vestibules.
- High containment needs HEPA exhaust and directional airflow; standard units aren't fully suitable (inward airflow may fight the stock design; door flaps reduce but don't stop escape; units are rarely tight, so material accumulates inside). Best: specify containment performance at purchase. Retrofit options: HEPA units on exhaust ports; solid trays to catch runoff and debris for disinfection/autoclaving; drains only if piped to an autoclave or kill tank.

## Retrofit vs. rebuild
- Retrofitting to BL1-P, even BL2-P, is probably cheaper than building new. BL3-P may need structural changes and is unlikely to be feasible or cost-effective. BSL-3Ag and BL4-P need a dedicated, highly engineered, isolated facility, so no retrofit.
- Inspect existing houses closely; sound buildings are often nearly adequate with simple, readily available fixes. Structurally suspect houses are often better rebuilt.
- **Rule of thumb: if retrofit cost is within 20% of new construction, renovation generally isn't recommended.** Consult a greenhouse builder, engineer, architect or experienced consultant before major renovation.
