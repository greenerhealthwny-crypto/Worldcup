# Strategies of Containment

Source: Guide Section IV (pp. 17–26).

## What needs containing

- **Organisms:** insects and mites, microbes, nematodes, plant propagules (especially seed and pollen), whole plants.
- **Applications:** biocontrol, research with exotics, plant-made pharmaceuticals and industrial compounds (PMP/PMIC), quarantine, traditional and transgenic research. Combinations (e.g., plant + virus + insect vector) multiply the issues.

Containment gets harder, and requirements stricter, once plant-associated insects or microbes are part of the experiment. For insect quarantine work, contact APHIS early and be ready to describe the containment precisely.

## Routes of escape (plan against every one)

Most common:
- Hitchhikers on people, clothing, shoes and personal items.
- Staff not following protocols.
- Air currents created when passing through doorways.

Also:
- Small animal intruders (birds, rodents, insects).
- Irrigation and waste water.
- Ventilation air currents.
- Material handling equipment, refuse removal, maintenance products and equipment.
- Mobile organisms escaping secondary cages and cross-contaminating other experiments.

## Purposes of GEO containment (NIH Guidelines, as summarized in the guide)

1. Avoid unintended transmission of rDNA plant genomes or release of rDNA-derived plant-associated organisms.
2. Minimize unanticipated harm to organisms and ecosystems outside.
3. Avoid spreading a serious plant pathogen to local crops.
4. Avoid introducing and establishing an organism in a new ecosystem.

## Special application notes

- **PMP/PMIC:** in a greenhouse or growth chamber these count as "in containment" and fall under the NIH Guidelines. Staff should follow GLP or cGMP: facilities easy to disinfect, everything documented, environment precisely and uniformly controlled. (2008 references: APHIS-BRS draft guidance on pharmaceutical/industrial permits; FDA/APHIS draft guidance on bioengineered-plant drugs, biologics and devices.)
- **Exotics:** APHIS-PPQ inspects labs, tissue culture rooms, growth chambers, screenhouses and greenhouses for permitted exotic material, before research and possibly any time during the permit. APHIS prefers to help applicants reach containment; ask for advice and schedule inspections early.
- **Insects and mites:** many carry human or zoonotic diseases. The American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene defines Arthropod Containment Levels ACL1–4. APHIS also has guidance for non-indigenous and transgenic arthropods.
- **Quarantine:** containment greenhouses and screenhouses often serve as observation and treatment sites for restricted nursery/greenhouse material; almost always under APHIS permit, and states may add rules.
- **Select Agents:** possession, use or transfer requires registration with CDC or USDA-APHIS. Researchers manage both biosafety and biosecurity ("prevent loss, theft or misuse of microorganisms, biological materials, and research-related information", BMBL 5th ed.). Greenhouse containment likely not permitted below BL3-P or BSL-3Ag.

## Physical containment

Primary containment comes from the facility plus equipment inside it, maintained by trained staff who follow SOPs and notice abnormal conditions.

**Planning order for a new facility:** site → type of growth facility (air infiltration varies a lot by type) → floor plan, vestibules and ancillary spaces → construction materials and features (glazing, sealing, screening, airflow) → door hardware (critical at every level) → utility routing (maintainable without compromising light or containment) → air supply/exhaust/filtration/pressure if airborne material matters → corrosion resistance to disinfectants → specialty hardware (insect traps, foot baths) and security.

BL1-P and BL2-P need little beyond normal research greenhouse features. BL3-P, BL4-P and BSL-3Ag need expensive advanced features; retrofitting to these is expensive at best and may be impossible.

### Glazing
Properly installed and maintained glazing of any typical material can be a suitable barrier for many materials. Most common: single tempered glass panes lapped over the pane below. Loose or badly installed glazing leaves gaps both ways.

### Caulking and sealing
Seals panes, sills and small openings; restricts insects and helps temperature control. Not a substitute for well-fitting components, but extra sealing can upgrade a conventional facility. Seal conduits where cables cross the primary barrier (silicone; firestop products work well for electrical/data conduits).

### Screening
Effectiveness depends on screen material, the target insect's size and shape, hole size and shape, and air pressure across the screen. Maximum hole size to exclude common pests (Table 3, adapted from Rutgers Cooperative Extension):

| Adult insect | Mesh | Microns | Inches |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leafminers | 40 | 640 | 0.025 |
| Silverleaf whiteflies | 52 | 460 | 0.018 |
| Melon aphids | 78 | 340 | 0.013 |
| Flower thrips | 132 | 190 | 0.0075 |

Mesh = threads per linear inch. Commercial fine screens include Anti-Virus™, Econet™, No-Thrips™. See facility-design.md for screen sizing against airflow.

### Pollen filtration
Pollen size, shape, viability and stickiness determine airborne risk. Netting goes down to about 100 µm (traps large pollen such as maize), but most pollen is smaller and needs meltblown or spun fabric filters (pores <1 to 100 µm). HEPA filters are 99.97% effective for particles larger than 0.3 µm. Effectiveness depends on filter choice, installation and maintenance.

### Air pressure
- **Negative pressure** (exhaust exceeds intake) keeps contaminated air from flowing to clean areas or outside; air flows inward when a door opens. Negative-pressure benchtop chambers (e.g., wood and clear plastic box with blower and filter) add cheap local containment for pathogens and insects.
- **Positive pressure** keeps outside pests, pollen or contaminants out, e.g., when an unwanted insect would wreck a plant/insect gas-exchange experiment.

### Vestibules
Double-door vestibules or anterooms, optionally with light traps, airlocks, air wash, interlocking doors and differential pressure. Doors slide or open outward and self-close. Keep vestibules darker than adjacent rooms (often painted black); lights switch off when a door opens; darkness is needed for light traps. Place at greenhouse entries, emergency exits and other areas holding the organism.

### Cages
Properly used insect cages (metal, wood, glass, screen; or commercial e.g. BugDorm®) raise containment if screen sizing rules are met. Sleeved cages let you handle plants and arthropods without breaching. Small seeds such as *Arabidopsis* are easily carried out; use seed-collecting systems (e.g., Aracon™).

### Location
Climate and surroundings are containment. A tropical pathogen studied where winters are severe may not survive outside. A crop pest or noxious weed is riskier next to large susceptible cropping areas; consider what crops may be grown nearby over the facility's life. GEO work generally hasn't needed remote siting.

### Isolation distances (field reference)
Isolation distance = minimum distance between varieties of the same species to prevent cross-fertilization by wind- or gravity-dispersed pollen; affected by environment and pollination mode. The guide reproduces a Canadian Food Inspection Agency table (Table 4, Guide p. 24) of minimum isolation distances, post-harvest land-use restriction periods (1 to 5 years) and monitoring frequencies for 28 crops in confined field trials. The extracted text scrambles its columns, so do not quote crop-specific values from memory; use the PDF page or the current CFIA guidance. Border rows that trap pollen significantly reduce the distance needed for GE maize, and PMP/PMIC rules required border rows.

## Biological containment and layering

**Layering** = combining physical methods, adding a biological method inside a physical facility, or both. Benefit: higher overall effectiveness, which can justify the next lower biosafety level (subject to IBC/regulator review).

Worked example: tomatoes engineered for tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) resistance, with the virus and its thrips vector. Physical: AntiVirus™-screened greenhouse or insect-proof cages in a normal greenhouse. Layered: remove alternate virus hosts inside and outside, and run stringent insect control around the facility.

**Biological containment** = biological means that block sexual and vegetative reproduction and prevent spread and persistence of genetic material. Examples in the guide:
- Chloroplast engineering (transgene in plastid genome; no pollen-mediated flow).
- GURT: V-GURT (sterile seed) and T-GURT (viable seed, trait off until a chemical trigger).
- Virus-induced gene silencing (no transgenes in pollen or seed).
- Marker-free transformation (not containment, but lowers risk).
Biological methods may not be 100% effective, which is why they're layered with physical measures. NIH Appendix P lists further practices; researchers can propose others for IBC/regulator review.

### Containing plants (pollen and seed)
- Engineer transgenes into non-propagative parts, block propagule survival, or confer sterility.
- Cover or remove flower and seed heads (paper or glassine bags; bag females against incoming pollen, males against outgoing).
- Harvest before sexual maturity.
- Use male-sterile lines.
- Time flowering so pollen shed misses the receptive period of compatible plants nearby.
- Keep cross-fertile plants out of pollen dispersal range.
- Choose the season: e.g., grow transgenic sunflower only in winter in northern climates when no compatible plants are flowering outside.

### Containing microbes
Invisible and unrecoverable once dispersed, though many won't persist. Biological measures are often the best option.
- Avoid aerosols during inoculation.
- Keep distance between infected plants and other susceptible hosts (especially airborne or contact-spread organisms).
- Work when susceptible plants aren't growing nearby.
- Eliminate vectors of insect-borne microbes.
- Choose microbes with obligate host association.
- Genetically disable the microbe to limit survival and reproduction.
- Treat runoff water to kill organisms.

### Containing insects, mites and small animals
- Use non-flying, flight-impaired or sterile strains.
- Work when escapees can't survive outside.
- Choose organisms obligately tied to a plant absent from the area.
- Treat or evaporate runoff to kill eggs and larvae.
- Avoid using small insects in greenhouse cages.
- Kill all pollinating insects in cages after pollen transfer.
