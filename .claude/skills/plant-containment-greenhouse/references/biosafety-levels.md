# Biosafety Levels for Plant Work

Source: Guide Section III (pp. 11–15), with level-specific practices pulled from Sections V–VI. Regulatory framing is as of 2008.

A biosafety level is a bundle of administrative controls, work practices, equipment and facility features that together deliver a set degree of containment. In plant work the main purpose is preventing transfer of propagules to receptive environments outside.

## Which scheme

- **NIH Guidelines, Appendix P (BL1-P to BL4-P):** the guide treats these as the most relevant and widely accepted levels for plant greenhouse work. Section III of the NIH Guidelines defines the four physical containment levels; Appendix P (added 1994) assigns plant rDNA experiments to them using its section P-II criteria and lists greenhouse requirements per level.
- **Plants grown in the lab** (growth chambers, tissue culture rooms, open lab benches) fall under NIH **Appendix G** (laboratory physical containment), not Appendix P.
- **BMBL BSL-1 to BSL-4:** laboratory levels focused mainly on worker and research-subject protection.
- **BSL-3Ag:** a USDA-ARS designation for high containment in agricultural settings, created for large-animal work and sometimes applied to plants. Requirements are in the ARS Facility Design Standards (2002) and BMBL Appendix D. Shares many features with BL3-P/BL4-P; the building itself is the primary barrier.
- **USDA-APHIS does not assign biosafety levels.** It publishes a construction standard for containment with suggested ways to meet it, sets containment for regulated articles case by case, inspects before issuing permits and may inspect any time during the permit. The permit holder is responsible for preventing breaches.
- In every scheme, containment increases as the number increases.

## Criteria the IBC weighs

The level depends on sound science and knowledge of the recipient organism and how it spreads:

- **Introduced DNA:** source and nature (exotic infectious agent or pathogen?); a fragment or a complete genome?
- **Recipient organism:** mode and ease of dissemination; invasiveness; is it a noxious weed or able to interbreed with one; potential to outcross with related species; potential harm to natural or managed ecosystems.
- **Expressed protein:** vertebrate toxin, known or potential allergen, toxic to other organisms locally.
- **Local environment:** nature and importance of nearby crops; presence of sexually compatible wild or weedy species.
- **Experimental procedures:** transport to and from the greenhouse; containment measures needed.

The IBC typically first sets the level for the **unmodified** organism, then asks whether the modification raises, lowers or leaves it unchanged.

## The levels

### Exempt (from the NIH Guidelines; no IBC approval needed under NIH)
USDA, EPA or local regulators may still make their own calls. Per the 2008 Guidelines, exempt work included:
- Synthetic DNA not part of any organism or virus.
- DNA segments from a single non-chromosomal or viral source only.
- DNA from a host propagated only in that same host (e.g., wheat DNA into the same or another wheat variety), whether from chromosomes, chloroplasts, mitochondria or plasmids, provided no other DNA is used, including promoters and enhancers.
- Transfers between species that naturally exchange DNA by established physiological means (NIH Appendix A list; mostly bacteria and yeast, some plant-pathogenic bacteria genera).

### BL1-P
For transgenic plants with no evidence they could survive and spread, and no environmental risk if released. Includes sterile or non-propagative plants, and modified common microbes that spread slowly and have no known negative effects (e.g., *Rhizobium*, *Agrobacterium*).
- Examples: transgenic potato with insect-resistance genes from primitive potato cultivars; transgenic *Rhizobium* carrying *Agrobacterium* root-colonization genes; plants transformed using *Agrobacterium* DNA segments.
- Biological containment can drop a BL2-P experiment to "BL1-P +", e.g., using a genetically attenuated viral strain.

### BL2-P
Organisms that could survive outside but would have negligible impact or be readily managed.
- Transgenic plants that may gain a new weedy trait or can interbreed with weeds or relatives nearby. Example: transgenic sunflower with wheat genes for *Sclerotinia* resistance (sunflower hybridizes with wild relatives and volunteers as a weed).
- Transgenic work using the entire genome of an indigenous infectious agent.
- Transgenic plant-associated microbes that are indigenous and potentially harmful but manageable, or exotic with no potential for serious harm.
- Plant-associated transgenic insects or small animals that pose no threat to ecosystems.

### BL3-P
Organisms with recognized potential for significant harm to the environment.
- Non-GE plant work with exotic infectious agents capable of serious harm (the pest or pathogen is what needs containing; the plant may be harmless).
- Transgenic plants with genes from an EIA where its complete functional genome could be reconstituted.
- Plants or organisms with genes coding for vertebrate toxins.
- Transgenic microbial pathogens of plant-associated insects or small animals that could harm the local environment.
- Examples: challenging citrus engineered for Asiatic citrus canker resistance with the pathogen (a release in Florida could devastate citrus); inoculating transgenic peanut with *Aspergillus flavus* (aflatoxin producer).

### BL4-P
Certain exotic, readily transmissible infectious agents that are potentially serious pathogens of major US crops, especially airborne fungi or viruses together with their arthropod vectors. Human pathogens or vaccines made in plants that could cause serious human illness would be BL4-P. Very few such greenhouses exist; they are hard and expensive to build.
- Example: testing maize streak virus coat protein for protection in corn using its leafhopper vector (*Cicadulina* spp.) for challenge inoculation. The virus is absent from the US but capable leafhoppers are present. The greenhouse is the primary barrier against vector escape; the transgenic maize itself is not the risk.

## Practice and facility matrix by level

Compiled from Sections V–VI. "Rec." = recommended, "Req." = required. Where a level is not mentioned, the guide doesn't specify; apply the next lower level's practice at minimum.

| Topic | BL1-P | BL2-P | BL3-P | BL4-P / BSL-3Ag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Access | Limited at manager/PI discretion during experiments | Req. limited to people directly involved | Authorized individually by manager with PI | Through change/shower rooms only; entry/exit log Req. at BL4-P |
| Apparel | Street/lab clothes; lab coats kept in facility rec.; gloves encouraged; wash hands on exit | Same | Disposable gowns, gloves, caps/hair nets and/or shoe covers usually Req.; removed before leaving and autoclaved | Remove street clothes, wear protective clothing, shower out; clothing stored in inner change room and autoclaved before laundering |
| Signs | None special | "Authorized personnel only"; risk sign with contact name/phone, plants in use, special rules if organisms threaten local ecosystem/agriculture | Same | Same |
| Transfer | — | Closed, non-breakable containers | Plus sealed secondary container; decontaminate its exterior if host/vector is within dissemination distance | Same as BL3-P |
| Records | Experiments in progress | Plus all plants/organisms entering or leaving | Same | Plus dates/times of personnel visits |
| Screens / vents | Screens rec. | Screens Req. on vents; exhaust fan louvers open only when fans run | Closed, sealed, break-resistant windows; filtered exhaust; negative pressure | Same, highly engineered dedicated facility; BSL-3Ag needs single-pass (100% outside) air |
