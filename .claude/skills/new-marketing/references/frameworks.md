# The New Marketing: frameworks and templates

Step-by-step frameworks from *The New Marketing* (Burgess & Burgess, 2020), organized by task. Section numbers match the routing table in `SKILL.md`. Labels for a few multi-step models are reconstructed from the book's text where its figures don't spell them out; the substance follows the book.

## §1 Consumer insight: seeing into the customer's mind (Ch. 2)

**Principle:** People don't always say what they think or do what they say. Combine methods that capture what they *say* (self-report) with methods that capture what they *feel and do* (behavior, nonconscious response).

**Toolbox, from traditional to cutting edge:**
- **Focus groups** (Ernest Dichter, "father of motivation research"). Still valuable. Attend live rather than reading the report; watching faces and body language changes executives' minds. Use them strategically as a "disaster check" before committing big media budgets (e.g., testing animatics).
- **Online focus groups.** Wider geographic reach, flexible for hard-to-reach segments, more candor on sensitive topics, real-time transcripts so moderators can adjust mid-session. Watch for tech problems and group-mix issues.
- **Social listening.** Track reviews and social chatter about your brand, competitors and category. Bring external voices into internal conversations. Act fast on what you hear (Southwest's winter de-icing video).
- **Cultural intelligence.** Look at culture, not just customers: daily signal scanning across news, memes, patents, policy and VC activity to spot trends early (sparks & honey model).
- **Consumer neuroscience / neuromarketing.** EEG, eye tracking, skin conductance, heart rate and facial coding measure attention, emotion and memory activation second by second. This is often more predictive of market response than stated liking. Use it to optimize specific moments of an ad (the Shelter Pet "Jules" case). Treat it as a complement to traditional research, not a replacement.
- **Emotion AI / affective computing.** Facial and voice analytics read emotional engagement with content at scale. Raise ethics and privacy questions as the tools mature.
- **Small data** (Lindstrom). Close observation of individual customers in their real context reveals causation and new hypotheses that big data misses (the Lego shoe story).

**Neuro-informed branding cues:** Colors carry associations (red: energy and excitement; blue: trust and strength). Emotional connection drives memory and choice.

## §2 Buyer personas (Ch. 3)

A buyer persona is a semi-fictional representation of your ideal customer based on market research and real customer data, covering demographics, behavior patterns, motivations, goals and pain points. It puts a human face on data so you market to people, not data points. Also build personas for people who **influence** the purchase (parents, coaches, procurement, etc.).

### Seven-step persona process
1. **Gather buyer data.** Demographics, psychographics, purchase behavior, web analytics, CRM, surveys, social listening, reviews, competitor and industry research. "It is a capital mistake to theorize before one has data."
2. **Assemble your team.** A cross-functional brainstorm: candid, open, phones away, all ideas welcome. Expect disagreement.
3. **Get to know your buyer.** Give them a name, job, age, income, location, family, media habits, hobbies, values, goals and a quote capturing their philosophy. Prefer **several tightly focused personas** over one catch-all. A persona that blends a student and a parent helps no one.
4. **Evaluate pain points.** Identify barriers that stop the purchase and remove them. Brainstorm causes, then investigate before concluding. Look at customer feedback, behavior (where they go when they leave), industry trends and social listening. Example: an online shoe retailer whose cart abandonment turned out to be caused by shipping costs revealed at checkout.
5. **Craft your persona.** Finalize the persona card. Don't be surprised if it differs from what you first assumed.
6. **Validate your persona.** Market to it and watch sales and engagement. If the needle doesn't move, go back. Use qualitative and quantitative surveys and statistical clustering.
7. **Revisit and revise.** Customers change. Revisit at least **quarterly**.

### Persona card template
```
Name / photo:                Personal philosophy (quote):
Job title / role:            Age / gender / status / location:
Income / education:          Buying process role (decision maker, influencer…):
Goals & motivations:         Challenges & pain points:
What they want in a product: Beliefs about our brand:
Media & channels used:       Content preferences (formats, sources):
Hobbies / interests / values:
How we help (solution & message):
```

### Segmentation
Personas sharpen segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP). Focus on buyers predisposed to buy (e.g., premium diaper bags target parents with higher disposable income, not all parents). AI enables **micro-segmentation**: real-time, continuously updated personas and one-to-one relevance at scale ("hitting the right person at the right time").

## §3 Brand, trust and purpose (Ch. 4)

**What a brand is:** a promise and a signal. It's the sum of every interaction someone has with you, and a relationship built on shared utility, purpose and trust. A strong brand becomes **the filter for every business decision** (Southwest's vision guides each employee interaction).

**Trust:** Brands are a form of trust. People increasingly expect companies to help solve problems where institutions have failed, which is both a responsibility and an opportunity. Younger buyers favor brands whose values they share. Trust accumulates through long-term, consistent delivery on the promise (Amazon, Costco, Southwest, USAA).

### Brand purpose
- Purpose is a company's fundamental reason for being: the "why" beyond profit (Sinek: *why* is never money; profit is a result).
- It must be **lived, not claimed**. Retrospective "so-called purpose" is deceptive and backfires.
- Not every brand must "save the world"; for some categories price and convenience dominate. But purpose helps differentiate in crowded markets.
- **Finding purpose (Susan Credle):**
  1. *Interrogate the past.* The founder's story, why the company was created, the equities built, and the values that stayed consistent.
  2. *Understand the present.* How the business positions itself for short-term success.
  3. *Anticipate the future.* Where the business could go (e.g., is a car company really in the business of mobility or data?).
- Align purpose with company values, customer values and culture (Edelman's Cultural Purpose Platform).

### SWOT analysis
- Evaluate **Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats** candidly.
- Get input from across the company: leadership, marketing, customer service, product, HR, IT. Marketing runs the process. Total honesty, especially about weaknesses and what competitors do better.
- Your situation analysis feeds the SWOT; the SWOT feeds marketing strategy.
- Treat "opportunities" as a brainstorm (use IDEO rules, §5). "The best idea is the boss."
- Strengths: what you do best (Amazon: customer obsession; Disney: immersive family experiences). Weaknesses: learn them through listening, especially to detractors (Lego). Opportunities: shifts that invigorate you (Apple and streaming). Threats: seek them out (Kodak, Blockbuster, Toys R Us ignored them).

### Brand value proposition (BVP)
Answer: *What value do we deliver? What customer needs are we satisfying? Why do customers buy from us? Why should they care?*
- Distill the most valuable SWOT insights into one concise, powerful statement. Iterate; peel back layers.
- **Focus on the problem you solve, not what you sell.**
- A strong BVP creates organizational alignment, drives messaging architecture, acts as the north star, and keeps you out of price competition and commoditization.

### Positioning statement
Positioning communicates your value and shapes perception, which is "everything."
1. Build a **positioning document**: What similar products exist and how are you different? How would you describe competitors' brands? Who is the target buyer? Be specific.
2. Condense into: **To [target market], [brand] is the brand of [competitive framework] that provides [meaningful point of difference].**
   *Example:* To business and consumer air travelers, ABC Airlines is the brand of airline that is dedicated to delivering the best customer experience at affordable prices.
3. **Test with target buyers** (focus groups, social listening, surveys): Do they relate to the point of difference? See value in the benefit? Find it persuasive? Unique, or does it remind them of a competitor? Expect several rounds.
4. Use it as the blueprint for product development and all sub-strategies: copy, media, naming, promotion, distribution.

(Case: Audi A4 positioned as the *progressive* luxury brand with "Intelligence is the new rock and roll," consistent with a BVP of challenging industry standards.)

### Cross-cultural lens: META model (Prophet × Alimama/Alibaba)
A universal brand rubric (Maintain, Expand, Transform, Amplify) that focuses on *why* and *what* and lets each brand find its own *how*. It was built for China's shift from price and availability toward brand building; positioning must address specific local needs.

## §4 Customer journey models (Ch. 4)

| Model | Stages / idea | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **AIDA** | Awareness, Interest, Desire, Action | Simple starting frame; outdated for modern CX |
| **McKinsey Customer Decision Journey** | Consideration, Evaluation, Purchase, Experience, Advocacy (loyalty loop) | Shows experience and advocacy create repeat sales and advocates |
| **Consumer Odyssey™ (Blue Focus)** | Winding road: awareness, consideration, purchase, retention, advocacy, with touchpoints at each stage | Mapping relevant touchpoints and micro-moments |
| **Journey as maze / scatter plot** | Many dots and touchpoints around the purchase at the center | Being present at as many relevant touchpoints as possible |
| **Customer Life Cycle (IBM, Sangster)** | Where tech and AI enhance each stage | Planning AI-enhanced CX |
| **Gartner B2B Buying Journey** | Six "jobs": Problem Identification, Solution Exploration, Requirements Building, Supplier Selection, plus Validation and Consensus Creation, which are always on | B2B; buyers loop back, so it's a maze, not a line |
| **Flywheel (HubSpot)** | Attract, engage, delight, with momentum from aligned marketing, sales and service ("smarketing") | Replacing the funnel with self-reinforcing growth |

**Takeaways:** Customers control their own path. Be where they prefer to be, with many useful touchpoints (blogs, infographics, case studies, video, newsletters, apps). CX is becoming *the* differentiator. Many firms now have chief customer experience officers.

## §5 Designing experiences to WOW (Ch. 5)

**WOW** (Tom Peters): impress customers so much they can't help but appreciate you. "Excellent customer experience depends entirely on excellent employee experience."

### Design thinking
- Human-centered: put yourself in the end user's shoes. Functionality is king; good looks alone don't make good design.
- **Focus on the core job to be done** (Peter Skillman's "sitmo": situation, motivation, outcome). Sometimes the best redesign means *less* (Skype rolled back an overloaded redesign after user feedback).
- Use multidisciplinary teams; a single discipline narrows problem-solving.
- Marketers should play a dual role: part product manager, part product designer.

### IDEO brainstorming rules
1. Defer judgment
2. Encourage wild ideas
3. Build on the ideas of others
4. Stay focused on the topic
5. One conversation at a time
6. Be visual
7. Go for quantity

### Spaghetti (marshmallow) tower lessons
Kindergartners beat MBAs and CEOs because they skip status games and prototype immediately.
- Don't waste time on status; nobody needs to be "CEO of Spaghetti."
- Keep your ego out of brainstorming; job titles don't make ideas better.
- Stop talking and build. Fail fast, and you'll fail less next time.
- Unleash your inner child, not your inner critic.
- The first designs are rarely best, and the best designs are rarely first.

### Customer Choreography Map (gap analysis)
1. Choose the experience journey to map (e.g., "buy a new smartphone").
2. Set the journey stages, or moments of truth, across the top (e.g., Research → Evaluate → Buy → Use → Advocate).
3. Map a typical customer's **current state** journey (actions, touchpoints, emotions).
4. Map the **ideal state** journey you'd like every customer to have.
5. Identify the **gaps** between current and ideal.
6. Estimate the **cost to fix** each gap and the potential ROI; prioritize.

```
| Stage          | Research | Evaluate | Buy | Use | Advocate |
|----------------|----------|----------|-----|-----|----------|
| Current state  |          |          |     |     |          |
| Customer mood  |          |          |     |     |          |
| Ideal state    |          |          |     |     |          |
| Gap            |          |          |     |     |          |
| Fix / cost/ROI |          |          |     |     |          |
```

### Simplicity
Simple brands (Netflix, ALDI, Google) can state what they do in a sentence. Companies that fail to provide simple experiences leave money on the table. Solve the real problem in the most straightforward way (PillPack's pre-sorted daily packets) rather than adding bells and whistles (Juicero).

### Future CX trends (IBM view)
New hybrid skills (data plus creativity); design, data, product and marketing teams merging; AI as *augmented* intelligence for real-time personalization; responsible data stewardship as the basis of trust. Remember that the "last best" experience a customer had anywhere sets their expectations.

## §6 Content marketing and storytelling (Ch. 6)

**Content marketing:** sharing helpful information rather than pitching; a dialogue, not a monologue. The Blue Focus definition: the creation and distribution of content across all relevant touchpoints to the right person at the right time and place. Content means any media asset used to provide value to the target audience. Ad trust is low and consumers "smell a sales pitch a mile away."

### Storytelling
- Humans are hardwired for stories. The enduring works (Gilgamesh, Beowulf, scripture) are stories that teach.
- Build a **signature story** (David Aaker): an intriguing, authentic, involving narrative that supports a strategic message about the brand vision, customer relationship, values or strategy, and is managed as a long-term asset. It can't be faked or conjured overnight.
- Make it *your* story. The Coca-Cola test: if the story works without your brand, it's not your story.
- Use symbols as story vehicles (Budweiser Clydesdales).
- Lead with **why** (Sinek): Why does your organization exist? Why do you get out of bed in the morning?
- Stories make people remember, be persuaded and feel connected to the storyteller (Jennifer Aaker). Stories are far more memorable than facts alone.

### Best practices from award-winning content
- Strong visuals and production quality.
- Challenges and tension ("will it happen?") to hook viewers.
- Useful tips and education that improve lives (IKEA Ideas, Fidelity).
- A human, people-first feel, not a faceless corporation.
- Everything aligns with the brand's story.
- It's a long game.

### Seven-Step Content Strategy Blueprint
1. **Scan the marketing landscape:** competitive, economic, legal, political, technological and sociocultural forces.
2. **Audit your content:** Does it map to buying stages? Is it in sync with the BVP? Find gaps.
3. **Link the BVP to the brand story:** What value do we deliver? Why do customers buy? Why should they care? Keep these answers visible.
4. **Target the buyer persona:** The better you know them, the more resonant the content.
5. **Source and staff content:** employees, customers, freelancers, influencers, agencies, crowdsourcing. Roles include content creators, channel managers, curators, editors and SEO specialists, often led by a chief content officer. Don't sacrifice quality for speed.
6. **Publish with an editorial calendar:** Think like a publisher; time content around launches, milestones and current events.
7. **Measure:** traffic, reach, conversions, shares, engagement, leads and revenue, with KPIs chosen by content type.

### Content strategy template (5 Ws + How)
| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Which buyer persona? |
| **What** | Which content types (blog, video, infographic, podcast, case study)? |
| **When** | Best time and frequency? |
| **Where** | Which channels? |
| **Why** | Purpose, brand story, cause? |
| **How** | Content delivery, plus measurement (metrics/ROI)? |

### KPIs by content type (examples)
- **Website/articles:** traffic, unique and new vs returning visitors, time on site/page, bounce/exit rate, page views, traffic sources.
- **Email:** open rate, click-through, conversion, opt-out, churn, delivery rate.
- **Social:** amplification, applause rate, followers, reach, shares, comments.
- **Video:** views, unique viewers, average view duration, subscribers, conversion.
- **Podcasts:** downloads, subscribers, reviews/ratings, backlinks.
- **Paid/PPC:** CTR, ad position, conversions, cost per conversion, cost per sale, ROAS, wasted spend.

### Video
Attention hasn't vanished; it has moved online and there's a war for it. Video gets to the point and immerses the viewer. Entertainment plus product proof beats dry specs (Blendtec's "Will It Blend?", Dollar Shave Club's launch video). Established brands can tell life-stage stories (Subaru).
- **Common video mistakes:** no regular analysis, no strategy, over-reliance on YouTube, weak promotion, ignoring metrics.
- **Fixes:** use design thinking, do a video content gap analysis, use the correct context, run strategic promotion, optimize performance.
- **Live video:** immediacy and authenticity (no filters, no undo). Choose the platform where your audience already is, set goals, and still plan the message.

### AI in content
Personalization, micro-targeting, send-time optimization, chatbots, headline analyzers, tone analysis, AI-generated copy at scale (e.g., Phrasee). Use it to free people from the mundane so they can focus on creative and strategic work. Human discretion stays essential.

## §7 Content distribution and the media mix (Ch. 7)

**Emotion and sharing economy:** Storytelling is the primary currency. Values-driven, inclusive stories tied to a cultural moment connect deeply (Microsoft's Xbox Adaptive Controller Super Bowl ad).

### Start with the basics
- Why are you distributing this content?
- Where does the brand purpose and story play best, and which media will help reach objectives?
- What's the main message, and is it relevant to the target persona?
- Does the content fit the medium, and does it follow the 80/20 rule?

### Trifecta of media possibilities
Find the sweet spot where **traditional, digital and social** overlap for *your* persona's media habits. Follow the leaders in your audience and place diverse early bets (Alpha's podcast and Medium). TV isn't dead; its power is flowing to streaming, smart TV and online video. Ask how each channel **complements and amplifies** the others, not which one wins.

### POEM: Paid, Owned, Earned Media
- **Paid:** ads, sponsorships, paid social, paid influencers. Enhanced reach beyond followers, precise targeting (demographic and psychographic, lookalikes), richer creative, deeper analytics. Needed as organic social reach declines.
- **Owned:** website, blog, podcast, email, and brand pages on social (the platform still controls the rules).
- **Earned:** reviews, press, shares, word of mouth, peer recommendations. The most trusted form.
- **Converged media:** plan all three together on one canvas. Like a quarterback, arrive with a game plan and be ready to call an audible.
- **Rolling thunder:** build a strong base on one owned channel (e.g., a blog), then carry followers to other channels, rather than spreading thin everywhere at once. The goal is to be where the channel gives the best chance to reach and engage your persona, not to be everywhere.
- Short-form video (under two minutes) and static visuals tend to perform best.

### Seven-step digital/social marketing blueprint (enterprise)
Starts with the marketing environment and SWOT, then target personas, content and story, media/platform selection, distribution, execution (Brand Choreography), and measurement and feedback, with learnings applied to the next cycle. Integration is a process, not a project.

### Mobile-first
Customers reach for their phones to decide what to buy and where to go, and social use is increasingly mobile-only. Segment mobile users by needs, design mobile-centric journeys, and integrate digital touchpoints. You have seconds to hook attention.

### Other levers
- **O2O (online-to-offline):** buy online, pick up in store; browse online and buy in store; return online purchases in store.
- **Product placement:** feels native when it fits the story (Stranger Things).
- **Podcasts:** growing, intimate medium.
- **Experiences over possessions:** Millennials and Gen Z share experiences, not products; design shareable, on-brand experiences.
- **First-party data and context:** cell numbers and geolocation let you frame messages to the moment (e.g., the commute).
- **Omnichannel:** a seamless integrated experience however or wherever the customer reaches out. Consumers want digital and physical blended; few brands deliver it.
- **Account-based marketing (B2B):** treat each target account as a market of one, with personalized content, events and campaigns for the people in it. Requires sales and marketing alignment.
- **AI for distribution (Roetzer's three T's):** the right **topic**, the right **type**, the right **time**. Use machine learning for concrete use cases: which audiences, which channels, which next email, which headline. When everyone has the same AI, **the strategist is king**.

## §8 Integrated marketing: Brand Choreography® (Ch. 10)

Brand Choreography (BC) is the orchestration of all appropriate content marketing messages and tactics across traditional, digital and social media, aimed at critical brand touchpoints and linked to each step of the buyer's journey. It's a customer-centric, adaptive update to integrated marketing communications (IMC). Goal: **the right message, to the right person, in the right place, at the right time**, consistently. It follows a **holistic marketing** philosophy: internally, every employee's job is to serve customers; externally, every touchpoint is a chance to win or lose a buyer. One pain point anywhere can lose the sale.

### Seven-step roadmap
1. **Start with the brand:** SWOT → BVP → positioning statement (see §3). "If you don't know where you're going, any road will take you there."
2. **Develop buyer personas** (see §2). Several focused personas beat one catch-all.
3. **Tell your brand story:** communicate your *why* (Golden Circle: why → how → what). Emotional media outlast logical media. Word of mouth from friends and family is the most trusted "advertising." Monitor who's talking about you and which content resonates.
4. **Deploy your POEM mix** (see §7): paid, owned and earned, with a game plan plus agility.
5. **Map the customer experience with a Brand Choreography Mapping taskforce:** a cross-functional team (product, design, IT, customer service, etc.) spending two to three days mapping. Answer how you *want* customers to experience the brand versus how they *actually* do; whether you support every stage (research, purchase, post-purchase); assess the current state; design the ideal state; close the gaps. Prioritize fixes by cost-benefit, validate with employee and customer feedback, and reconvene quarterly or twice a year.
6. **Build the social ecosystem** (decide who's in charge and break down silos):
   - **Social Governance Council (SGC):** cross-department, cross-level group that sets objectives, KPIs and guardrails and keeps the brand unified across channels. Meets regularly. Gives clear roles so no one's "in charge of nothing."
   - **Employee Experience (EX):** employees are trusted messengers (more than CEOs). Engage them with the mission, relevant content and a two-way voice; let them amplify through their networks.
   - **Customer Experience (CX):** helpful content at every stage, chances to give feedback, communities for fans, co-created content that celebrates customers, and above all **stand for something** (Starbucks' experience, Warby Parker's give-back).
7. **Launch Brand Choreography:** perform, create WOW by design, then keep listening, gathering data and refining. Great performances look spontaneous but rest on preparation. Put mobile at the center: consumers control when and where they engage; make things easy, test usability, respect market standards, support iOS and Android, use device features (biometrics), recognize users across devices, and remove steps.

## §9 Social employees, influencers and Gen Z (Ch. 8)

**Why:** Reach requires people. The traditional brand is a T-Rex (big but slow, with limited reach); the social brand is an octopus (many independent, sensing arms coordinated from a central hub). People trust friends, employees and peers more than brands, CEOs or celebrities. Employee advocacy is **earned**, never forced. "Everyone is in the customer contact business."

### Culture first: Branding from the Inside Out
1. **Test:** Do employees know what the brand stands for, and do they care?
2. **Understand the why:** Are employees aligned on mission, vision and values?
3. **Provide value:** Share content that isn't fluff; it drives leads, sales and retention.
4. **Leverage employees' voices:** Social marketing is everyone's job; build support around them and give them a platform.
5. **Create impact:** Don't count platforms; be where your fans are and add value.

Humor and happiness build community, break down silos, and signal status and competence (Aaker & Bagdonas). Happy, connected employees carry the brand spirit outward. **Social executives** lead by example: transparency, accountability, measurable KPIs, modeling behavior, being available.

**Traits of good social employee candidates:** engaged and passionate (believe in the brand), socially savvy (broad understanding of social tools), people-smart (relate to and celebrate fans).

### Five-step Social Employee Pilot Activation Plan
1. **Discover:** Select a cross-functional pilot team (HR, sales, product, accounting, new hires). Do a skills inventory (early adopters, systematizers, teachers) and an engagement inventory (who's already active, who has the strongest personal brand).
2. **Design:** Write a social media policy with reasonable boundaries and freedom:
   - Employee identification (disclose the affiliation, e.g., in bios)
   - Confidentiality
   - Engaging in good faith (integrity, benefit of the doubt, no arguments)
   - Respecting copyrights (credit sources)
   - Accountability (own your mistakes; don't double down)
3. **Develop (train):** Follow the 70:20:10 model; focus on the *how*, since tools evolve; encourage outside training and certifications; keep it social (self-set goals, badges, leaderboards); make training available to everyone; add **reverse mentoring** for executives.
4. **Deploy:** A Brand Choreography team supplies a steady stream of brand-approved, valuable, varied content. Follow the 80/20 rule; make sharing a few clicks (advocacy platforms such as LinkedIn Elevate); build in **ownership**, so employees choose what they believe in and track their own impact.
5. **Evaluate:** Measure what matters (who's talking, what they're saying, how conversations spread), measure employees (who's most active, which platforms work, who drives the most engagement), identify your biggest fans, then refocus, reapply and repeat.

Scale lessons (Domo case): move orientation into an LMS, tie completion to a benefit, run occasional contests (not too often, to avoid fatigue) timed to big events, use a single global advocacy platform with regional admins and topic "divisions," and push major news to employees immediately.

### Influencer marketing
Influencers are word of mouth at scale; followers treat them as trusted sources.
- **Tiers:** nano (<10K followers), micro (~10K–50K), macro (50K–1M), mega (1M+).
- Engagement often **drops** at mega/celebrity level; micro-influencers reply to fans and feel reachable.
- Match influencers on **demographics** (age, gender, location), **topic relevance** (they talk about your brand or category) and **psychographics** (AI personality matching to brand values).
- Measure beyond impressions: store visits, test drives, ticket sales and other full-journey KPIs.
- **Always disclose** sponsorships (FTC, GDPR, CCPA). Hidden deals destroy the authenticity that makes influence work.

### Millennials, Gen Z and Gen Alpha
- Millennials (~1980–1995) turned out to have grounded adult needs but still demand authenticity.
- Gen Z (~1995–2010) distrusts traditional ads, is communal, values shared experiences, and ranks **quality/value first**. Their key value is **authenticity**. They're digital natives who still shop in stores and influence family purchases heavily. Their priority is **trust and control over their data**: explain what you collect and why, let them opt in or out, and add a small incentive.
- For retailers serving Gen Z, the lesson is to deliver the fundamentals: stock, assortment, easy checkout, and a product that lives up to its hype.
- Gen Alpha (children of Millennials) is growing up talking to machines (voice), which will reshape how brands earn attention.

## §10 Personal branding: "the brand called You" (Ch. 9)

A personal brand is **personal career insurance**: a reputation that holds value however the economy moves and lets you charge fair or premium prices (Dorie Clark). Balance **hard skills** (what you can do, which gets you in the door) with **soft skills** (communication, empathy, adaptability, grit, collaboration, which is how you function). "Hard is soft; soft is hard" (Tom Peters). It must be **authentic**, tethered to the real you.

### Seven steps
1. **Do an environmental scan:** What's happening in my field? Major trends? Skills valued in the future, and do I have them? Where do I honestly stand now (not where I want to be)?
2. **Create your BVP:** What are your beliefs and values? Personal traits? What do you offer, and why are you different? Ask trusted friends and colleagues for five words that describe you; don't take criticism personally. Maximize strengths, minimize weaknesses. Write one short sentence, your north star.
3. **Position your brand:** How do you want employers/clients to think of you? What space in their minds can you own? What sets you apart? Formula: **For [target market], [brand] provides [point of difference] because [reason to believe].** Iterate until it clicks.
4. **Figure out your brand story:** Translate hard skills into a compelling story through soft skills. What's your purpose? Why choose you over competitors? (Tools such as personality-insight analyzers and LinkedIn's Social Selling Index can help you see yourself from outside.)
5. **Develop a content strategy:** Who is my audience, and how do I reach them? What topics excite them? What formats (articles, video, podcasts, infographics)? Mix original and curated content. Every piece should reinforce your story. Make it sticky (SUCCESs). Plan: "Those who fail to plan, plan to fail."
6. **Develop a distribution strategy (personal media plan):** Choose channels that fit your story and audience.
   - **LinkedIn:** your profile is the new resume. Professional photo, clear Summary telling your story, short posts (~200–300 words), and stay active (like, comment, share).
   - **Twitter/X:** photo, a creative bio, don't follow far more than follow you, show skills, follow 80/20, use images and video, check analytics.
   - **Facebook:** reach and a separate business page if relevant.
   - **Instagram:** a visual, human side; essential for visual work.
   - **Hashtags:** join and build communities (REI's #OptOutside).
7. **Measure results:** Set goals that are aspirational but realistic and **quantifiable** (not "raise awareness"). Check in regularly with analytics. Celebrate small wins; they signal you're moving the dial.

### Personal branding workbook prompts
- What are my strengths and weaknesses? What do I admire in people with strong personal brands?
- Which content channels most likely reach my target audience?
- How can I make my personal brand "sticky"?

### Tom Peters' "Everything Paper" themes (excellence agenda)
Extreme employee engagement ("people first"); visible moral leadership; *hard (numbers/plans) is soft, soft (relationships/culture) is hard*; extreme humanization of products and services as differentiator #1 (the one least likely to be matched by AI); managing by wandering around; fierce listening; investing for the long term; supporting small and midsize enterprises; "excellence is the next five minutes."

## §11 Data, AI and martech (Ch. 11)

**"Data in, branding out."** Data alone is useless; analyzed data teaches you about customers and powers personalization (Netflix's recommendations and content decisions). Brands evolve from the Age of Identity toward an "Age of You," where brands act as personal partners built around individual needs.

### Principles
- **Tie analytics to specific business questions and goals**, not analytics for its own sake. Many AI initiatives fail to get past the pilot because data was collected without a clear purpose.
- **Big data + small data:** Big data finds correlations in the past and present; small data (observing real people) reveals causation and future hypotheses.
- **Augmented, not artificial, intelligence:** Humans bring common sense, compassion, dilemma-solving, dreaming and imagination. AI excels at locating information, finding patterns, automating simple tasks and working at scale. Combine them.
- **Beware too much automation:** "Customers are humans, not machines." Efficiency for the company that costs the customer experience is dangerous.
- **Bias and ethics:** Algorithms inherit their creators' biases. Build diverse teams, curate diverse training data, and apply human checks.
- **Voice and assistants:** Voice will change the buyer journey; if someone just says "order cookies," unbranded defaults win. Build brand salience now so people ask for you by name. Chatbots work best for structured, repetitive requests (UBank's Mia); broad open-ended bots often disappoint.
- **Martech roles:** the marketing technologist (a hybrid of IT and marketing), "citizen data scientists," and agile experimentation that produces statistically valid feedback every week.
- **CRM:** Organize contacts and engagement history, segment, and personalize outreach at scale. Modern CRM is automated and available everywhere.

### Choosing metrics
Set specific goals (not "grow blog traffic" but "200 blog leads per month by Q4") and map each objective to KPIs:
- Awareness: reach, impressions, mentions, share of voice
- Engagement: likes, retweets, shares, comments, time on page, CTR
- Conversion: leads, form completions, cart and landing-page conversions, sales
- Advocacy: conversations driven by advocates, reviews, referrals
- Business: revenue, ROI, customer lifetime value, retention

### BQ matrix (Publicis Health: prioritizing data/AI "business questions")
Plot candidate questions by **potential business impact** (if solved) against **ability to activate** (can we act on the answer?). Pursue high-impact, high-activation questions. Avoid "pipe dreams" (high impact, can't act) and low-impact busywork. Use propensity models to personalize messages by predicted responsiveness.

### Privacy and trust checklist
- Disclose what you collect, how long you keep it, and who you share it with (GDPR, CCPA and successors).
- Give customers **control** (opt in/out) and a **clear value exchange** (savings, convenience, rewards).
- Treat data protection as a design principle: security, transparent documentation, process design.
- Be careful with retargeting; some customers find it helpful, others creepy.
- Expect consumers to increasingly own and trade their own data; earn the right to it.

## §12 Strategy, the CMO and the future (Ch. 1 and 12)

### Big M vs small m marketing (Ravi Dhar)
- **Big M:** strategic. Where to play, how to win, how to frame value, how to engage customers along the journey, and how it ties to P&L and demand *generation* (selling to more people, on more occasions, at higher prices).
- **Small m:** tactical. Ads, click-throughs, likes, ROI of individual campaigns (demand *capture*).
- Without Big M, performance marketing is "rearranging the deck chairs on the Titanic." Balance both.

### CMO priorities (Dhar)
1. Be the customer's voice in the boardroom; know the customer better than anyone in the C-suite.
2. Show how the brand creates value for society and all stakeholders, not only sales.
3. Craft a marketing message that the CEO champions for growth: new customers, retention, innovation.
4. Reorganize for agility: put data scientists and creatives together, and break silos.

**Three drivers of business that marketing influences:** innovation (products and services), **brand** (why Apple commands a premium), and **customer experience** across the whole journey, including buying, using and disposing.

### Legacy fundamentals still matter
The 4 Ps (product, price, place, promotion), STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning) and the 3 Cs (company, customers, competitors) remain valuable lenses. Winning future business models use customer data to **serve** customers better, not to sell it. Analytics should end in a strategy decision. We overestimate change in two years and underestimate it in ten (Bill Gates).

### Challenges facing the new marketing organization (checklist)
Content that stands out; long-tail niche targeting; social content plans; voice search; the shift to the emotion economy and purpose; evolving journey models; too much data; B2B vs B2C differences; innovation pace and design thinking; social brands and influencers; building from the inside out (social employees); privacy regulation; integrated marketing amid media proliferation; ad blocking; inconsistent messaging; Big Data overwhelm; silos; AI adoption and hyper-personalization; the marketing technologist role; bias and diversity.

### Future trends
- Video everywhere; content *experience* (how content is merchandised and consumed) complements content marketing (Jay Baer); personalization at scale; **dark social** (private shares in messengers, email and texts) is growing and hard to track.
- AR/VR will reshape shopping, training and learning.
- Customer data as real-time instrumentation, moving marketing from segments to **unique individuals** (Jon Iwata). CMOs must unlearn reliance on blunt segmentation.
- The corporate brand must reflect how the company treats employees, suppliers, the environment and society. Purpose-driven, "green-driven" consumers reward it. Profits and purpose are linked (Larry Fink; the Business Roundtable's move beyond shareholder primacy).
- AI will keep learning in every function; companies that don't adopt it fall behind.
- Five forces reshaping marketing (Kevin Randall's afterword): the transformed CMO role, employee experience, society and purpose, technology and customer intimacy, and AI/empathy. Future marketing ecosystems must be **adaptable, engaged, intelligent and purposeful**.
