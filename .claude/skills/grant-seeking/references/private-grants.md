# Parts 3–4: Foundation and Corporate Grants, and Putting It Together (Chapters 19–29)

Distilled from Bauer, 7th ed. (2011). The wording is our own. Dollar figures are the book's, from about 2009, for orientation only.

---

## A. Private foundations

### §1 Understanding the foundation marketplace (Ch. 19)

**Why foundations matter.** They fund nontraditional projects, pilots, needs assessments and preliminary data that government won't. Grants were about $38 billion in 2009.

**What they're like:**
- Most require only a short **letter proposal** (often 2–5 pages) or a one-page letter of inquiry.
- That brevity tempts people to shotgun 20–30 foundations with "Dear Occupant" letters. Don't. It produces rejections and negative positioning, and it wastes overworked foundations' time.
- **Few have staff:** most private foundations have no paid staff at all. **Board members** usually read proposals and decide, with no professional reviewers or scoring.
- A foundation's administrative costs count toward its IRS-required **5% annual payout** of assets. That's why many keep no office and why many have no website or online application.
- Think like the wealthy founder: the foundation exists to further *their* values.
- Beware "free grant money to pay off your mortgage" scams. **Foundations don't pay personal debts.**

**Grantseekers' Private Foundation Decision Matrix:**

| Type | Geographic focus | Funds | Award size | Contact | Application | Decision makers |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **National general purpose** (e.g., the Rockefeller Foundation) | National / international | Model, innovative projects that others can replicate | Large | Write, phone, e-mail; some visits | Longer; detailed | Board and professional staff; some outside review |
| **Special purpose** (e.g., the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation in health) | National, within a defined field | Breakthroughs, model projects in its field | Large to medium | As above | As above | Staff with field expertise and the board |
| **Community** (about 700 in the US) | A defined city, county or region | Local needs, community building; seed money, needs assessment, even operating gaps | Small to medium | Phone, e-mail, visits possible | Short; local review committees | Staff and community board |
| **Family** (about half of foundations) | Varies, usually local | The family's values; often buildings, equipment, local nonprofits | Small; most give less than $1 million a year | Write; few staff | Short letter | Family board |
| **Nonprofit-organization grant makers** (service clubs, professional societies, associations) | Local or field | Their members' interests; match for government grants, travel, conferences, scholarships | Small | Through members | Short | Committee |

- Private foundations rarely give to individuals except through an affiliated 501(c)(3). **Program-related investments (PRIs)** — low-interest loans and similar — are a niche used mostly for housing and community development.
- **Health-conversion foundations**, created when nonprofit hospitals or HMOs were sold, are big local players in health.
- **Who gets funded (2009):** education about 22% of foundation grant dollars (higher education the largest share within it), then human services, health, arts and culture, and so on.
  - Program support and research make up a large share.
  - Capital campaigns, endowment and computer equipment are small shares. Redefine computer requests as part of program development.
  - Most money goes to organizations in the foundation's own state. Large foundations are concentrated in a few states (CA, NY, NJ).
- **Coordinate inside your institution.** Universities often split government grants (sponsored-programs office) from foundations (development office). Never let several colleagues approach the same foundation. Many foundations accept only one proposal per institution and will return all of them.

### §2 Researching foundations (Ch. 20)

**Foundation Research Form (Exhibit 20.1):**
- name and contact;
- links to your organization;
- areas of interest;
- eligibility and restrictions (activities, organization types, geography);
- application rules and deadlines;
- materials on hand (990-PF, guidelines, newsletters, annual report);
- board members;
- staff (full-time, part-time);
- financial information: assets, gifts to assets, number and amount of grants, high, low and average grant, and the same figures **in your interest area**;
- sample grants like yours.

**Rule:** don't record a foundation's name until you know it funds **what you do where you are**.

**Funding Executive Research Worksheet (Exhibit 20.2)** on board members and trustees: education, military service, clubs, corporate boards, business history, other philanthropy, articles, awards, and links to your organization. It helps you find links, guess their values, and pick who should visit.

**Tools:**
- **The Foundation Directory, Parts 1 and 2, and Supplement**, and **Foundation Directory Online**, from the Foundation Center [now **Candid**]. Indexes cover subject, type of support, geography, and **donors, officers and trustees**; run the trustee names against your webbing lists.
  - Search order: subject (from your key terms) → type of support → geography.
  - The best match is all three. If you only get a national match, still consider those funders.
- **IRS Form 990-PF:** the foundation's tax return. It lists assets, all grants paid (including small ones), trustees and managers, and application information. Find it at Candid and GuideStar, at the Foundation Center libraries, or in Foundation Center cooperating collections at public libraries.
- Other databases: GrantSelect, *Chronicle of Philanthropy* grant listings, BIG Online. Use institutional subscriptions.
- Foundation websites give guidelines, grant lists, trustees and staff. The book's example is the Jean & Louis Dreyfus Foundation: NYC focus, aging, arts, education, social services; deadlines in January and July.

### §3 Contacting a foundation before submission (Ch. 21)

**Why:** to validate your research, learn priorities, choose the approach, and set the right ask. Not to sell.

**How:**
- Contact is hard because most foundations have no staff.
- Most valuable: a **link** — a board member you or your advocates know (use webbing). The link can talk to the trustee informally, arrange a conference call, or endorse you. Remember the one-third rule from Part 1.
- If there's an office and contact isn't discouraged:
  1. Send a letter or e-mail requesting information (Exhibit 21.1: guidelines, annual report, application). It's not a proposal.
  2. Phone. Calls are often screened, and phone contact may replace a visit. Have a concept paper ready to send.
  3. Visit, which is rare. Bring the right person: a board member, advocate or graduate plus you, two people maximum, matched to the funder's profile. Dress conservatively. Bring the workbook, a 3–5 minute DVD or laptop presentation, and the cost-benefit analysis of alternative approaches.

**Questions to ask a foundation** (choose a few):
1. Which of our approaches interests you, or would interest the board, most?
2. Your average grant in our area was $X. Is our request realistic?
3. Is your deadline the same as last year? Do early proposals get more attention?
4. How are proposals reviewed: board, staff, outside experts? Is there a checklist or scoring system?
5. Are your current priorities the same as in your published information?
6. *If encouraging:* would you look at a draft? Can I see a well-written funded proposal?
7. *If discouraging:* can you suggest other funders?

Record every contact on the **Private Foundation Report Form** (Exhibit 21.2). That stops colleagues repeating the same questions and builds the relationship history.

### §4 Applying: letter of inquiry and letter proposal (Ch. 22)

**Letter of inquiry (LOI).** A one- to two-page miniproposal meant to win an invitation to submit a full proposal.
- Give enough to excite, not every detail. Spend hours, not minutes, on each paragraph.
- Use letterhead with board names (or the project advisory committee) in the margin.
- Elements:
  - **title** (focused on the change, tested on outsiders);
  - opening (the problem in a few powerful facts);
  - the issue, framed as shared: "Together we can…";
  - what you'll do;
  - why you're the right organization;
  - total cost, with other funders committed or approached;
  - contact information.
- Follow any LOI forms or online templates, which some foundations require.

**Letter proposal.** Usually 2–5 pages, sometimes the whole application. Board members read many in meetings that typically last 1–3 hours, held 1–3 times a year. Use large fonts (12–14 point), white space, bullets and short paragraphs. Some regions use **common grant application forms**; use them only for funders that accept them. Components:
1. **Introductory paragraph:** the reason you're writing. Mention a link if one exists and they agree to be named ("Katie Macrino suggested…"). Otherwise lead with why this grantor.
2. **Why this grantor:** show your homework (e.g., "Your foundation has granted over $4 million to indigent health care in three years…"). Express a shared value. Never send what reads like a form letter.
3. **Needs:** the gap, with a few strong statistics, the funder's geography, and the **human side** (one real case). Don't assume they know the problem because they've funded it before; showing your command of it earns respect.
4. **Solution:** a short summary of what will change and how you'll know. Avoid jargon. Refer to an attached project planner if one is allowed.
5. **Uniqueness:** why you're best suited: staff experience, accreditations, track record, volunteers, partners.
6. **Request for funds:** a specific amount matched to the grantor's typical awards. Mention other committed or prospective funders. Summarize the budget in a paragraph. Give **cost per person served** ("about $5.63 per student over five years"). Explain how the program will be sustained.
7. **Closing:** offer more information and name the contact person, with phone, e-mail and website. Don't beg for a meeting.
8. **Signatures:** your highest-ranking officer, plus a board member and partners. The signature is an institutional commitment.
9. **Attachments**, only if allowed:
   - board list with affiliations;
   - IRS determination letter;
   - audit;
   - organization budget;
   - project budget.

   Avoid unsolicited extras.

The book's **sample letter proposal** is Nevada Read by 3, to the A.O. Smith Foundation. It opens with the referral from another foundation-funded expert and the foundation's track record. It documents Nevada's dropout and graduation rates and links them to third-grade reading. It explains the program (no promotion past grade 3 without grade-level reading, a teacher "Tool Box," mentoring) and lists commitments from 11 districts. It asks $X for year 1 with district matches rising to 10% and 20%, invites a visit, and is signed by the director.

### §5 Submission, decision and follow-up (Ch. 23)

- **Quality circle, foundation version:** there's usually no scoring system, so gather 3–4 people who resemble the board (use the 990-PF trustee list and *Who's Who*). Have them skim the proposal in a few minutes, as trustees do. Mark what they like (+) and dislike (−). Thank them and tell them the outcome.
- **Deadlines:** take them as seriously as federal ones. Use tracked delivery with signature confirmation, or the foundation's online system. Print and check online submissions, and offer a hard copy. As a courtesy, you may supply enough copies for each board member.
- **Links:** after submission, ask friends of the foundation to mention it informally, but don't push.
- **If funded:**
  - Send a **handwritten and a typed thank-you letter.** One trustee said grantees who don't say thanks don't get funded again.
  - Learn the payment and reporting procedures; send a report even if none is required.
  - Ask what they liked, and invite a visit.
  - Add them to your press list.
  - Write again two years later about the results.
- **If rejected:**
  - Thank them anyway.
  - Ask for comments, whether they'd look favorably on a resubmission and when, and whether they'd suggest other funders.
  - Note that some foundations never send rejections; no news is bad news.
  - "Successful grantseekers are persistent."

## B. Corporate grants

### §6 Understanding the corporate marketplace (Ch. 24)

**History and law:**
- The 1935 Revenue Act let corporations deduct up to 5% of pretax income for charitable gifts.
- **A.P. Smith Manufacturing Co. v. Barlow (1953):** the NJ Supreme Court upheld an unrestricted gift to Princeton. This "Magna Carta of corporate philanthropy" allowed gifts that don't directly benefit the business.
- The limit was later raised to 10% of pretax income. Actual giving has averaged about **1%**, and fewer than a third of corporations give at all. [Tax rules change; for example, a 1% floor on corporate charitable deductions applies from 2026. Verify current law.]
- **Corporate giving is enlightened self-interest:** a "this-for-that" exchange. Frame every request around their benefit:
  - employees: recruitment, retention, training, health;
  - communities where they operate;
  - products, sales and market position;
  - public image;
  - research.

  Bauer's example: a museum won corporate support by offering tours to help recruit employees relocating to the area.
- **Size:** about $14 billion in 2009. Much of the 2009 increase was in-kind product donations (pharmaceuticals). Corporate foundations gave about 31% of the cash; direct giving programs gave the rest.
- **Recipients of corporate foundation grants:** education about 23%, human services about 20%, then public and society benefit, arts and culture, health, environment, and international.

**Four mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Corporate contributions program** (direct giving from pretax profits) | Decided by executives or employee committees. Rises and falls with profits. Not publicly reported, so hard to research. Often favors sites with employees and controversial or local causes. |
| **Corporate foundation** | A separate entity funded from profits that smooths giving over the business cycle. Must file a public 990-PF, so it's researchable. Tends to avoid controversial grants. |
| **Marketing / in-kind** | Product donations and sponsorships, often booked as marketing expense rather than gifts. May exceed reported cash. |
| **Research contracts and grants** | R&D money for university labs. Distinguish a true grant (results for the public good; the researcher is free to publish) from a fee-for-service contract (the corporation owns results and patents). |

**Ethics of corporate research funding** (Bauer cites Krimsky, *Science in the Private Interest*):
- Watch conflicts of interest: researcher stock ownership, suppression of unfavorable findings, exclusive IP.
- Get IP, publication and data-ownership terms in writing **before** you accept.
- Some universities warn researchers about liability if results hurt a sponsor's profits.

Use the **Corporate Grants Support Worksheet** (Exhibit 24.1) to match your project's self-interest benefits to each corporate mechanism.

### §7 Researching corporate grantors (Ch. 25)

**Corporate Research Form (Exhibit 25.1):**
- names (corporation, foundation, contributions program);
- contact and links;
- eligibility and restrictions;
- materials on hand (990-PF for foundations only, guidelines, annual report);
- contributions committee or board members;
- deadlines and process;
- financial information: sales, parent company, sites, number of employees, profits, public or private, stock price, products;
- for foundations, assets and grant ranges;
- proposals received vs funded;
- sample grants.

**Where to look:**
- **Start local**, where you live and where companies have **employees**: the chamber of commerce's top-employer lists, economic development agencies, business incubators, industrial parks.
  - Bauer's lesson at UAB (University of Alabama at Birmingham): of the top 10 Birmingham employers, only 3 were for-profit, and most of the rest were nonprofits competing with him. Check with your development office before approaching any local company.
- **Beyond local:** match products and services to your project using industry codes (SIC → **NAICS**) and trade or industry associations.
- **Directories and databases:**
  - Foundation Center's *National Directory of Corporate Giving* and Corporate Giving Online;
  - Dun & Bradstreet Million Dollar Database (sales, employees, officers, SIC/NAICS);
  - Standard & Poor's Register;
  - SEC EDGAR, Yahoo! Finance, Hoovers, annual reports;
  - local business journals.
- Tip: buy one share of local public companies to get annual reports and proxy statements with officer and board changes.
- Corporate giving data are mostly self-reported and unverified, except for corporate foundations' 990-PFs.

### §8 Contacting corporate grantors (Ch. 26)

- Corporations have offices and staff, but staff are busy. **Follow stated instructions.** If a contact is listed, you may call or e-mail.
- Informal contact through links is powerful: employees who volunteer with you, salespeople, parents of your students, advisory members. Many companies favor causes where their employees volunteer.
- Sample inquiry letter or e-mail (Exhibit 26.1): requests guidelines and suggests a meeting during a planned trip.
- **Visits:** bring a credible partner such as a corporate advisory member. Be concise, confident and rehearsed, like a good sales call but not a used-car pitch. Dress conservatively and businesslike. Bring the workbook and a 2–3 minute laptop DVD.
- **Discuss:** the need and alternative solutions (ask which they prefer); verify their priorities ("your 990-PF shows 50% of grants in education…"); who reviews and decides.
- Record contacts on the **Corporate Grantor Report Form** (Exhibit 26.2). Check with your institution's administration before contacting any corporation.

### §9 Applying for corporate funds (Ch. 27)

- Corporate readers — board members, salespeople, marketers, R&D staff — are the **most critical audience**. They expect business-like, succinct proposals.
  - Speak their language. One corporate officer told Bauer the "g-word" is "give," and never to use it; propose an **investment** with a return.
  - Expect them to ask what the company gets.
- **Letter proposal components:** the same as for foundations, with a corporate slant:
  1. **Introduction:** the employee link, or the employees who volunteer with you (with their permission).
  2. **Why this company:** shared values, prior giving, its plants or employees in your area, its products related to your work.
  3. **Needs:** your command of the field and the gap. No solution here.
  4. **Solution:** a brief summary. A one-page spreadsheet or project planner suits corporate readers if allowed.
  5. **Uniqueness:** like a product brochure; why you're the best "brand" to invest in.
  6. **Request:** a specific amount, other funders, cost per person served, return to the company, and a sustainability plan. Corporations want their contribution to leverage support from others and lead to self-sufficiency.
  7. **Closing:** offer information and name a contact.
  8. **Signatures:** a senior officer, and a board member or partner.
  9. **Attachments**, only if allowed.
- Example from the book: the BellSouth Foundation limited proposals to 5 pages with no attachments. Of 200 applicants, 20 got follow-up questions, and the winners answered in a strict format.
- The book's **sample corporate letter proposal** is Nevada Read by 3, to Nevada Power. It ties the company's interest in an educated workforce to dropout economics (the lifetime income lost by dropouts), and spells out the start-up phase and the district matches that lead to self-sufficiency.
- Some corporations require **online letters of inquiry** with specific fields (e.g., J.P. Morgan Chase guidelines: mission, program description, budget, geography, audited financials, board list, prior grant report).

### §10 Corporate submission, decision and follow-up (Ch. 28)

- **Quality circle:** 2–3 advisory members from business, or a business-school professor, role-playing executives who'll spend under 5 minutes. Mark + and − sections and rewrite.
- **Submit** by the method required, early.
- **If funded:** thank-you letter; learn reporting rules; ask what they liked; invite a visit; joint publicity if the company wants it.
- **If rejected or not heard from within about 6 months:** thank them; ask for comments and about resubmission rules (some require a 1–2 year wait). Don't be a sore loser.
- Keep contact between cycles: articles, conference meetings, news of your results. Build a long-term, mutually beneficial relationship.

## C. Putting it all together (Ch. 29)

- Integrate grant steps with your real job. Put grant-related professional tasks — publications that serve as citations, surveys, advisory meetings, conference talks, trips to program officers — on the same monthly **action-steps chart** as the grant steps.
- Bauer's sample one-year plan: literature search, solutions and concept paper, search terms, capability brainstorm, advisory committee, preproposal approval, federal research, contact past grantees and the program officer (e.g., a visit to Washington or a conference), a pilot foundation grant for preliminary data, a quality circle, submission, follow-up.
- Make it visible; one fellow used her chart as a screensaver. "If you don't know where you're going, any road will get you there."
- Plan well and do well. Bauer never wishes his trainees "good luck": luck has little to do with it.
