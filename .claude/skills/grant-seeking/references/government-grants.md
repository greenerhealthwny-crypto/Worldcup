# Part 2: Government Grants (Chapters 10–18)

Distilled from Bauer, 7th ed. (2011). The wording is our own. **Federal systems have changed since 2011.** Current equivalents are given in brackets; verify them before use.

---

## §1 How the government grants marketplace works (Ch. 10)

**History:** land-grant colleges, Depression-era public works, 4-H, WWII medical and scientific grants (penicillin), NSF after Sputnik, the Kennedy and Johnson programs, CETA job training, and the Faith-Based Initiative. Republicans tend to favor sending money to states (New Federalism, block grants). Democrats tend to favor federally directed categorical programs.

**Mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | What it is | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Categorical / project grants** | Congress defines a problem area; grantseekers design the approach; peer review picks the best | Most room for creativity; detailed applications; 3–6 month review; federal audits and site visits |
| **Earmarks** | Congress writes specific recipients into bills, bypassing peer review | Can eliminate competitive funds in a program; program officers resent them, so it can be hard to move from earmark to peer review later. [Earmarks were banned in 2011–2021 and returned in FY2022 as Community Project Funding / Congressionally Directed Spending.] |
| **Formula grants** | Money allocated by census or need formulas to states, counties or cities, which pass it through | Apply to the intermediary; usually training or services, rarely research |
| **Block grants** | Related categorical programs consolidated and given to states to set priorities | State priorities and politics matter; federal rules still follow the money |
| **Contracts** | The government specifies exactly what it wants; often low bid | Little flexibility; posted on FedBizOpps [now SAM.gov]; subcontracting with a winning bidder is a good way in |

- **State grants:** often federal pass-through funds. They are easier to reach, with less travel and local politicians, but states may add rules or use political review. Information is less transparent than for federal grants, so ask state officials directly.
- **Fiscal year:** October 1 to September 30. Unspent grant funds go back to the Treasury. Multiyear awards depend on annual appropriations. Continuing resolutions delay new competitions.

## §2 Researching federal programs (Ch. 11)

**Federal Grants Research Form (Exhibit 11.1)**, one per program. Record the program, CFDA number [Assistance Listing number], agency and contacts. Collect:
- the program description;
- **last year's grantees**;
- **last year's reviewers**;
- the **scoring system** used on past applications;
- a **sample funded proposal**;
- staff profiles;
- submission instructions and log-on details;
- rules, interim rules and final rules from the Federal Register or the agency.

"If you can't get these, stop now; you could be wasting your time."

**Tools:**
- **Grants.gov:** search and apply. Searches by key word, opportunity number or CFDA number. Registration took 3–5 days or more; register early.
- **CFDA (Catalog of Federal Domestic Assistance)** [now **Assistance Listings on SAM.gov**]. Entry fields to read:
  - objectives;
  - types of assistance;
  - uses and restrictions;
  - **applicant and beneficiary eligibility** (if you're ineligible, partner with an eligible lead);
  - application and award process;
  - deadlines (if one has passed, ask to be a reviewer and get last year's grantees);
  - matching requirements ("not required but advised" usually means expected; ask past grantees);
  - post-award reporting;
  - financial information (agencies may have more money than listed);
  - accomplishments;
  - regulations;
  - contacts;
  - related programs;
  - examples of funded projects;
  - selection criteria.

  Search Grants.gov **and** the assistance listings with several terms, because they categorize differently (e.g., "geophysics" vs "physics").
- **Federal Register:** daily notices, proposed and final rules, deadlines. Comment on proposed rules. NSF and NIH use their own publications (NIH Guide, NSF bulletins).
- **FedBizOpps** [SAM.gov contract opportunities]; the **Congressional Record**; **federal depository libraries**; the *U.S. Government Manual*; agency newsletters and e-mail lists (NSF, NIH, CDC, Department of Justice, Department of Education, NIST).
- **Fee-based databases:** GrantSelect, SPIN, IRIS, Community of Science [now Pivot], BIG Online. Use your institution's subscriptions.
- Review your action-steps chart and set a deadline for finishing the research.

## §3 Contacting grantors, past grantees and past reviewers (Ch. 12)

**Why:** the 10,000-applicant study showed contact before submission tripled success. Successful applicants used contact to ask questions that aligned their proposals with what the program really wanted. Most grantseekers dread it and put it off.

**When — the federal grants clock:**
1. The agency publishes proposed rules and invites comment (30–60 days).
2. Final rules and deadlines are published.
3. The application package (RFP/RFA) is posted.
4. The deadline passes.
5. Peer review, then staff review; staff may adjust for things like geographic spread.
6. Awards and rejections go out, and the cycle repeats.

Contact is most useful well **before** the application package comes out, ideally months to a year ahead.

**Steps:**
1. **Request past grantees and guidelines.** Sample e-mail (Exhibit 12.1). The list is public; you can get it under FOIA if needed, but that can annoy staff. Some databases (NSF Award Search, NIH RePORTER) list awards directly.
2. **Past Grantee Analysis Worksheet (Exhibit 12.2):**
   - applications received vs funded (your odds);
   - largest, smallest and typical award;
   - grantee types and locations;
   - PI degrees and titles;
   - grantees to contact.
3. **Contact a past grantee** who is not a competitor, ideally one outside your region or one you have a link to. Congratulate them and ask for a copy of the funded proposal **and the reviewers' comments** for format and style. People usually say yes. Questions to ask:
   - Did you contact the agency first, and who was most helpful?
   - Did they review a draft?
   - Was there a hidden agenda?
   - What materials helped?
   - Was there a site visit?
   - How close was the award to the request?
   - What would you do differently?
4. **Learn the review process.** Sample e-mail requesting reviewer information (Exhibit 12.3). Ask for:
   - the reviewers' backgrounds;
   - how reviewers are chosen;
   - the scoring rubric and weights;
   - how many proposals each reviewer reads and how much time they have;
   - how discrepancies are handled;
   - whether staff review follows peer review.

   **Ask to be a reviewer.** It's the best education available.
5. **Contact the program officer.** Use e-mail plus phone, or in person (Exhibit 12.4 is an appointment request). Find a 10-minute slot at a conference or visit Washington. Send a one-page concept paper beforehand. Dress conservatively. Bring the need data, the workbook, and a DVD or slides of no more than 3 minutes. Never leave the proposal; you're there to gather information.

**Questions for a program officer** (pick 2–3):
- Are there other materials or rules I should review?
- Will funding stay at the listed level?
- How do success rates differ across award types (research, demonstration, new-investigator, career)?
- How will last year's grantees affect new awards (continuations vs new)?
- Are there unannounced or unsolicited-proposal funds?
- Would a larger match help? How much match is really expected?
- What are the most common mistakes in proposals?
- Which of our approaches interests you most?
- Would you review a concept paper or an early draft?
- Can you recommend a well-written funded proposal?
- Do you expect more or fewer new awards this year?
- Can we get the reviewer instructions and scoring sheet for our mock review?

**Records:** the Funding Source Staff Profile (Exhibit 12.5: education, experience, publications; record only what's volunteered, never pry) and the Public Funding Source Contact Summary Sheet (Exhibit 12.6), every time.

**Decide:** the Tailoring Worksheet (Exhibit 12.7). Pick the program with the closest fit and estimate your odds (25%? 50%? more?). Send the optional **letter of intent** when invited; it's a courtesy that helps staff plan reviews.

## §4 Planning the federal proposal (Ch. 13)

**Follow the agency's format exactly.** Use its headings, its order and its vocabulary. "The golden rule of grantseeking: they who have the gold rule."

**Components:**

1. **Need / gap** (the most important section, often scored first).
   - *Projects:* statistics; quotes from leaders; case examples; national figures scaled down locally (e.g., "x% of teens nationally → about N students at West Side High"); stating the need for one person; community statements. Show it gets worse each day.
   - *Research:* the latest studies, the scope and sequence of work in the field, a literature search that includes conflicting views, and why your question must be answered before the field can advance.
   - Bauer's **Gap Diagram**: "what is" vs "what ought to be."
   - Put credibility (uniquenesses, partners) at the end of the need section if the form has no capability section.

2. **Objectives** (projects): what will change and how it will be measured.
   - **Methods** are means. A visitors' center is a method; the objective is effective use of the museum. If there's only one way to do it, it's probably a method.
   - Seven steps to an objective:
     1. Result area (e.g., reading levels of 6th graders).
     2. Measurement indicators (test scores, readmissions, wait time).
     3. Performance standard (how much change, e.g., 80th → 90th percentile).
     4. Time frame (in months from the start, not calendar months).
     5. Cost frame (from the project planner).
     6. Write it: "To [action verb] [indicator] by [standard] by [deadline] at a cost of no more than [$]."
     7. Evaluate it: does it reflect the result you want?

3. **Research question and hypothesis** (research):
   - The question must be specific and focused. Too broad, or too many questions, sets you up to fail.
   - **Null hypothesis:** no difference between treatments.
   - **Alternative hypothesis:** predicts a difference and its direction.
   - Define dependent and independent variables and the significance level (e.g., p < .05 or .01) **before** collecting data.

4. **Methods / protocol:** what will be done, by whom, how long it will take, and what materials are needed. Methods must meet the objectives or test the hypothesis.
   - Don't pad with extra methods to negotiate away later. Agencies now cost-analyze each method, and dropping methods lowers credibility and certainty.
   - Include the sequence, flow, staffing, client selection, time frame, cost-benefit, risk, and what's unique about your design.

5. **Project planner** (Exhibit 13.1, Bauer's spreadsheet tool). One row per objective, then per method or activity, with columns for:
   - month begin and end;
   - personnel (time, salary, fringe);
   - consultants and contract services;
   - nonpersonnel (supplies, equipment, materials, travel);
   - activity subtotal;
   - milestones / progress indicators and their dates.

   It produces:
   - the budget and the budget narrative;
   - job descriptions;
   - a quarterly cash-flow forecast (Exhibit 13.4);
   - matching and in-kind contributions;
   - a negotiating tool. If the award is cut, show which activities or objectives must go rather than promising everything for less.

   Show equipment's percentage use across activities so it isn't cut as a "single-activity" item.

6. **Budget:** standard forms SF-424 and SF-424A (sections A–F: summary, object-class categories, non-federal resources, forecast cash needs, future-year needs, other information including the indirect cost rate). Write a **budget narrative** justifying every item. Taxpayer money demands precise, honest numbers; padding destroys credibility.
   - **Indirect costs (F&A):** recover overhead such as heat, lights, maintenance, payroll and purchasing at the negotiated rate, applied to total direct costs or salaries. Don't also charge those items as direct costs. Clerical salaries need special justification.

7. **Evaluation:** heavier for federal and state funders than for private ones. Use a professional evaluator, such as a university professor or graduate student in statistics. An independent third party adds credibility. Affective outcomes (values, feelings) are hardest to measure, so get help.

8. **Summary / abstract:** usually the second thing read after the title, and often made public. Write it **last**. Mirror the proposal's order and scoring weights: need, aims, approach, evaluation. Use bullets and bold if allowed. Follow rules on length and key words. NSF requires a one-page summary that addresses **intellectual merit** and **broader impacts** separately, in the third person; proposals without them are returned without review.

9. **Title:** descriptive, not cute. No jargon, unexplained acronyms or obscure references (Bauer's example: "Apollo's Lament"). Test it on people who don't know the project. Look at past grantees' titles. Respect length limits.

10. **Future funding (sustainability):** service and membership fees, United Way, major-gift campaigns, endowment, foundation and corporate grants, direct mail. You may budget for the fund-raising effort itself.

11. **Dissemination:** reports, newsletters, conferences (grants can pay your travel), seminars, satellite links, DVDs, websites. Funders want their name in lights, so budget for it.

12. **Attachments / appendix:**
    - studies, tables, charts;
    - CVs or biosketches of key staff;
    - advisory committee minutes;
    - board list;
    - audit;
    - letters of support;
    - tax-exempt letter;
    - pictures;
    - publications;
    - other funders approached.

    Follow page limits. Add a table of contents to the appendix.

**Writing style:**
- Follow the guidelines exactly, even when they seem redundant. Fill in all blanks (N/A where needed). Double-check the math.
- Write for the reviewer's level of expertise. Short words, short sentences (no more than 2 commas), short paragraphs (5–7 lines) with strong lead sentences. Define acronyms. No humor that could offend.
- Make it **skimmable and "reviewer-friendly"**: headings that match the scoring criteria, numbered lists in the margin, bold and underlining, white space, charts, tables and graphics within the rules. If you write "first," include a "second."
- Help one reviewer champion your proposal to the panel. Organize it so they can find answers fast.

**Electronic submission:**
- Grants.gov has no spell check. Write and edit offline, then upload.
- Allow up to 4 weeks for registration problems.
- Coordinate with your sponsored-programs office early. Only the Authorized Organization Representative (AOR) can submit.

## §5 The grants quality circle / mock review (Ch. 14)

**What and why.** Before submitting, 4–5 colleagues role-play the federal reviewers, using the real rubric, time limits and setting. Bauer and the universities he worked with report large gains in quality and funding. Institutions that adopt it develop a culture of constructive peer review.

**Set-up:**
- Finish the draft early.
- Give participants:
  - the reviewers' backgrounds;
  - the time each reviewer spends per proposal (don't let mock reviewers read more slowly than the real ones);
  - the setting (home or panel);
  - the scoring system and weights;
  - how scores are combined (averaged, or high and low dropped);
  - the program guidelines and the CFDA listing;
  - contact notes.
- Use the invitation letter (Exhibit 14.1) and the **Grants Quality Circle Worksheet** (Exhibit 14.2: reviewer profile, time, scoring areas and points, the score ranges that mean poor to outstanding, positives and suggested improvements).
- Offer lunch. Some institutions give release time or honoraria. Use nondisclosure agreements or outside reviewers if confidentiality is a concern.

**Roles (Exhibit 14.3):**
- **Facilitator:** keeps time and asks for the score on each section, then **positives first**, then improvements. Rotates who speaks first and keeps the tone constructive.
- **Proposal writers:** listen. They can't interrupt or defend, only ask clarifying questions at the end of each section.
- **Participants:** score as the real reviewers would. The abstract and title are discussed first, for the expectations they set.

**Know the scoring.** Examples from the book:
- Department of Education: national significance, project design, personnel, resources, management plan (out of 100).
- NIH: 1 (exceptional) to 9 (poor) for overall impact and for each criterion, with minor, moderate and major weaknesses. [NIH revised its review framework for research grants in 2025; check the current criteria.]
- NSF: intellectual merit and broader impacts.

## §6 Submission (Ch. 15)

**Do:**
- Submit **days to a week early.** Last-minute Herculean submitters look like poor grant managers.
- Read the submission rules early. Get the right signatures and follow page and font rules.
- Register in Grants.gov / SAM and the entity identifier [DUNS → UEI] well in advance. Ask your sponsored-programs office about the E-Business Point of Contact and Authorized Organization Representative roles.
- Check how the electronic upload renders: graphics, layout.
- You may send your congressperson's office the abstract and ask to be told if they learn of an award first, for joint publicity. Make clear you're **not** asking for intervention.

**Don't:**
- Promise to send missing parts later.
- Ask for an extension.
- Use elected officials to pressure an agency.
- Contact the program officer after submission. The one exception is a material new development, such as a breakthrough or a price cut, which you can send for the officer to pass on at their discretion.

## §7 Federal grant requirements and compliance (Ch. 16)

- Fear of federal money is mostly unwarranted. Disallowed costs are rare when your fiscal, purchasing and personnel systems are sound. If your nonprofit lacks those systems, use a university or experienced partner as fiscal agent.
- **Grants Requirement Worksheet (Exhibit 16.1):**
  - award number;
  - official start and end dates (**never charge costs before the start date**);
  - reporting due dates;
  - years of eligibility;
  - matching requirements and who keeps those records;
  - governing circulars;
  - special assurances: drug-free workplace, fair and equal employment, civil rights;
  - IP and copyright rules;
  - review-board approvals: human subjects (IRB), animal subjects (IACUC), research misconduct, recombinant DNA, radioactive materials.

  Small organizations without an IRB can use a university partner's.
- **Matching / cost sharing (Exhibit 16.2):** personnel time (including volunteers if allowed), equipment, facilities, other grants, fund-raising events. Match can't come from other federal funds, and the match may rise each year (e.g., 20%, 40%, 50%).
- **OMB circulars cited in the book:** A-21 (colleges), A-87 (state and local governments), A-122 (nonprofits), A-110 (administrative requirements), A-102 (state and local administrative requirements), A-133 (audits). [Consolidated since December 2014 into the **Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR 200**. The single-audit threshold has since risen from the book's $500,000 to $750,000 (2014) and then $1,000,000 (2024 revision). Verify current figures.]

## §8 Dealing with the decision (Ch. 17)

| Outcome | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Funded as requested** | Send a thank-you right away. Ask for reviewers' comments (even funded proposals can improve). Invite the program officer to visit. Ask what mistakes other grantees make. Learn the reporting rules. |
| **Funded with budget cuts** | Thank them. Negotiate with the project planner: cut methods or activities, or drop an objective or lower the expected change. **Be willing to decline** if cuts would make success impossible and cost you credibility. |
| **Rejected with comments** | Thank the staff and reviewers. Ask for more feedback and whether a pilot or needs-assessment version could be funded. Ask about resubmitting and whether you could be a reviewer. Analyze the comments; get an outside expert if reviewers contradict each other. Show how the resubmission responds to them. Never resend it unchanged. |
| **Rejected without comments** (triaged or streamlined review) | No angry replies. Request comments or a meeting, rethink the approach with your advisory group, consider a new direction, and ask to review. |

## §9 Follow-up (Ch. 18)

- Be an **asset, not a pest**:
  - send copies of articles and accepted papers;
  - invite officers to visit or speak at your conference;
  - ask which conferences they'll attend;
  - offer to review;
  - comment on proposed rules;
  - stay on agency mailing lists.
- Support the program legislatively through professional associations, and copy the agency. Write appropriations committees when program funding is at stake.
- Program officers move around but reappear; keep good relationships.
- Plan the next cycle right after decisions are announced.
