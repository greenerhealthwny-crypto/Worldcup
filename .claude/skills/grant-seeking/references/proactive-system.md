# Part 1: Getting Ready (Chapters 1–9)

Distilled from Bauer, *The "How To" Grants Manual*, 7th ed. The wording is our own. Worksheet names match the book's exhibits; Bauer's publisher offered them as downloadable files.

---

## §1 Career grants plan (Ch. 1)

**Your Personal Grants Plan (Exhibit 1.1).** Quantify a five-year vision. Most of Bauer's seminar participants (98%) had no written career plan.
- Your vision of success in your field, and what part of it you'll have reached in 5 years.
- Projects, programs and research you'll be doing, and which will be grant-supported.
- **Release time:** the percentage of your time on grant work each year, and its salary and fringe cost. You can't add a grant on top of a full workload and work at 200%.
- **Personnel by year:** project coordinators, lab assistants, graduate assistants, work-study students, with costs.
- Facilities (square footage) and equipment (cost).
- Planned publications and presentations each year, and target journals and conferences.
- Total resources needed each year, split into what the organization provides and what grants must provide.

**One-year plan (Exhibit 1.2):**
- Steps for the next 12 months, such as small start-up grants, needs-assessment grants, preliminary-data grants and essential equipment.
- People who can help: mentors, advisors, former colleagues as co-investigators or consortium partners.
- Whom you'll contact, and when.

**Mindset:**
- Grant success "gathers momentum." Beware chasing money into areas you don't care about. You can make a living doing many things; the point is to make a living while making a difference.
- *Who Moved My Cheese?*: the cheese (funding) keeps moving, so grantor research must be redone each cycle. Strategies that won last time may not win now.

## §2 Documenting need: the gap (Ch. 2)

**Write the need before the solution.** Start your Swiss cheese workbook with a one-paragraph description of the problem that contains **no solution**. Test it on a colleague with about the grantor's level of expertise. Write for a "scientifically literate lay reader," as NSF puts it. Avoid jargon.

**Document the end, not the means.** "A leaky roof" doesn't justify a building. The need is what a new facility would let you do for clients, research or services. For research, the need is the knowledge gap and the benefit of closing it, not the protocol.

**Needs Assessment Worksheet (Exhibit 2.1):**
1. What is the problem?
2. What data document it (what exists now)?
3. What information is still needed for an accurate, compelling assessment?
4. What is the desired state (what ought to be), backed by quotes from experts, officials and funders on its value?
5. What happens if the problem isn't addressed now (urgency and motivation)?

Search local, state, regional, national and international data, and match the grantor's geographic focus. If you can't document the need locally, consider a **small needs-assessment grant** from a local foundation or corporation. It positions you as the local expert and leads to larger grants.

**Types of needs documentation:**

| Method | Pros | Cons | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statistical analysis (census, government studies, reports) | Abundant, cheap, flexible | "Anything can be proven with statistics"; bad source data gives bad extrapolation | Government and large foundations |
| Survey | Tailored, local, shows concern early | Takes time; small or nonrandom samples can't be generalized (say so) | Projects |
| Studies / literature (controlled, scientific) | Shows command of the field, unbiased | Can seem disjointed; staff bias in what's cited | Research and model projects |
| Key informants (officials, police chiefs, caseworkers, experts) | Easy, cheap, positions you with important people | Biased by who's chosen | Foundations and corporations |
| Community forums / public hearings | Visibility, grassroots image | A vocal minority can take over; costly | Community projects |
| Case studies (one real client, described) | Moving, human side | Must be a real person, not a composite; protect confidentiality; may not be typical | Foundations and corporations; also humanizes federal proposals |

**Cautions:**
- Don't disparage other researchers you cite. They or their students may be your reviewers.
- Citing work reviewers dislike also creates dissonance.
- Test of conviction: "Would I spend my *own* money to close this gap?"

## §3 Time management (Ch. 3)

- **The Swiss cheese book (proposal development workbook).** Adapted from Lakein's mouse that eats a huge block of cheese in small bites. Use a three-ring binder or folders per project, with tabs:
  1. Introduction and documenting need.
  2. Developing solutions and redefining ideas.
  3. Credibility and partners.
  4–7. Government: researching, contacting, creating the proposal, submission and follow-up.
  8–11. Foundations: the same four steps.
  12–15. Corporations: the same four steps.

  Drop articles, notes, data and contact records under the right tab as you find them. A hard copy is handy in grantor meetings.
- **Tickler file** (from David Allen): 12 monthly and 31 daily folders, or calendar reminders. You can't do a proposal, only its next action step. Plan around academic calendars, vacations and conferences, and book meetings with program officers at conferences months ahead.
- **Protect one hour a week** (52 hours a year) for proactive grant steps: phone off, door closed. Use it for a literature update, a one-page concept paper, a call to a potential collaborator, and similar tasks.
- **Action-steps chart:** a PERT or Gantt chart of every step for each grant opportunity, laid out over 12–18 months before the deadline. Show which steps run in parallel and which depend on others. Repeat for each grantor and project.
- **Results Bauer reports:** one fellow went from 0 of 8 proposals funded to 7 of 8 funded (about $900,000 in 18 months).

## §4 Expanding possible solutions (Ch. 4)

**Avoid falling in love with one approach.** Develop several so that during preproposal contact you can find the grantor's "hot spot." Will it judge the best approach by:
- cost-efficiency?
- reliability and validity?
- greatest impact in the field?
- likelihood of being applied in the field?

**Brainstorming rules (Exhibit 4.1):**
1. Groups of 5–8.
2. A neutral facilitator.
3. A recorder.
4. A 10-minute limit.
5. One stated problem.
6. Generate as many solutions as possible.
7. Piggyback on others' ideas.
8. Record everything and combine similar ideas.
9. **No evaluation until the end.**

Fear of idea theft is usually unwarranted. Use noncompete or nondisclosure forms if needed.

**Cost-Benefit Analysis Worksheet, projects (Exhibit 4.2).** For 2–3 approaches, list:
- a summary of the approach;
- total cost;
- number of people served (roll the benefit out over several years or the equipment's life);
- **cost per person served** (funders distrust very high per-person costs, which are hard to sustain);
- positives;
- negatives.

Tip: ask how your favored approach would change with half the money.

**Research version (Exhibit 4.3):** for each protocol, list the total cost, the broader impacts on the field, and challenges or limitations.

## §5 Key search terms and redefinition (Ch. 5)

- Grantor databases match **key words** chosen by grantors or data-entry staff. Their vocabulary may differ from yours. Example: an agency serving blind people found no funders under "people without sight," but found many under "blind" and "visually impaired."
- Mine the literature, press releases and conference programs for current terms. Use quotation marks for exact phrases.
- **Redefine the project** to open up more grantors without leaving your mission.
  - Example: a 6th-grade writing project became an intergenerational e-mail program with seniors. It was ultimately funded by a grantor interested in slowing Alzheimer's progression.
  - Basic science could go to NSF for the science, or to NIH for a health application.
- **Redefinition/Key Search Terms Worksheet (Exhibit 5.1):** values words; subject areas; other relevant fields; constituency groups (children, at-risk youth, elderly, economically disadvantaged…); location (city to international); type of grant (model or demonstration, research, needs assessment, planning, training, discretionary, unsolicited, contract); possible consortium partners.
- **Corporate Redefinition Worksheet (Exhibit 5.2).** How does the project benefit corporations through:
  - employee recruitment, retention and skill development;
  - employee benefits, health and quality of life;
  - public relations and image in the community;
  - product development, positioning or testing;
  - profits?
- Set up **automatic alerts** with your search profile in the grant databases.

## §6 Capabilities, uniquenesses and case statement (Ch. 6)

**Review criteria reward capability.** Federal applications score things like "Quality of Project Personnel" and "Adequacy of Resources" (Department of Education). NSF asks about the proposer's qualifications, prior work and access to resources. Identify weaknesses early and fill them with partners.

**Uniqueness exercise:**
1. Brainstorm with staff, volunteers, clients, board or advisory committee, using the Uniqueness Worksheet (Exhibit 6.1).
2. Ask: what makes us best suited? Consider mission fit, geography, client population, equipment, facilities, data, years of experience, awards, publications, and you yourself as project director.
3. Rank answers from a grantor's view. Keep the top 3 per question.
4. Role-play: "Our organization is particularly suited to implement this solution because…"

Store the list in the workbook. Reuse it in proposals, endorsement letters and contact. For a **model project**, stress the traits you share with similar organizations (so the model replicates) plus the unique capacity to develop and disseminate it.

**Case/mission statement (Exhibit 6.2):**
1. How and why the organization got started (the original societal need).
2. Where it is now (programs, clients, changes).
3. Where it will be in 5 years (it will survive beyond the grant).

Show the project was already on your agenda. A grantee won more than $250,000 from the BellSouth Foundation by showing 5 years of meeting minutes and budget spending on the problem. They told the foundation they'd do it anyway, in 10 years without the grant or 3 years with it.

## §7 Teams, consortia, advisory committees and advocates (Ch. 7)

**Teams.** Funders now favor multi-investigator, multi-site, interdisciplinary consortia. Most researchers were never taught teamwork. Bauer's cautionary tale: a dysfunctional $1 million team produced zero funded proposals.
- **Thinking styles:** conceptual (big ideas), spontaneous (many ideas, few rules), normative (relies on past experience, critical), methodical (step-by-step).
- **Team roles** (Fahden & Namakkal's Team Dimensions):
  - **Creator:** generates ideas.
  - **Advancer:** promotes them and plans.
  - **Facilitator:** keeps the process moving and hands work between roles.
  - **Refiner:** devil's advocate; finds flaws.
  - **Executor:** detail and follow-through.

  Make sure every role is covered and work is handed off between roles.
- Recommended reading: Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*; Avery's *Teamwork Is an Individual Skill*. You don't have to like teammates; you do have to value their contributions. Leave ego at the door.
- **Protect everyone** with written agreements: conflict of interest, nondisclosure, noncompetition, and patent/copyright/IP ownership. University IP offices supply templates.

**Grants advisory committee:**
- Name it for the problem, not the money (e.g., "Committee for Promoting Research on Children's Diseases").
- Invite professionals, community members and corporate people who care about the issue.
- **Grant Resources Inventory (Exhibit 7.1):** what each volunteer can give, such as evaluation, statistics, computing, budgeting, writing, editing, web, travel to funders, equipment, space.

**Advocacy:**
- **Endorsement letters:** give advocates a draft with the facts to include, such as their relationship to you, hours, and accomplishments. They put it on their own letterhead and sign.
- **Advocacy Planning Sheet (Exhibit 7.2):** who will do what (letters, introductions, appointments, accompanying you).
- **Advocacy Webbing Worksheet (Exhibit 7.3):** asks staff, board and volunteers who they know on foundation or corporate boards and in government. Promise that no link is used without consent, and keep the data confidential.
- Bauer's foundation insight: a foundation director told him that about **one-third** of grants went to board members' favorite charities, one-third to board members' friends' favorites, and one-third to unsolicited proposals that fit the foundation's interests. Links matter.
- **Community support (Exhibit 7.4):** use the committee for needs assessment, brainstorming, uniqueness lists, endorsement letters, press coverage and media.

## §8 Choosing the marketplace (Ch. 8)

**Prestige ranking** (it matters for tenure and promotion):
1. Peer-reviewed federal grants.
2. State and local government grants.
3. National foundations.
4. Community foundations.
5. Professional societies, service clubs and associations.
6. Corporate grants. Their perceived objectivity depends on whether the research is free of the sponsor's bias.

**Size.** The book cites about $500 billion a year in federal grants. That dwarfs foundation giving (about $38 billion in 2009) and corporate giving (about $14 billion). Individuals give most private philanthropic dollars. Most seminar participants wrongly guess that foundations dominate.

**Strategy:**
- Research government programs first. Program officers often want **preliminary data**.
- Use a foundation or corporate grant to pilot, test and publish, then go for the larger federal award. Bauer: a foundation pilot turned into a 4-year, multimillion-dollar federal grant.
- Watch the economy. Recessions shrink foundation assets and corporate profits, and the "herd mentality" scares applicants away from programs that are still funding.

## §9 Preproposal summary and approval (Ch. 9)

**"The grantseeker's insurance policy."** Before writing, get your administration to sign off. The Preproposal Summary and Approval Form (Exhibit 9.1) covers:
1. Project director.
2. Statement of the problem.
3. Brief solution.
4. Relation to mission and goals.
5. Suggested grantor and its special requirements.
6. Matching funds.
7. Estimated cost.
8. Personnel (including release time and new hires).
9. Facilities (square feet, location) and equipment (on hand or to buy; maintenance).
10. Signatures: approval, or conditional approval with conditions listed.

**Benefits:** criticism comes before you've invested heavy writing time. Matching funds, release time and space are committed early. Administrators aren't surprised when the final proposal needs their signature.

Many institutions route this by e-mail attachment.
