# Grantseeking (Bauer, *The "How To" Grants Manual*)

A working guide distilled from David G. Bauer's *The "How To" Grants Manual*, 7th edition (2011). The book has 29 chapters in four parts:
1. Getting ready.
2. Government funding.
3. Foundation funding.
4. Corporate funding.

**Bauer's thesis:** grants success comes from organization and a planned, proactive system, not from luck or last-minute writing. Research the grantor before you write. Then write a tailored proposal from the grantor's point of view that closes a gap the grantor already cares about. He reports success rates of 50% or more from his own system, and over 80% among the grants fellows he coaches.

## Core principles

1. **Be proactive, not reactive.**
   - Reactive grantseekers write a generic proposal in a 72-hour rush before a deadline and get rejected 90–100% of the time.
   - Proactive grantseekers spend the same total time, but earlier. Bauer's example is about 10 hours a month for 7 months. That time goes into researching the grantor, making preproposal contact and tailoring the proposal.
   - **Finish 3–4 weeks before the deadline** so there is time for a mock review.
   - If a deadline is looming and you've done none of the homework, Bauer advises skipping that round and preparing properly for the next.
2. **Three principles:**
   1. Every proposal should move *your* organization toward its predetermined goals and mission. Don't chase money that pulls you off mission.
   2. The grantor cares about *its* goals, not yours. Write from the reader's point of view.
   3. Grantors want their money to be the **catalyst** that speeds up a change that was going to happen anyway, and they want it to continue after their funding ends.
3. **Values-based grantseeking.** Bauer builds on Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory.
   - Reviewers filter what they read through their own "values glasses."
   - Anything that clashes with their values or vocabulary creates dissonance and leads to rejection.
   - Research the grantor's values, vocabulary and hidden agenda, and present your project so it reinforces them (consonance).
   - Never try to change a grantor's values or criticize its past funding.
4. **Document the gap.** The most important variable in being funded is a clear, documented need: the gap between what exists now and what could or should be. Document the end (the problem or knowledge gap), not the means (the building, the equipment, the protocol). Show urgency: what happens if nothing is done now?
5. **Offer several solutions.** Brainstorm several approaches and compare their cost and benefit. Bring alternatives to grantors so they can tell you which they prefer. Arriving with one fixed solution looks myopic and leaves nothing to fall back on.
6. **Know why *you*.** List your organization's uniquenesses: staff, track record, location, facilities, partners, mission fit. Use them as credibility builders. Grantors fund capability, not humility or pity ("woe-are-we" begging backfires).
7. **Contact the grantor before you write.** In a study of 10,000 federal applicants, those who contacted program staff before submitting had about **three times** the success rate. Contact is for confirming your research and uncovering the grantor's real priorities and review process, not for lobbying.
8. **Avoid negative positioning.** Every rejection damages your name with that grantor. Keep your success rate at or above **50%** by applying only where your research shows a real match. Never shotgun the same proposal to everyone.
9. **Run a quality circle.** Before submitting, have 4–5 colleagues role-play the actual reviewers, using the real scoring system and time limits. Bauer calls this the most powerful quality-improvement technique in the book.
10. **Match the marketplace.**
    - Government is the largest source. It uses detailed applications and peer review and carries the most prestige.
    - Foundations want short letter proposals and are run largely by boards with few or no staff.
    - Corporations fund out of enlightened self-interest: employees, communities where they operate, products, profits, image.
    - Small foundation or corporate grants can build the preliminary data that wins larger federal grants.
11. **Use a system.**
    - A five-year career grants plan.
    - A "Swiss cheese" proposal-development workbook with one tab per step.
    - A tickler file.
    - An action-steps chart (PERT/Gantt).
    - A protected hour each week for grants work.
    You can't "do" a proposal; you can only do its next action step.
12. **Follow up and say thank you.** Thank every grantor, funded or rejected. Ask for reviewers' comments, offer to serve as a reviewer, and keep in touch between cycles. Persistence builds the relationship.

**Workflow when helping someone pursue a grant:**
1. **Clarify the problem and goals.** What gap will the grant close? How does it fit the organization's mission and the person's five-year plan? Draft a short, jargon-free statement of need.
2. **Build the case:** a literature or data search, the gap, urgency; several solutions with costs and benefits; uniquenesses; partners.
3. **Choose the marketplace and research grantors.** Use key terms and redefinition. Analyze past grantees, reviewers and review criteria. Get internal preproposal approval.
4. **Make preproposal contact** where it is allowed. Validate fit, the preferred approach, budget size and review process. Record every contact.
5. **Write the tailored proposal** in the grantor's format and vocabulary. Use a project planner to drive the budget.
6. **Run a quality circle**, revise, and submit early.
7. **Handle the decision and follow up:** thank-yous, reviewers' comments, resubmission strategy, compliance, ongoing contact.

## Output style

- Write proposals in the grantor's vocabulary, from the grantor's point of view. Avoid opening with "We want / we need."
- Lead with the need and the gap. Use a few well-chosen statistics for government funders, and add the human side (a case example) for foundations and corporations.
- Objectives must be measurable: action verb + result area + measurement indicator + performance standard + time frame + cost.
- Follow guidelines exactly: page limits, fonts, order, required forms. Fill every blank (write N/A where it doesn't apply).
- Offer templates and checklists from the reference files, and adapt them.
- **Currency note:** The book is from 2011. Several federal systems and rules it names have since changed:
  - CFDA → **Assistance Listings on SAM.gov**.
  - FedBizOpps and CCR → **SAM.gov**.
  - DUNS → **Unique Entity ID (UEI)**, since 2022.
  - OMB Circulars A-21, A-87, A-110, A-122, A-133 and A-102 → the **Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR 200**.
  - The Foundation Center + GuideStar → **Candid**.
  - Community of Science → **Pivot**.

  Verify current portals, forms, review criteria, audit thresholds and tax rules before advising. The book's strategy is timeless; its URLs and dollar figures are not.
- **Ethics:** Warn against "free government money" scams; Bauer was an expert witness for the FTC against them. Never fabricate data, letters of support or past results. Warn against misusing elected officials or earmarks. Respect conflict-of-interest, nondisclosure and intellectual-property agreements with partners.
