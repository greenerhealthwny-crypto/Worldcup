# Understanding ADHD and VAST

Distilled from Hallowell & Ratey, *ADHD 2.0* (2021): the introduction, chapters 1–3 and Appendix A. The wording is our own except for short quoted phrases.

---

## §1 What ADHD is (introduction and chapter 1)

**Scale and stakes.**
- ADHD occurs in at least 5% of people (likely more: many high-functioning people go undiagnosed, and few professionals know how to diagnose it). Heritable estimates put it at 5–10%.
- Untreated, it can lead to underachievement, shame, addictions, crime (prisons are full of people with undiagnosed ADHD), accidents, suicide and a shorter life. Russell Barkley: ADHD costs nearly 13 years of life expectancy on average — worse than the top five US killers combined — and roughly two-thirds of people with ADHD have reduced life expectancy.
- Yet Barkley also calls it "the most treatable disorder in psychiatry", with medications that have large effect sizes and are among the safest in psychiatry.

**Common myths the authors reject:** that it's rare, a drug-company invention, laziness, or a lack of discipline. The ADHD mind is constantly at work.

**Ferrari engine, bicycle brakes.** The core analogy for children and adults: a powerful brain with weak brakes. The job is to strengthen the brakes.

**Late discovery.** Many people compensate until life's demands exceed their coping: a first baby, medical school, a promotion. That is often when ADHD shows up and should be diagnosed.

**Who we are.** The underachieving kid lectured about wasted talent, the executive who misses deadlines, many addicts and prisoners ("I coulda been a contender") — but also entrepreneurs (Dan Sullivan of Strategic Coach estimates at least 50% of his clients have ADHD), inventors, comics, SEALs, surgeons, prize winners. Alan Turing: "Sometimes it is the people no one can imagine anything of who do the things no one can imagine."

**The lunatic, the lover and the poet** (Shakespeare), the authors' richer version of distractibility, impulsivity and hyperactivity:
- *Lunatic*: at home in uncertainty and risk; not choosing nonconformity so much as not noticing the standard.
- *Lover*: unbridled optimism, can't hold back, sees possibilities where others see limits.
- *Poet*: creative, dreamy, brooding; an "omnipresent itch" to create; the cycle of vision, disappointment, despair and re-inspiration.
- **Boredom is kryptonite.** A lack of stimulation is a "mental emergency"; the brain reflexively seeks stimulation — anything from picking a fight or binge-shopping to drugs, or to inventing something brilliant.

**Paradoxical tendencies:** lack of focus with superfocus; lack of direction with directed entrepreneurialism; procrastination with a week's work in two hours; impulsive bad decisions with inventive problem-solving; social cluelessness with uncanny intuition and empathy.

**Telltale signs** (less clinical than the DSM):
- Unexplained underachievement relative to talent.
- A wandering mind; inconsistent performance (good and bad days). People blame effort — but without stimulation the person *can't*, not *won't*.
- Trouble organizing and planning (executive function): the child who never gets dressed, the spouse who forgets the trash and is wrongly seen as passive-aggressive.
- A porous short-term memory; tasks forgotten in a heartbeat. Yet hyperfocus and brilliance when stimulated.
- High creativity and imagination (often snuffed out by years of criticism).
- **A different sense of time**: only two times, "now" and "not now"; procrastination; but brilliance under deadline pressure.
- Stubbornness and refusal of help ("I'd rather fail doing it my way than succeed with help").
- Generosity, optimism, enthusiasm, charisma (many excel in sales).
- Restlessness (especially boys and men) or woolgathering (girls and women — the most undiagnosed group).
- A quirky, sophisticated sense of humor; thinking outside the box.
- Social trouble from misreading cues and interrupting; can seem rude or aloof.
- **Rejection-sensitive dysphoria (RSD)** (William Dodson's term): plunging into despair at the slightest perceived slight. Its opposite, which the authors coined: **recognition-sensitive euphoria (RSE)**, an enhanced ability to use praise and encouragement.
- Impulsiveness and impatience ("fire, ready, aim"; flunking the marshmallow test). "Creativity is impulsivity gone right."
- An itch to change the conditions of life, leading to achievement or to addiction.
- High energy alongside lassitude; accurate intuition that misses the obvious.
- Transparency and honesty to a fault (but children may lie impulsively when cornered — wishing reality were different, not sociopathy).
- 5–10x higher risk of addictions and compulsions (drugs, alcohol, gambling, shopping, sex, food, screens). The upside: a creative outlet scratches the same itch.
- A "lightning rod" (the one who gets caught, blamed, disciplined) and a "weather vane" (sensing shifts in mood or opportunity first).
- Externalizing blame and poor self-observation.
- A distorted, overly negative self-image — "attention deficit distorter."

## §2 Causes and VAST (chapter 1)

**Biology.**
- Highly heritable; many genes, not one or two. One parent with ADHD: about a 1-in-3 risk for a child. Both parents: about 2-in-3. (Dr. Hallowell has ADHD, his wife doesn't, and all three children do.)
- Brain insults: head injury, lack of oxygen at birth, early infections, fever, toxins (lead, mercury), trauma.
- Pregnancy factors: maternal obesity, alcohol, drugs, smoking.
- Being studied, unproven: magnetic-field non-ionizing radiation (power lines, appliances, Wi-Fi, phones).

**VAST — the variable attention stimulus trait** (a term suggested by NPR's Carrie Feibel). Modern life's flood of stimuli and constant screens have trained everyone's brains to go faster, leaving many people with ADHD-like traits without the clinical condition. VAST is a *trait*, not a disorder: no impairment is required. Detoxify the label and identify the upside.

**DSM-5 terminology:** "ADD" no longer exists officially. There is ADHD, predominantly inattentive (what used to be ADD); combined type; and the rare predominantly hyperactive-impulsive presentation (see §5).

**VAST paired traits (useful ↔ problematic):**

| Useful | Problematic |
|---|---|
| Passionate, zealous, will sacrifice for a cause or friend | Rigid, fanatical ("Captain Ahab syndrome") |
| Meticulous on projects that matter | Usually disorganized, even chaotic |
| Gets a lot done in a short time | Different sense of time; procrastination |
| Appreciates the offbeat | Bored by reality, ignores rules |
| Visionary dreamer | Trouble organizing ideas into action |
| Honest to a fault, blunt | Can hurt feelings unintentionally |
| Fiercely independent | Trouble on teams, taking orders, with intimacy |
| Creative, ideas pop like popcorn | So many ideas none grows |
| Curious, never satisfied | Easily distracted by novelty |
| Energetic | Impulsive, can't linger over a conversation |
| Remembers details from years ago | Forgets keys, wallet, why they walked into a room |
| Decisive, split-second decisions | Impatient, shoots from the hip |
| Initial surge of excitement | Excitement peters out in the middle phase |
| Takes responsibility, gets it done | Can't delegate |
| Tenacious, never gives up | Stubborn, would rather fail than take help |
| Original, sees what others don't | Seems whacky or arrogant |

| Extremely hardworking | Driven, compulsive; can't shut the mind down; addiction risk |

| Sees the big picture | Weak on implementation and details |
| Generous | Can give away the store |
| Life of the party | Secretly lonely |
| Intensely attentive when interested | Mind wanders when not; glued to devices |

## §3 The Demon of the mind: DMN vs TPN (chapter 2)

**Hank** is a natural salesman tortured by hours of brooding, underachieving at 40; antidepressants and therapy didn't help. His worry has worn a rut.

**Neuroplasticity and epigenetics.** Genes load the gun, environment pulls (or doesn't pull) the trigger; good nurture can blunt bad genes, and bad nurture can suppress good ones. The brain keeps changing throughout life — what you do, who you love, how you move, whether you laugh or have a pet. You can change at any age.

**Two networks (connectomes):**
- **Task-positive network (TPN)**: active when you're engaged in a task. You're absorbed and not self-assessing; momentary frustration passes. This is the **Angel** mindset. Downside: you can get trapped in hyperfocus. The TPN is like a muscle — modern flitting attention lets it atrophy.
- **Default mode network (DMN)**: where the mind goes when it's off task. Imaginative and creative (the back part, the posterior cingulate, holds autobiographical memory; the front part, the medial prefrontal cortex, imagines and plans the future). This is where daydreams, jokes and new connections happen. But at rest it becomes the **Demon**: rumination, catastrophizing, self-criticism. People with ADHD have stored up a lifetime of failures, so their DMN expects the worst.

**The glitchy switch** (MIT's John Gabrieli): in most brains the TPN and DMN are anticorrelated (a seesaw). In ADHD, the DMN stays on during tasks and intrudes, and the DMN's front and back parts are out of sync. "The toggle switches… are off in those with ADHD." This explains creativity and depression within the same hour, the "tortured artist", and why the hunger for stimulation can lead to great achievement or addiction.

**Everyday signs:**
- *Uncle Ron* — deep in the DMN planning his garden, then deep in the TPN planting it — forgot he'd left his wife at the hardware store and told his learner-permit daughter to fetch her.
- *Pirouette syndrome*: circling back to check the door, the stove, the sunglasses on your head.
- *Chicken Little syndrome*: catastrophic thinking (the lawyer obsessing over everything that could go wrong).
- Ruminating over a boss's comment; rehearsing confrontations.

**Outsmarting the Demon.** "Neurons that fire together, wire together" — so fire them in another direction.
- The moment you start to brood, **do something that engages the TPN**: walk, dance, dice celery, play piano, feed the dog, do a crossword, sing standing on one leg. Productivity isn't the point; flipping the switch is.
- **Patterned breathing**, e.g., 6-3-8-3: inhale 6 beats, hold 3, exhale 8, hold 3.
- Once in the TPN, redirect the imagination to constructive work and the DMN becomes the Angel again.
- **Recap:** Don't feed the Demon. Shut off its oxygen by denying it attention. Do something else that engages your mind. Stay in action!
- Meditation, exercise and human connection also reduce the glitchiness.

**Caveat — "attention surplus disorder":** being stuck in the TPN makes people rigid, mechanical and unempathic (task-obsessed managers are less supportive). Oxytocin from a hug, warm social bonding or pets is a quick fix.

## §4 The cerebellum connection (chapter 3)

- The cerebellum is 10% of brain volume but holds about 75% of its neurons. With the inner ear it forms the **vestibulocerebellar system (VCS)**, which coordinates balance and movement and makes learned skills automatic ("like riding a bike"; a quarterback's 2.8-second read).
- **Jeremy Schmahmann (1998, "dysmetria of thought")**: just as the cerebellum smooths movement, it regulates the speed, consistency and appropriateness of thought and emotion — an "oscillation dampener" that keeps a homeostatic baseline. Damage causes cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome.
- The cerebellar vermis is slightly smaller in ADHD (in group MRI data; not a diagnostic test). The cerebellum is among the most plastic brain regions, so **training it may strengthen the brakes**.
- **Balance-based programs** (the authors are cautiously enthusiastic): Frank Belgau's balance board; Brain Balance (Robert Melillo; intensive and costly; best for more severe ADHD or autism); Zing Performance (assessment of eye tracking and attention, then 10 minutes of exercises twice a day for 3–6 months: spinning, tilting, hopping, wobble board with eyes closed while doing arithmetic, juggling; not yet backed by a randomized controlled trial). Dr. Harold Levinson's motion-sickness medications are outside the mainstream and the authors don't prescribe them.
- **Case: Samuel in Shanghai** (treated by email). A 7-year-old with corrected left-handedness, struggling and being hit at school. The plan was trust with his mother, education (she read *Driven to Distraction*), the race-car-brain model, lots of hugs and love, asking the school to stop corporal punishment, a "you can do it" mindset, reading aloud nightly, a loving morning message, and 30 minutes a day of balance exercises. He improved within weeks and kept a prize chocolate because it was "too important" to eat. "Shame is the most disabling learning disability."
- **Home balance routine** (30 minutes daily, any order):
  1. Stand on one leg for a minute.
  2. The same with eyes closed.
  3. Take socks off and on without sitting.
  4. Wobble board up to 5 minutes, then eyes closed.
  5. Sit on an exercise ball, feet off the floor, up to 5 minutes, then eyes closed.
  6. On one leg, pick up five playing cards one at a time.
  7. Low plank up to 3 minutes.
  8. Learn to juggle, then juggle 3–5 minutes.

## §5 Diagnosis: DSM-5 criteria and mimics (Appendix A and chapter 6)

**DSM-5 (abridged).** A persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development.
- **Inattention:** 6+ symptoms (5+ from age 17) for 6+ months, inappropriate for developmental level. Careless mistakes; trouble sustaining attention; not seeming to listen; not finishing tasks; disorganization; avoiding sustained mental effort; losing things; easily distracted; forgetful.
- **Hyperactivity-impulsivity:** 6+ (5+ from 17). Fidgeting; leaving the seat; running or climbing (restlessness in adults); can't play quietly; "on the go, driven by a motor"; talks excessively; blurts answers; trouble waiting; interrupts or intrudes.
- **Also required:** several symptoms before age 12; present in 2+ settings; clear interference with functioning; not better explained by another disorder.
- **Presentations:** combined; predominantly inattentive; predominantly hyperactive-impulsive. These can change over time. In adults, hyperactivity may look like extreme restlessness or wearing others out.

**Conditions that mimic or accompany ADHD (the differential diagnosis):** sleep apnea (looks just like ADHD; get a sleep study if you wake tired, are overweight or irritable), hyper- or hypothyroidism, depression, caffeinism, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, pheochromocytoma, substance use disorders, PTSD, and carrying too many secrets or too much shame.
