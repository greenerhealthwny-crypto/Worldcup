# Strategies for Thriving

Distilled from Hallowell & Ratey, *ADHD 2.0* (2021), chapters 4–7 and 9. The wording is our own except for short quoted phrases.

---

## §1 The healing power of connection (chapter 4)

**Evidence:**
- **The ACE study:** Vincent Felitti's obesity patients dropped out after losing weight; a slip of the tongue ("How much did you weigh when you had your first sexual experience?") uncovered histories of childhood abuse. This led to the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study and questionnaire, now a standard screen. Even a score of 1 raises adult risks (alcoholism, depression, divorce); 4 or more sharply raises chronic lung and liver disease, depression and suicide attempts.
- **ACEs run high in ADHD families**: weak brakes make parents more likely to lash out and children more likely to provoke — a dangerous setup on both sides.
- **Loneliness** (former US Surgeon General Vivek Murthy, *Together*) is the most common pathology; it shortens life like smoking 15 cigarettes a day and impairs performance, creativity and decision-making.
- **The rabbit study:** rabbits fed the same high-cholesterol diet had far less heart disease when a kind technician petted and talked to them.
- **The Harvard Grant Study** (George Vaillant, now Robert Waldinger): the strongest predictor of health, longevity, success and happiness is love — "full stop." What matters is being able to *take love in* (metabolize it). People can recover from a loveless childhood.
- **Dr. Hallowell's own ACE score is 8**, yet he thrived — through connection, especially his grandmother "Gammy."
- **"Dislocation"** (Bruce Alexander, *The Globalization of Addiction*): the loss of psychosocial integration is toxic and leads to addiction, depression, school refusal and more. Kids with ADHD often feel like an invisible minority (Dav Pilkey spent much of elementary school in the hallway).

**Case: Peter, 16.** Smart, failing, believed he was stupid. His parents believed in him; he discovered woodworking (they built a shop at home and planned a vocational tech school). He also did cerebellar stimulation, learned about the DMN, and tried an off-label medication. He reported feeling understood for the first time.

**Mrs. Eldredge's arm.** Dr. Hallowell, dyslexic in first grade, wasn't skipped or laughed at; his teacher sat beside him with her arm around him while he stuttered through reading. "She cured the real learning disabilities, which are fear, and shame, and believing that you can't do something." **Fear and shame are the major learning disabilities.** We live with a "Vitamin Connect" deficiency.

**Tips for a richly connected life:**
- Have family dinners, and bring guests to them.
- Get a pet.
- Make a daily stop at a favorite coffee shop or gas station and get to know the people there.
- Keep up with at least two good friends regularly (a standing lunch or call).
- Arrange sleepovers, or time with grandchildren.
- Give each child at least 30 minutes a week of one-on-one **"special time"** (Peter Metz): no agenda; the child chooses.
- Join a group that meets (book club, lectures, knitting). The MacArthur study on aging found this, with visits with friends, most linked to long life.
- Practice forgiveness of others and yourself as often as you fill your gas tank. Forgiving isn't condoning.
- Take a daily gratitude inventory, and pay compliments.
- Have a spiritual practice, alone or in a group.
- Walk in nature; visit graveyards; climb a tree.
- **Never worry alone.**
- Minimize the news and other things that upset you.
- Associate with dream makers and avoid dream breakers (cynics).
- Look for the person who can give your child (or you) what you can't, especially a **charismatic mentor**. Studies show mentors matter more than grades, study habits or IQ for kids with ADHD.

## §2 Find your right difficult (chapter 5)

- People with ADHD/VAST have an omnipresent itch to create or build. Boredom is kryptonite, and a job beneath their abilities or demanding their weak skills crushes them. The answer is a **Goldilocks challenge — the "right difficult"**: hard enough to engage, aligned with strengths.
- **Strength-based treatment:** "We do not treat disabilities, we help people unwrap their gifts." Most people with ADHD are bad at many things and exceptional at one or two.
- **Examples:**
  - *Allen* turned wrong-number calls for a rug-cleaning company into his own business at 14, then film-machine cleaning, asbestos removal (he took a three-day course rather than say "I don't know"), dog grooming and tour guiding. His superpower is problem-solving, and he now holds a patent.
  - *A future physics luminary* was about to drop out to be a ski bum until a date dragged him to one physics lecture. "Lightning strikes" of passion are common.

**Ten-question strengths inventory.** Do it with another person, who writes down the answers, and keep the document. Bring it to school (to add positives to a file full of negatives and to let teachers use the child's interests) or use it for career planning.
1. What three or four things are you best at doing?
2. What three or four things do you like doing the most?
3. What three or four activities or achievements have brought you the most praise?
4. What are your three or four most cherished goals?
5. What three or four things would you most like to get better at?
6. What do others praise you for that you take for granted?
7. What is easy for you but hard for others?
8. What do you spend a lot of time doing that you're really bad at?
9. What could your teacher or supervisor do so your time is spent more productively?
10. If you weren't afraid of getting in trouble, what would you tell your teacher or supervisor that they don't understand about you?

**Three circles:** spend most working hours where *what you love*, *what you're good at* and *what someone will pay you for* overlap.

**The Kolbe Index** (Kathy Kolbe; measures conative style — *how* you take action — not IQ or personality; validated in 1.6 million cases). Kolbe A for 16+, Kolbe Y for ages 10–16; 36 questions, fee-based. It gives four scores from 1–10:
- **Fact Finder**: how you gather information. ADHD is often low: summarizes and cuts to the chase.
- **Follow Thru**: need for organization and process. ADHD is often low: takes shortcuts and lets solutions evolve.
- **Quick Start**: approach to risk and uncertainty. ADHD is often high: jumps in, "fire, ready, aim."
- **Implementor**: hands-on work and physical space. ADHD is often low (envisions rather than builds), though some need physical, hand-built solutions.
- Dr. Hallowell's score is 5-3-9-2. There are no bad scores.

**Self-defeating patterns:**
- Choosing the *wrong* difficult: the impossibly hard for its own sake ("Jon" would write business plans in his own blood on a forced beach holiday).
- **Refusing help** ("Greg": "then it wouldn't be me succeeding"). The upside is nonconformity, a sharp nose for hypocrisy and not joining cults. The downside is that, in the authors' experience, refusing help is **the single biggest reason people don't progress after diagnosis**.
- The goal is not independence but **effective interdependence**: delegate what you're bad at and do what you're good at. The same applies to relationships.

## §3 Create stellar environments (chapter 6)

- **Environment shapes gene expression.** Major life changes (a first baby, medical school) often unmask ADHD. Charles Bradley, who first gave amphetamine to children, also experimented with lighting and structure.
- **Five controllable areas** (connection, exercise and stress reduction are covered elsewhere): daily structure; nutrition; sleep; populating your world with positivity; and accepting and finding the right help.

**Daily structure:**
- Structure is the walls of the bobsled run — it lets you go fast without careening off.
- Start with a schedule and a to-do list (writing tasks down reinforces them neurologically).
- Use backup reminders (apps, alarms, a partner's nudge), but don't make your partner a perpetual nag.
- Start with one or two commitments and build on the satisfaction of following through.
- **Parents:** be the bumpers on the pinball machine, not hovering fixers. Kids do better knowing who's in charge (a clear chain of command helps adults at work too). Build in **unstructured play**, and hand over responsibility gradually.
- **Screens:**
  - Beware the internet "rabbit hole."
  - Adults should turn devices off for several hours a day (family policy helps) and charge them outside the bedroom.
  - Delay a child's first personal screen as long as you can, set clear limits, and take devices away at night.
- **Rewards work much better than consequences** for the ADHD mind.

**Stellar home:**
- A positive, playful attitude.
- Enough structure to avoid chaos.
- Meals together.
- Worry together — no one worries alone.
- Encourage speaking up.
- Never go to bed angry.
- Pets.
- Lots of laughter, but no ridicule or teasing.
- Honesty, kept kind.
- Gratitude.
- Cheer for one another.

**Stellar classroom (to advocate for):**
- Low fear, high trust; no shaming.
- Clear rules, posted.
- Seating that promotes connection.
- Project-based learning; innovation and initiative encouraged.
- Frequent movement breaks.
- Teachers and administrators who look for strengths.

**Stellar workplace:**
- Low fear and high trust from the top down.
- Structured but not regimented.
- Space that encourages connection.
- Permission to be honest, and a policy against gossip.
- Clear lines of authority and clear policies.
- Work problems out directly with colleagues (HR for harassment).
- Acceptance of both weaknesses and strengths.
- Room for initiative and credit.
- Management that matches talents to tasks and states expectations clearly.

**Nutrition:**
- Whole foods: vegetables, fruits, whole grains, protein.
- Avoid processed foods, additives, colorings, preservatives and trans fats, and limit sugar and juice.
- Drink plenty of water; coffee and tea are fine, and help focus.
- Be your own investigator (e.g., notice a sugar "balloon missile" after parties).
- Some people do better gluten- or lactose-free.
- Benjamin Feingold's elimination diet (no sweeteners, additives, colorings or salicylate foods) fell out of favor but helps some, probably those with food sensitivities.
- Book recommended for alternatives: *Non-Drug Treatments for ADHD* (Richard Brown & Patricia Gerbarg).
- **Omega-3s** support the myelin around neurons (fish such as salmon, mackerel, anchovies and sardines, or a clean supplement). The authors take OmegaBrite; disclosure: it sponsors Dr. Hallowell's podcast.
- **CBD** (via GABA and the endocannabinoid system) may ease the anxiety that often accompanies ADHD; Dr. Hallowell says it reduces his reactivity. This is early.

**Sleep:**
- Get enough to wake without an alarm. Too little raises the risk of obesity, depression, high blood pressure, weakened immunity and anxiety.
- **Sleep apnea can look exactly like ADHD** — get tested if you wake up tired.
- The **Fisher Wallace Stimulator** (FDA-cleared for insomnia, depression and anxiety) has helped many of Dr. Hallowell's ADHD patients; no compensation.
- **Sleep hygiene:** screens off an hour before bed; devices charged outside the bedroom; a dark room; a cool room.

**Populate your world with positivity:**
- Choose schools that work from strengths and share the strengths inventory with them.
- Spend time with people who lift you up; notice how you feel after seeing someone.
- **Accept and find the right help**: never worry alone; asking for help is strength. "Don't just tough it out. Work smart, not just hard."

**Social coaching:**
- Applied behavior analysis (ABA) changes habits (good for quitting smoking or overeating).
- **Social learning** teaches a child to *understand* social situations and choose how to respond, inductively rather than by rote. ABA is surface; social learning is deep.
- Recommended: Caroline Maguire, *Why Will No One Play with Me?*

## §4 Move to focus, move to motivate: exercise (chapter 7)

- Before a test, paper or presentation, get moving (a run, the stairs). Treat exercise as a daily dose of medicine. It is one of the most powerful non-medical tools for ADHD, anxiety and depression.
- **Case: David**, a marathoner professor, unraveled (procrastination, disorganization, forgetfulness) when a knee injury stopped his running. Running had been masking his ADHD. Ritalin helped; once he could run again, he tapered off it.
- **The science:**
  - Exercise releases **BDNF** ("Miracle-Gro for the brain").
  - It raises dopamine and norepinephrine, like stimulant medication: a burst of exercise acts like a dose of stimulant that corrects the deficit for a while.
  - It smooths the transition from the DMN into the TPN, turning on the frontal cortex (the brain's CEO).
  - A 2018 review (more than 700 people, 8 countries): 20–30 minutes of moderate exercise improved reaction speed and precision, and 65% improved planning and organization after a single session.
- **Schools:**
  - Allison Cameron's last-resort classroom in Saskatoon added treadmills, bikes and heart-rate monitors (target 65–75% of maximum) for 20 minutes before class. Suspensions dropped to zero, attendance rose, some students reduced medication, and reading grew about four grade levels in four months.
  - Protect recess and use brain breaks.
  - **"Time-in" instead of time-out**: a stationary bike, mini-trampoline, stairs or an errand. (A Boston school built a "Ratey Room.")
  - Tatsuo Okada's after-school play centers in Japan.
  - Home game: tape numbers on the walls and call them out for kids to tag.
- **Balance and coordination:** martial arts (strict protocols built focus and control in troubled youth); yoga (a Taiwan study improved attention and reaction time); balance training for preschoolers.
- **Meditation** quiets the DMN ("monkey mind"). Mindfulness thickened the hippocampus in 8 weeks (Harvard). Apps such as Headspace help. **"Ha" breathing**: inhale through the nose for 3–4 counts, exhale through the mouth for 6–8 with a "ha" sound.
- **Motivation:**
  - The best long-term motivator is how good exercise makes you *feel* (Michelle Segar), not weight or appearance goals.
  - *Lucy* jumped rope for five minutes before math; it became her "conditioned motivator" through nursing school.
- **Prescription:** at least 20 minutes of activity every day, fun and varied, mixing:
  - Aerobic (about 70% of maximum heart rate).
  - Balance (yoga, BOSU).
  - Focused fitness (dance, racket or team sports, martial arts).
  - Strength training.
  - Nature when possible.
  - Be accountable to someone; exercising together adds bonding.

## §5 Putting it all together: find your feel and make it real (chapter 9)

- The science now shows our issue isn't laziness or disrespect. It is a glitchy DMN–TPN connection and an under-strengthened cerebellum.
- Connection, focusing on strengths, structure, exercise and (when wanted) medication all help.
- **Golf lesson** from Dr. Hallowell's brother-in-law, a golf pro: "Find your feel and make it real. And the key to putting is not caring if the ball goes in the hole." Focus on the moment, not the outcome.
- The techniques are general; how you use them must be personal.
- Overemphasizing winning is shallow. The joy lies in trying, and even flubs teach and make you want to try again.
- "No brain is the same, no brain is the best, each brain finds its own special way."
