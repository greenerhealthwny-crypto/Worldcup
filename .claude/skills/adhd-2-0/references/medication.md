# Medication: "The Most Powerful Tool Everyone Fears"

Distilled from Hallowell & Ratey, *ADHD 2.0* (2021), chapter 8. The wording is our own except for short quoted phrases. This is educational background on what the book says, as of 2021. Decisions about whether to take a medication, and which one and at what dose, belong to the patient and their prescriber.

---

## §1 The authors' stance

- "Ritalin is not a religious principle!" — nor an agent of Big Pharma. Bring reason back into the conversation. (Neither author receives drug-company funding.)
- In the long run, skills, the right school or job, mentors, connection and purpose matter most. In the short term, nothing gives the "bang for your buck" of medication: properly prescribed, it is the fastest, most effective tool.
- **Evidence:** a large meta-analysis by Samuel Cortese (University of Southampton) concluded that medication for ADHD is effective — not 100% of the time, but **70–80%** of the time.
- Critics rarely hear patients' stories of relief within days after years of suffering.
- Many critics self-medicate with caffeine or energy drinks. Over-the-counter "study drugs" (Adrafinil, ginkgo) are unregulated and their effects inconsistent.

## §2 Deciding when (and whether)

- Waiting to "try everything else first" is "like saying 'Let's try a year of squinting before we try eyeglasses.'"
- But **no one should take a medication, or make their child take one, unless they want to.** Medication works better when you want it (the placebo effect, and the general principle that we do better at what we choose). Wait until you or your child is comfortable *and* wants to try it.
- **Do a risk/benefit analysis** (Dan's grandson Steven, 9: outbursts, failing, held back, unpopular; his parents feared stigma). Weigh how ADHD is affecting school, friendships and self-image (is the child coming to see himself as a failure?) against the risks.
- Children with ADHD need boundaries and limits from parents. Outdoor play, exercise, sleep, food and screen habits are essential either way.
- **Three questions:**
  1. Besides consulting my healthcare provider, have I learned as much as I can from reputable sources?
  2. Am I doing everything I can with non-medical treatments (connection, structure, exercise, sleep, eating well, meditation)?
  3. How much is ADHD negatively affecting my life or my loved one's?

## §3 The options

Paul Wender: "Some drugs work in some people, at some dose, some of the time." Be patient with the prescriber; it may take several tries or combinations. Track side effects, duration, peak effect, and positive and negative shifts.

**Stimulants (first choice; most effective, fewest side effects).**
- *Methylphenidate type:* Ritalin, Concerta, Focalin, Metadate, Quillivant and others.
- *Amphetamine type:* Adderall, Dexedrine, Evekeo, Vyvanse, Mydayis.
- **How they work:** they raise dopamine and norepinephrine, effectively stimulating the brakes.
  - Dopamine helps signals pass cleanly ("quiet the chatterbox") and raises motivation. A 2020 Brown University study found methylphenidate increased dopamine in the caudate nucleus and people's willingness to take on hard tasks.
  - Norepinephrine raises arousal and alertness ("reading the room").
  - Both boost executive function in the prefrontal cortex: planning, sequencing, memory and putting the brakes on inappropriate actions.
- Amphetamines act somewhat more on norepinephrine, methylphenidates more on dopamine (small differences). In one analysis, methylphenidates did slightly better for children and amphetamines slightly better for adults.
- **Non-drug ways to raise dopamine:** healthy ones are exercise, creativity and connection to people or a higher goal. Unhealthy ones are carbs, alcohol, drugs and compulsive gambling, shopping, sex or work. Failing to master the healthy pursuit of dopamine leads to addictions; mastering it leads to success and joy.

**Long-acting stimulants** were the big change: about 70% satisfaction versus 40–50% for short-acting, and no midday doses to forget.
- *Vyvanse* is a prodrug activated by an enzyme in red blood cells, so it is harder to misuse. It lasts about 10–13 hours, dissolves in liquid and can be taken with or without food.
- *Mydayis* has a triple-bead release lasting up to 16 hours.
- The authors advise starting with **short-acting** forms until tolerance is established.

**Stimulant-like drugs** (developed as antidepressants):
- Wellbutrin (bupropion), Strattera (atomoxetine), Norpramin (desipramine).
- They work around the clock with no abuse potential, so they are good for people at risk of substance abuse or who can't tolerate stimulants.
- They are usually less effective than stimulants and take weeks to work.
- Possible side effects: insomnia, agitation, dry mouth, nausea, headache, constipation; heart rhythm problems with Norpramin.

**Other stimulant-like options:**
- **Modafinil (Provigil):** works on histamine and dopamine and gives smooth wakefulness for 8–12 hours. It is not FDA-approved for ADHD, so insurance coverage is hard; some people get anxiety or insomnia.
- **Amantadine** (originally for Parkinson's): acts weakly like dopamine. It can give a smooth effect for up to 24 hours with few side effects, is not addictive, and is not a controlled substance. It is used off-label and is being investigated.

**"Outliers": clonidine and guanfacine (Intuniv).**
- Old blood-pressure drugs used alone or with stimulants. They calm agitation, aggression and emotional hypersensitivity and help focus.
- **Rejection-sensitive dysphoria (RSD):** extreme emotional pain at real or imagined rejection, criticism or falling short. It can cause hypervigilance, misreading cues, outbursts or withdrawal. Simply naming it helps (William Dodson). About 1 in 3 people with RSD get relief from clonidine plus guanfacine.
- These carry no abuse risk but can drop blood pressure, and must be **tapered slowly** to avoid a rebound rise in blood pressure and pulse.

The book reprints *ADDitude* magazine's detailed medication chart: formulations, durations from about 3–4 hours to 16 or more, flavors and liquids, patches (Daytrana) and savings programs. A note in it says not every "generic Concerta" is equivalent; ask for the authorized generic.

## §4 Addiction, misuse and side effects

- **Stimulant treatment begun early helps prevent, not promote, later addiction.** Most addictions start between 13 and 23, and untreated teens with ADHD are 5–10x more likely to develop substance problems. The authors favor starting stimulant treatment before age 13 when it's indicated.
- Misuse is real (ADHD drugs are among the most misused by high school and college students), but it is mostly by people *without* ADHD who use them to study or to intensify highs.
- The bigger clinical problem is patients taking *less* than prescribed, not drug-seeking. Mild evening "withdrawal" (tiredness, irritability) can occur as doses wear off.
- **Common side effects:** irritability, dry mouth, sleep disruption, headaches, reduced appetite, and small increases in heart rate and blood pressure. Long-term heart risk appears very low, but **close monitoring by the prescriber** is essential, especially at the start.
- **Bonus:** properly treating ADHD can protect against secondary anxiety and depression.

## §5 Genetic testing

- Pharmacogenetic testing can't yet say which drug will work best ("promising, but we're not there yet"). It can show how fast you metabolize certain drugs, which helps with dosing and can prevent a bad reaction if an enzyme is missing.
- The authors like **Tempus**, which uses whole-exome sequencing (most companies test small panels of 12–15 genes) and has a financial-assistance program (often $100 or less out of pocket). The authors receive no compensation ("Not even lunch!").
- They now think it's time to at least consider routine genetic testing when prescribing.
